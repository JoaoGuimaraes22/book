# Chapter Fifty-Four — The Trade

He had every day. That was the trouble with them.

On the road out of Qeshet he had kept the days because the days were the only thing in the box that was his, and each one had been a different weight. Now he had them the way he had everything, whether he wanted them or not, and for the first time in his life having them was no use, because he could not have told anybody what the fortieth was that the thirty-ninth had not been. A fold of ground above a stream. Canvas up before he had seen who carried it. Three fires dug down, the stones on the side they had come from. Six mules to hobble with one hand and a knee. The count went on making its numbers and the numbers went on being the same, and somewhere in the third week he stopped taking them out to look at them, which was not the same as their being gone.

The camp never stayed. That was the first rule and nobody had said it. It moved the way smoke moves off a fire when the wind cannot decide: two nights under trees that dripped on them, then a gorge with a floor of dry stones that the mules hated, then a ravine, then a ruin on a hill with half a wall standing and a hearth in it older than the empire. They never went back to a place. They never went through a place twice. He tried for a while to build the country out of it, the way he had built Ishareth out of a walk under a sack, and gave it up, because a country made of places you are never going to see again is not a map, it is a list.

He asked Borren once, in the second week, where they were.

"Between," Borren said.

"Between what."

"Ishareth and Solvenna." He did not look up from the strap he was mending. "You will get the name of the next town on the day we go into it, and not before, and you will forget it on the day we go out. That is not a punishment. That is what a name is for."

---

It started with watching, because the watching was what he was for.

They came down on a road three days out and lay along the top of a cut above it for most of a day, five of them and Kael, and did nothing. A convoy went under them in the afternoon: eleven wagons, a man riding at the head of it who was somebody and a man at the tail who was paid, guards walking, four and four and two, the two at the back the only ones whose eyes were on the road and not on their own boots. He had all of it in the first minute. He lay there for another six hours and learned what the crew wanted out of the other five, which was not the count but the thing under the count. When the guards changed. Which of them looked back up the road when the head of the column went round a bend, and which did not. Where the man who was somebody stopped to piss, and how far he walked from the wagons to do it, which was a thing about the man and not about the road. What the drivers talked about, which Oran could hear from up there and Kael could not.

"They sleep at the river," Oran said, low. "The one on the second wagon has a woman at the river."

"How do you know it is a woman."

"Because he said her name twice and nobody laughed."

Kael filed it, and then he took it out again and looked at it, because it was not a thing his father's school would have had. His father would have had the four and the four and the two, and the bend, and the man walking too far from his wagons to relieve himself. His father would have had the convoy taken on the road above the river by dark, with the guards who did not look back doing exactly what they had done all day. He lay in the grass and built it his father's way and it was a good sum and it had eleven men dead in it, and then he took it apart, because nobody here was going to spend eleven men for anything, and he did not yet know what they were going to spend instead.

They did not take the convoy. They took one wagon out of it, at the river, after dark, while the man on the second wagon was with the woman whose name nobody had laughed at, and the guards were where they slept, and the thing that came off the wagon went onto a mule and the mule went up a track and the wagon was found in the morning where it had been left with its tarpaulin tied down exactly the way it had been tied. Four of the crew had done it. Kael had been forty strides off, on a stone, with the count on the two guards at the back, who were the only two who mattered, and the whole of his work was to put two fingers up if either of them stood.

Neither of them stood.

"That was for profit," he said to Borren, on the track, in the dark.

"Yes."

"Only for profit."

"What else is there," Borren said. It was not a question.

---

He learned the trade in pieces, from whichever of them had the piece.

Tirzah taught him to walk like a seller, which was not the same as walking. A seller's weight is in the front of the foot and his hands are never empty, and he does not look at faces, he looks at hands, because hands are where the money is. She walked him up and down the flat above a camp with a basket in his good hand until his feet had it, and then she took the basket away and made him do it with nothing to carry, which was harder, because a seller with nothing in his hands is a man pretending to be a seller.

"You walk like you are going somewhere," she said. "Sellers are not going anywhere. They are already there. Slower. No. That is a soldier being slow. Again."

Ambros taught him the voice. Not a new one: the same Koine he had been given at his mother's table, worn down until it had a town in it. The empire's tongue as it was spoken forty miles north of the capital by a man who wanted to sell you something and had sold it to better men than you. Kael had four languages and had never once thought about where in a mouth a word sat. Ambros made him say the same nine words for an hour beside a fire, the steward's face doing nothing, until the *r* had gone to the back of his throat and stayed there.

"Now say where you are from."

"Forty miles north of the capital."

"No. You have never been north of the capital in your life and neither has the man you are talking to. Say where you are from as though you were bored of it."

"Forty miles north of the capital."

"Yes," Ambros said, and got up, and that was the lesson.

One of the four of Ishareth, the one who had lost a warehouse and said so in eleven words, taught him locks. He did it without any words at all. He put a lock on a stone in front of Kael, an ordinary lock, the kind that had been on the warehouse, and he took a length of twine out of his coat and wet it in his mouth and fed it into the gap under the shackle, and worked it, and the twine went round the tongue of the thing where a key would have pushed it, and he leaned on it, and the lock came open. Then he shut it and gave Kael the twine. It took Kael most of a morning, one-handed, with the left hand holding the lock against the stone the way it could hold things now, which was by weight and not by grip. When it came open the man from Ishareth nodded once and took his lock back, and Kael understood that he had been given a thing worth more than a warehouse and that the man was never going to say so.

The road he learned to smell like by walking on it. That one nobody had to teach.

The drunks were Borren's.

"Not the drunkest," Borren said. They were on a bench outside a house in a town whose name Kael had been given that morning and was going to forget. "The drunkest man in a room knows nothing and will tell you all of it. You want the one who is drunk on purpose. He came here to be drunk and he is doing it carefully and he has something he came here to stop thinking about. That man will sell you what he is trying not to think about for the price of being asked."

"How do you know which he is."

"You will know. You count everything. Count how many times he looks at the door."

Kael counted. He counted seven men in a room for the length of a cup of something he did not drink, and one of them looked at the door eleven times in the time it took the others to look at it once, and Kael went and sat by that one and was bored at him in the empire's tongue with a town in it, and came out an hour later knowing which night the toll-keeper's strongbox went up the road and who carried it.

"Good," Borren said, when he told him, and it was the first time the man had said the word.

---

The princess had gone brown.

Not brown the way the south was brown, but the colour of a girl who had been out of doors every day for two months for the first time in her life and had not been allowed a scarf across her face, only across her hair. She had freckles now, over the nose, which Kael did not remember having seen before and had no way of knowing whether they had always been there under the paint. Her hands had gone hard in the way hands go when they do a thing every day. She did camp work. Nobody had told her to. She had watched Tirzah do a thing once and then she did it, badly, and then less badly, and by the third week she was doing it and nobody was watching, which was the whole of what she had wanted out of it, he thought, and was not sure.

She spoke to the crew in the Koine and in Ivrit and once, to Selis, in something that was neither and that Selis answered. Kael did not have that one. He put it with the things he did not have.

Valeria did not go brown. Valeria did not change at all, which he had stopped expecting her to.

She was with the princess. That was her post and she kept it the way she kept everything, without any visible cost, one half-step off the girl's shoulder in the camp and one mule behind her on the track and across the door of the tent at night, and the crew had stopped seeing it by the second week, because a thing that never changes is a thing that stops being there. She polished the plain sword every evening. She drilled when she could, on her own, on whatever flat there was, with a stick when a sword would have been seen and with the sword when it would not, and Kael, coming back off a job at dusk with the road on him, would come over the lip of the fold and see her there below him going through a thing he knew the name of, in her own school, exactly as she had gone through it in a yard on an island where the yard was not there any more.

He stood and watched her one evening for as long as it takes to hobble a mule. She knew he was there. She did not stop.

"You have not asked me where I go," he said, when she had finished.

"No."

"You do not want to know."

"I know where you go." She put the stick down where she could reach it. "You go where he sends you and you come back. If you did not come back I would want to know where. Until then it is his."

"That is very tidy."

"Yes."

He sat down on the slope. She did not sit. She stood the way she stood, which was the way a thing stands that has been put somewhere, and looked at the camp below, where the princess was carrying water with Tirzah and laughing at something Tirzah had said.

"You have kept it," Kael said.

"Kept what."

"What you are for."

She was quiet for a moment. He had the time on the quiet and did not need it.

"I was made for one thing," she said. "It is not difficult to keep a thing you were made for. It is the other kind that is difficult." Then, without turning her head: "You are getting better at it."

"At what."

"Whatever it is you are being made into. I watched you walk into the camp. I did not know it was you until you were past the fire."

She went down the slope to the water. He sat where he was. He found that he admired her, which was an old thing and he had a place for it, and under the admiring there was another thing that he did not have a place for, and he turned it over and looked at it, and it looked back. She had walked off that island exactly what she had been when she walked onto it. He was not sure what he had been when he walked onto it. He was sure it was not what was sitting on this slope.

He did not have a name for the second thing. He thought it might be envy. It was not a word his mother had ever put in front of him, and he could not have said whether that was because she had not thought he would need it or because she had known he would.

---

The evening he was certain Borren was wrong came in the sixth week.

There was a bridge. Not a bridge like the one at Ishareth: a wooden thing over a river that a man could have waded in summer and could not now, with a toll bar at the near end and a house beside the bar with soldiers in it. Six soldiers, white and blue, imperial, the first he had seen since the yard at the bridge-house. He had them by the middle of the afternoon, from a hill: six, one who was over the other five, two on the bar at any time and the rest in the house or behind it; the change at the sixth hour and again at dusk; the one who was over the others coming out to look at anything with more than three animals in it.

He came down off the hill and gave it to Borren the way he had given the man the yard rate at Ishareth, in order, short, and then he said the rest.

"There is a ford four miles up. I found it from the hill; there is a track down to it on this side and cattle have used it. We go up tonight, cross at first light, and we are on the far side of that bridge before the man in the house has had his breakfast, and he never sees a mule."

"Say it again shorter."

"Six soldiers. Ford four miles up. Tonight."

Borren looked at him with the one eye for about as long as he had looked at him in the doorway of the small room. Then he looked at the mules, which had rope on them, and at the crew, which was eighteen people who did not look like anything, and at the girl four mules back with her head covered, and back at Kael.

"We go over the bridge," he said. "Tomorrow. In the middle of the day. We pay the toll and we argue about it."

Kael did not say anything. He found that there was a great deal he could have said and that all of it was correct, and he stood there with it.

"You are going to do it anyway," Borren said. It was not unkind. "I told you there would be evenings."

"You told me some of them you would be wrong."

"Yes. Work out which kind this is tomorrow." He went back to his strap. "A rope train that goes four miles up a river to cross at a cattle ford is a thing a man in that house hears about by supper. Six men with nothing to do talk about what did not come past them. Nothing ever came past them that they will remember tomorrow. That is the whole trade. Go and hobble something."

---

He walked over the bridge at noon the next day behind the fourth mule with his good hand on its rope and the count running on six soldiers so hard he could feel it in his teeth.

Two on the bar. The one who was over the others came out of the house at the sight of the animals, as he had come out for everything with more than three, and walked down the line of them, and Kael had the man's hands and the man's eyes and where the man's weight was, and knew that if the man went to the seventh mule and put a hand up to the scarf of the girl walking beside it there was nothing on this earth that was going to stop Valeria, one mule behind her, and the bridge would have six bodies on it and a hunt with a name in it by the third week.

The man did not go to the seventh mule. He never got there. Tirzah met him at the third with the basket on her hip and a price in her mouth, the toll as it was said on the board against the toll as it had been last year against the toll as it was at the next bridge down, which she had never seen, and the whole of the man's attention went where she had put it, into the argument, which was the thing on that bridge that was making a noise. Ambros paid, in the end, in the voice, and short by a copper, and got the copper back off the soldier with a look, and the soldier laughed. Kael walked over a bridge with soldiers on it in daylight and nobody looked at his face once, because there was nothing on his face to look at, and there was a woman with a basket six feet away being difficult about a copper.

On the far side he walked for a mile before he took the count off the six and let them go.

He had been wrong. He turned it over on the road for the rest of the afternoon, because it did not come out the way he wanted it to come out. He had been wrong that the bridge could not be crossed. He did not know whether he had been wrong that the ford was better. Nobody had looked at the seventh mule; that was a fact. Whether nobody had looked because the trade was what Borren said it was, or because the man who was over the others had been the kind of man who would rather argue with a woman than count a mule, was not a fact, and there was no way to run it twice with one thing changed. He would never be on that bridge again. The man would forget them by supper. That was the point, and it was also the reason he would never know.

Borren dropped back beside him toward evening.

"Well," he said.

"You were right."

"I was right that we are on this side. Whether I was right is a different sum and neither of us is ever going to get to do it." He walked a few strides. "You had the six before I did. You had the ford. I would not have found the ford. Next time you find me one, I might use it."

"But not this time."

"This time there was a bridge," Borren said, "and a bridge is the thing they built to let us across."

---

He was started small, and he knew he was being started small, and he did it.

Distractions. He stood in the doorway of a shop in a town he would forget and dropped a jar, once, at the moment a man he had never seen went past the counter with something under his coat, and picked the pieces up and was sorry in the Koine with a town in it, and the shopkeeper never found out that he had been robbed until the evening and never found out how. Watch. A night on a stone above a yard with two fingers ready and nothing to raise them for. Drops: a thing left under a particular stone at a particular hour, a thing taken up from under another, and Kael never once knowing what was in either and finding, somewhere in the fourth week, that he had stopped needing to.

Then it was not small.

There was a shipment: he never learned of what, only that it went in four crates and that the crates had a lord's mark on them and a man riding with them who carried the lord's paper. The crew did not take it on the road. They took it at the inn, on the second night, out of the yard, by the front gate, at first light, on a cart with the lord's own mark chalked on the crates and Ambros on the box with a paper in his coat that had a seal on it as good as the lord's, because it had been made off the lord's, off wax that a drunk had let a boy sit next to. The man on the gate held it for them. Kael sat on the back of the cart with his feet hanging and the count on the window of the inn where the man with the real paper was still asleep, and they went out of a yard in daylight past a man who was helping them, and he understood the joke about the honest men in the whole of his body, and did not laugh, because it was not funny, it was correct.

There was a lord. Not a great one: a man with a house on a river with a name in the empire's shape and a face of this country, who drank at his own table with the door open to the hall because he was that kind of lord, and who kept a document in a chest that somebody, somewhere, had paid Borren for the misplacing of. Kael never knew who. He was the boy who carried the wine. He was the boy who carried the wine for four days, with the voice, and the walk, and his left hand held the way a boy holds a hand that has been hurt in a kitchen, which nobody looked at twice because kitchens hurt boys. On the fourth night the lord was drunk on purpose and Kael was bored at him, and the lord told the boy with the wine what he was trying not to think about, and it was the document, and where it was, and by morning it was somewhere else and the lord had misplaced it and would go on believing he had.

There were supplies, moved between two provinces that did not want them moved, over a hill, at night, on mules, in the rain, and at the top of the hill Kael looked down at the fires of a camp of soldiers whose job was the hill and counted them and put the mules past them on a track the soldiers had never walked because it did not go anywhere a soldier would want to go, and Borren, at the bottom, said nothing at all, and the nothing was the second time.

He was fast. He was quiet. He did what the job needed, at the moment it needed it, without being asked, and if it needed a thing that a boy on an island would not have done, he did that too, and he did not stop to look at the boy. He was not doing it because he wanted to. He had looked for the wanting, in the early weeks, the way he had once looked for the far edge of the sea, and it was not there, and after a while he stopped looking, because the job did not need it.

---

The night with the letter was the last of that month.

A house. A stair. A man on the stair who should not have been on it, according to the drunk, who had been wrong about that one thing and right about everything else. The letter was in the room at the top, under the seal of a lord of the east, one of the old lords the empire had left in place with a new name on him, and it was wanted by somebody who was paying, and Kael was the one who could get up a stair without the stair knowing.

He was on the fourth step from the top when the man came round the turn above him with a candle.

He had the man's hands. That was the whole of it. The man's right hand had the candle and the man's left hand came down for his collar, and Kael had already priced the stair, the drop, the door at the bottom, the two of the crew outside it, the rule. He had the rule before he had decided anything: not a body. He had never once had a rule arrive before the decision it was for; it arrived, and the knife came out of the left sleeve on its two turns of thread into his good hand, and he opened the inside of the man's forearm from wrist to elbow, along it, not across, where it would bleed and let go and keep the hand. The hand let go. The candle went down the stair ahead of him and went out. He went up past the man, who had sat down on the step and was holding his arm and had not yet started to make any noise about it, and he was in the room and out of it with the letter inside his boot in the time it took the man to draw the breath.

Nobody in the house woke. The man on the stair had not shouted. He would not have been able to say afterward why he had not shouted, and Kael could have told him: because a thing that is done that fast to you does not feel like a thing yet.

He came back over the lip of the fold at the first grey with blood on both his hands to the wrist. Most of it was the man's. Some of it was his own, out of the left hand, which had done a thing on the stair that it was not ready to do, and had opened along the old line to tell him so. He went past the fire without stopping. Borren was awake, because Borren was always awake when somebody came in, and looked at the hands, and at the boot, and did not ask about either.

"He will keep the arm," Kael said.

"I did not ask."

"I know."

He went down to the stream and put his hands in it. The water was cold in the way water is cold at the end of autumn, when the year has decided. He washed the man off and then he washed himself off and he knelt there with his hands in the stream and waited for the thing that came.

It did not come.

He knew the shape of it. He had knelt in water before with somebody else's blood coming off him and there had always been something that came after, out of the part of him that nobody had built, and it had never once been the same thing twice, and it had never once not come. He waited for it the way he would have waited for a number, and the number did not arrive. He looked for the pride, because there ought to have been pride, he had gone up a stair past a man with a candle and come down it with what he was sent for and left the man his arm, and it was clean, and it was the best thing he had done for the crew. It was not there. He looked for the other thing, the thing that would have argued, the thing that had not said one word in the room over the mule yard and not one word at the rope-walk, and it was not there either, and this was the third time, and he had the count on it.

He knelt in the stream with the letter in his boot and looked for anything at all.

The water went on being water. Behind him the camp was making itself for the day, one pair of hands at a time, and somebody had put a pot on, and the princess was laughing at something, low, on the far side of the fire, where the mules were, where his sister would be one half-step off her shoulder with the sun not yet up.

He took his hands out of the water and looked at them. They were clean. The left one had stopped bleeding. He got up, and went to see what there was to hobble, and did not take the letter out of his boot until Borren held out his hand for it, and never learned what it said.
