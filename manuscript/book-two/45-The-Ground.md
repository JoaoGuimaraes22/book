# Chapter Forty-Five — The Ground

Damir came to the forge door when there was grey in the sky and no colour in anything, and stood in it, and said that his son was gone.

He did not say it the way a man says a thing he has just found out. He said it the way you give a weight to somebody who is going to have to carry it, squarely, with the whole of it in the first sentence, and then he stood there and let Kael have it.

"His boots are gone and his coat is gone and the bread from the crock is gone," Damir said. "He has taken nothing else. He has not taken a mule."

Kael was still sitting on the anvil block. He got up off it, which took longer than it should have.

"When."

"I do not know when."

"You do."

Damir put the flat of his hand on the door frame and looked at the floor of his own forge.

"After the second band," he said. "He was on the bellows for the first one and he was not there for the second, and I did not look for him, because a man who has been up two nights does not go looking for his son."

Kael went past him into the yard.

He had had the Art back for four hours. There was nothing anywhere in it that would tell him which way a man had walked in the dark, so he did it the other way.

The light was not enough for what he wanted and he did it anyway, because there was no version of this where he waited an hour. He went along the inside of the wall to the gap and down onto the track, and got low, and put his face about a hand from the ground and went along it looking sideways into what light there was, which is how you find a print in the dark: not by looking down at it but by looking across it, so that the edge of it stands up and throws a shadow the width of a hair.

There were prints all over that track. There had been horses on it two days ago and forty houses' worth of feet since.

He found what he was looking for eleven strides up, where the track went over a wet place and the wet place had a skin on it.

The skin was broken. The break had a rim on it, and the rim had gone hard.

He stayed down over it for a while.

"He went up," he said. "Not down."

"Yes," Damir said, from the wall.

"Four hours. Five." He got his knees under him and stood. "It froze after he went through it. It froze hard and it has not started to come back yet, so he was through it before the cold came down properly and that was somewhere near the middle of the night."

Valeria had come out of the house behind them and was standing where she stood, at the gap in the wall, looking up the fold.

"Five hours," she said.

"Near it."

"On the road by now."

"He does not know where they are. They went on west along the top and he has to find them, and he cannot ask, because a man asking after soldiers is remembered." Kael was doing it out loud because doing it out loud was faster. "He has to go to the road and go along it until he finds a place they stopped, and then he has to go on until he finds them. That is not five hours. That is a day of walking and he will not be in front of anybody before the middle of the afternoon."

"Then we have until the middle of the afternoon."

"We have until he opens his mouth," Kael said. "Which is not the same thing."

He turned round.

Damir had not moved off the door frame. Miresh was in the house door with her hands not doing anything, which Kael had not seen before, and behind her, in the dark of the room, there was a small shape standing on the bed to see over her shoulder.

"Go and get him," Kael said, to Valeria, in the empire's tongue. "Do not hurt him. Bring him back down to me."

Valeria looked at him for about half a second, which from her was a question and an answer to it.

"He will not come."

"He will come. He will not be able to do anything else." Kael said it and knew as he said it that it was the first true thing he had thought all morning. "You cannot talk to him and he cannot talk to you, so do not try. Stand in front of him and take hold of him. He has seen you for a week and he has decided you are a girl of thirteen."

"He is wrong about that."

"Yes," Kael said. "Show him."

She went out of the yard and up the track, and after about forty strides she stopped walking, and after that Kael could not follow her with his eyes at all.

---

He got two hours up the fold before he had to stop, and he was still stopped when they came back down to him.

The fold narrowed as it went. It had been wide and grassy at the village and it got tighter and steeper the higher it went, and the trees came back into it — the needle ones, standing in the folds where the water ran, which were the first cover the three of them had seen in two months and which he had walked past in the dark seven nights ago without being able to see any of it.

Valeria came round the bend above him with Tarim in front of her and one hand on the back of his collar, and she was not doing anything with the hand. She was only leaving it there.

Kael was sitting on a stone at the side of the water with his arm up on his knee.

"Let go of him."

She let go.

Tarim stood in the middle of the track and looked like somebody who has been walking all night toward a thing and has arrived somewhere else.

He did not say anything.

"You are going up to tell them we were in your father's house," Kael said.

Tarim's chin came up a little.

"Yes."

"Say the rest of it."

"There is no rest of it."

"There is." Kael did not raise his voice, because he did not have the air for it and because it would have been the wrong instrument. "You are going up because if those men find out from anybody else — from a boy at a well, from a man selling a mule at Ishareth in the spring, from anybody — then it is your house first and then every house in this fold. So it has to come from you, and it has to come now, and if it comes from you then perhaps it is only your house. That is the sum."

Tarim was looking at him and had stopped looking at the ground.

"Yes," he said.

"It is the correct sum." Kael said it flatly, because it was a fact and there was no point in being anything about it. "I did it under your father's floor two days ago while that man was in your yard, and I got the same answer you got, and I said it out loud. I said I would give us up."

Nobody said anything.

"There is one term in it you have wrong," Kael said, "and it is the whole of it."

"Tell me the term."

"You think you are confessing that three strangers passed through. You are not." He got his feet under him and stood up, and did it well enough. "You are going to stand in front of a man with a list and tell him that your father kept us for a week. That your mother fed us out of her pot. That an old woman came twice a day for my arm. That your father put us on his own block and cut imperial iron off three people in his own forge with his own tools, for two nights, in a village where nobody works at night, and that forty houses lay in the dark and listened to it and not one of them came out."

He let that sit for a moment.

"They asked this village a question two days ago," he said. "Forty doors said no. You cannot hand us over without handing them that. There is no sentence in the world where you give us up and Doresh is not in it."

Tarim's mouth had come open somewhere in the middle of it.

"Then—" he said.

"Yes."

"Then what am I to do."

"There is one way this fold is standing next week," Kael said, "and it is that not one of those men rides back up that water. Not one. Not a boy holding horses."

Tarim laughed. It was not a laugh; it was the noise a man makes when the thing he is being told is so far outside what is possible that his body answers for him.

"There are twenty of them," he said.

"About that."

"You are a boy with one arm."

"Yes," Kael said.

He drew the sword.

It was a plain soldier's sword off a dead man in a wood, and he could hold it one-handed and that was all he could say for it, and the light in that fold was the flat grey light before the sun comes over a rise, which is the best light in the world for what he was about to do.

He put a thread into it.

It ran the length of the blade and stayed there, white, thin, absolutely steady, from the guard to the point, and it did not flicker and it did not spread, and it made no sound whatsoever, and Kael held it for four seconds and let it go.

It cost about what a hard breath costs. He had been doing it since he was eleven and he had done it ten thousand times, and it was the cheapest and smallest thing in the whole of his kit, and he had never once in his life been able to show it to somebody who did not already know what it was.

Tarim had gone backwards one step. He had not decided to.

"What is that," he said.

"It is the Art," Kael said. "It is what your iron was for. You have been looking at the wrong part of the story since the first night. You thought a man puts iron on children because they are children. He put it on us because of that."

Tarim looked at the sword and then at Kael's face and then at the sword again.

"You did that in my father's forge," he said slowly. "On the anvil. He said—"

"Yes."

"And the others."

Kael did not blink and did not look at Valeria, and he made the two words come out of him at exactly the weight of everything else he had said that morning, which is a harder piece of work than most of what he had ever done with a blade.

"She is stronger than I am," he said. "And so is the other one."

Half of it was true past anything he could have made Tarim understand. He had watched Valeria Vel put a grown man into a door frame and split a flagstone under one step; he had seen four Resonants hold a hillside and he had priced her against them and she had not come out badly.

The other half was a girl of fourteen with mended hands and forty words of Ivrit who had never spent a grain of anything in her life.

Tarim believed all of it. Kael watched him do it. He watched the whole of it arrive in a face he had read wrong twice in a week, and he knew exactly which of the two halves had done the work, and he took it.

"There is one more thing," Kael said, "and you should have it before you decide, because it is the only part of this that is worth anything to you afterward."

"Say it."

"The girl in your mother's kitchen is the daughter of the Emperor."

The water went on going down past them.

"I know," Tarim said.

"You knew what her hair was. You did not know what it is worth." Kael came a step closer, because this part wanted to be near. "Those men offered you every roof in Doresh. She goes home, and she says a word, and what comes back up this water is more than that man could carry on twenty horses, for you and for your father and for every house that shut its mouth for us. She will say it. I have heard her say it to people who did not deserve it."

Tarim stood there for a long time.

Behind them, a long way down the fold, one of the dogs at the near end of the village started up about something and then thought better of it.

"What do you want me to do," he said.

---

"You go up and you find them and you tell them the truth," Kael said. "All of it, in order, the way it happened, because you will have to say it more than once and a lie comes apart the second time. Your father kept us. You did not speak to them in the yard because you were frightened of what they would do to your house. You have come now because you want the money and you want us out of this fold."

"They will ask why my father did it."

"Because we made him. Because we came over his wall in the night and we were armed and there was a child of four in that house." Kael said it without any weight on it at all, and put it down where he put things. "It is not true and it will not have to be. They will not care about your father. They will care about where we are."

"Where do I say you are."

"Above the village. In the fold, in the trees, in the wet ground on the east side of the water, because the boy is dying and cannot be moved and the two girls will not leave him." He watched Tarim take it. "That is why you are there and they are not. That is why you have had two days to come and only came today."

"Will they believe it."

"They will believe it because it is what they already think," Kael said. "They have three children on foot with no horses and no food and one of them with an arm that has been going bad for a week. They have been telling each other that story since the wood. You are not going to have to sell it. You are going to have to stand still while they buy it."

He crouched, badly, and drew in the dust at the side of the track with his right forefinger.

"Here is the narrow. You know it better than I do."

"Above the split rock."

"Above the split rock. The rise on the west and the water on the east, and about eleven feet of track between them, and it goes on like that for a hundred strides and then it opens." Kael drew the two lines and the gap between them. "You bring them down through it. Not up. Down."

"They will come down anyway. It is the only way in."

"I know. I want you to be certain of it, because if any one of them says *we will go round by the top*, you say that there is no round, and you say it once and then you stop talking about it."

"There is no round," Tarim said.

"Good."

Kael put a mark in the dust at the bottom of the two lines.

"When you get to the split rock you stop, and you get down, and you do something to your boot. Take your time about it. Whatever they say to you, you go on doing it until you cannot hear a horse standing up."

Tarim looked at the mark for a while.

"How long do you need," he said.

"A day."

"That is not a lot."

"No," Kael said. "It is what there is."

Tarim rubbed the back of his neck with his hand, which was a thing his father did, and Kael had not noticed until that moment that he had ever seen the father do it.

"If they come tomorrow," Tarim said, "and it does not work—"

"Then it will not have mattered what you did this morning, and nobody in that fold will ever know you went up." Kael straightened. "That is the last honest thing I have got. Take it or do not."

There was one more piece and it was not a good piece, and Kael had been holding it since the wet place on the track, and he brought it out because it had to be his and not theirs.

"You will want to take something to them," he said. "They have had a man say *I have seen them* in every yard on that road for a week. Go down to your father and tell him I said to give you one of the bands."

"He put those under the floor."

"I know what he did with them. Tell him I said."

---

They went back down and told the village.

Kael had not intended to and it was not his to decide, and the headman took it out of his hands inside about a minute, which was the correct thing for the headman to do.

He came up to the smith's yard with the stick doing most of the work and three men behind him and stood in the middle of it and heard the whole of it out of Damir, and did not once look at Kael while he was hearing it.

Then he did look at him.

"If you are wrong about one man," he said, "we are all dead, and it will be the children first, because that is how it is done."

"Yes," Kael said.

"You do not argue."

"No. That is the arithmetic. I would rather you had it than not."

The old man stood there and worked his jaw.

"Four winters ago there were two boys," he said. "I have told you about them. Nobody came looking for those two."

"I know."

"You are not those two."

"No."

The headman turned round and looked down the length of his village, at the smoke going up off forty roofs into a sky with nothing in it, and Kael watched him do the sum. It did not take him very long. He was an old man in a poor place and he had been doing sums like it his whole life, and there was only one term in it that could still be moved.

"You have until they come," he said. "After that we are all in it together and there is nothing to decide. Damir. Whatever he asks you for."

"Yes," said Damir.

"And the children go down to the bottom houses tonight and stay there."

He went out of the yard.

It went round the village in about a quarter of an hour, and after that nobody in Doresh worked and nobody in Doresh stopped moving, and it was worse than the morning with the horses had been, because on the morning with the horses there had been nothing anybody could do and now there was.

Two families packed. Kael watched one of them go down the water at the middle of the day with a mule and a handcart, and nobody in the village said a word to them going past. It was the most decent thing he had seen since he came down that water, and it was done by people who were frightened of him.

---

The forge went all day.

Kael drew it in the dirt for Damir first: two pieces of rod, each bent double, each laid across the other and twisted at the middle, so that whichever way it lands one point stands up.

"I know what it is," Damir said. "I have made them. Not many."

"For what."

"Dogs. There was a year." He was already going through the rod. "How many."

"How many can you make."

"By dark? Two hundred. They are nothing to make. It is the drawing out that takes it, and I have rod."

He made them in tens on a board and Miresh carried the board out to the trough and quenched them and put them in a sack, and by noon the sack was too heavy for her and by dusk there were three sacks, and Kael counted every one of them out onto a cloth in the yard because he was going to have to know the number.

A hundred and eighty-eight.

He put them into three lots and did not explain the lots to anybody.

The child came out at some point in the afternoon and wanted to know what they were, and got told by her mother, in a voice that made her go back into the house without arguing, and that was the only time all day that Miresh spoke above the fire.

Kael went and stood in the forge door in the last of it.

"You did not ask me anything this morning," he said. "About your son."

Damir was at the anvil and did not stop.

"I did not need to."

"You have not asked me since, either."

"No."

The hammer went on for a while.

"He gave you his bed," Damir said. "Then he went up that water in the night to give you to a man with a list. Both of those are my son. I have had twenty years of him and I will get the rest of it when this is over, and I am not going to spend the day in between deciding which one he is." He turned the piece and struck it twice and dropped it in the water. "You are thirteen. When you are older you will know that there is no third thing under those two. There is just the two of them, and they are the same boy."

Kael stood in the door with his arm across his chest and did not have anything to say to that.

"Give me the iron back at the end of it," Damir said. "If there is an end of it."

---

The ground took them the rest of that day and most of the night. Valeria did the part of it that mattered.

Kael walked the hundred strides of the narrow four times before he let anybody put a hand on it. He walked it up and he walked it down, and then he walked it down again at the pace a horse walks, counting. Then he sat on the rise above it for an hour and looked at it and did nothing at all, which was the part of the work his father had never had to explain to him twice.

The track ran along the west side of the water with the rise going up on its left. For a hundred strides the rise was too steep and too loose for a horse and the water was too deep and too fast for one, and in between there was eleven feet of dust with stones in it.

You cannot beat twenty men.

You can beat five men, four times.

"Here," he said, at the top of it, where the narrow began and where a man coming down would still be seeing the open ground he had just left. "The first lot goes here, across the whole width, for about fifteen strides. Not thick. Thin, so that the front two go down and the rest stop."

"And the rest stop," Valeria said.

"The rest stop, and they are on the wrong side of it, and they are looking at two horses screaming on the ground in front of them in a place eleven feet wide." He went on down. "Then nothing at all for sixty strides. Nothing. They will get off the horses and they will start to come down on foot along the edges, and the man in charge will be shouting at them to hold, and about half of them will not hear him, and the ones who do not hear him will come down along the water side, because the rise is a climb and the water side is a walk."

He stopped at a place where the flat between the track and the water opened out to about three strides wide, with the grass on it flattened where the winter had left it.

"There," he said. "That is where a man walks when the road stops being a road. That is the only place in a hundred strides where he does not have to think about his feet."

Valeria looked at it.

"How many."

"How many can you do."

She did not answer for a moment, and Kael understood that she was not being difficult; she was pricing something she had not been able to price for seventy days and did not entirely have the shape of yet.

"Three," she said. "Properly. I could do four badly and then I would be no use to you tomorrow."

"Three."

She knelt down at the first place he marked and put both hands flat on the ground and left them there.

Kael did not watch the ground. He watched her, because he had spent four years learning to read what a working cost the person doing it and about nine of those minutes had ever been useful, and this was one of them.

Nothing much happened for a while. Then the grass went, all at once, over an area about the size of a door. The ground under it did not fly anywhere and did not lift. It went down and sideways at the same time, the way sand goes when you pull the bottom out of it, packing itself into the sides of the hole it was making, and it made almost no noise, and it took a long time.

When she stood up the hole was as deep as Kael was tall. The sides of it were harder than the ground around it. There was no spoil anywhere.

Valeria stood over it and breathed.

"That is one," she said.

"That is a good one."

"Do not talk to me for a bit."

The second took her longer and the third took her longer than that. By the end of the third she sat down on the track with her forearms on her knees and stayed there about a quarter of an hour. Kael sat four feet away and did not talk either, and they watched the last of the light go off the top of the fold on the far side.

"It is not the spending," she said eventually, to nobody. "It is that it answers."

"Yes."

"Seventy days." She turned one hand over and looked at the wrist where the band had been. "I put my hand on the wall of your smith's yard the first night the iron was off and I could feel the whole of that wall. Every stone in it, and where the weight of it went, and which one of them the whole thing is standing on."

Kael waited, and nothing else came.

"You did not say anything about it," he said.

"No," said Valeria, and got up, and went to look at the third hole.

---

The princess covered them.

It was not a job anybody gave her. She came up the fold in the last of the light with the old woman's boy carrying a skin behind her, and looked at the three holes, and looked at the branch-litter under the needle-trees on the rise, and started carrying.

She did it badly for about an hour and then she did it well.

The trick of it — and Kael had it off his father, on a mountain, at nine years old, for rabbits — is that you do not cover a hole with what is near the hole, because the ground you took it off is then bare and the bare place is what the eye finds. You bring it from a long way and you leave the ground you took it from looking like ground.

He told her that once and she did not need telling twice, and after that she went further up the fold each time, and came back with an armful, and knelt at the edge of the second hole laying the pieces one over another the way you tile a roof.

"How thin," she said.

"Thin enough that it will not hold a cat."

"That is not a measurement."

"No." He came over. "Lay a stick across it. If the stick holds without bending, it is too thick."

She laid a stick across it. It bent, and went in, and she got it out and started again at the edge without saying anything about it.

Damir came up at dark with the third sack and stayed to help, and knelt on the other side of the same hole from her, and they worked for a while without a word between them, because there were not any words between them.

Then she pointed at the litter in her hands and said something in Ivrit.

Damir looked up.

It was two words, and they were the wrong two words, and Kael, ten strides up the track, heard exactly how wrong they were and did not say anything.

Damir said the right ones, slowly.

She said them back.

He said them again, differently. Kael's Ivrit came off a wharf four hundred miles away in his mother's mouth and Damir's came off this fold. She got it the second time and put it in her mouth twice more to keep it.

"That is not how it is said," Kael called down.

"It is how it is said here," said Damir, without looking up.

"He is right," said the princess, in the empire's tongue, laying another handful across the sticks. "You have an accent. Everybody in this village has told me so and I have not understood one of them."

---

He told her at the top of the fold, afterward, while she was washing her hands in the water and he was sitting on the bank because standing had stopped being free some hours before.

"I sold something of yours this morning," he said.

She went on washing her hands.

"Tell me."

"I told Tarim that when you get home you will say a word and your father will send back to this village more than those men offered, for him and for his father and for every house that lied for us. I said you would say it. I did not ask you."

The princess sat back on her heels.

"Is that all," she said.

"It is a large thing to promise on somebody else."

"It is the only thing I have ever had," she said. "I offered it in that box in the second week and I offered it again in the fourth, word for word, to two people who were chained to the same wall I was. Nobody answered me either time." She dried her hands on the front of her dress, which was a village dress and had been Miresh's. "This is the first time anybody has taken it."

"It might not be worth anything."

"It might not be worth anything," she agreed. "But it will be said. I want that put where you can find it later: whatever else happens, it will be said, and I shall have it written down."

She stood up.

"You should have asked me," she said. "Not because you were wrong. Because I would have liked to be the one who said it."

---

They came at the middle of the next day.

The princess had the eye. It was the most important job on that ground and Kael gave it to her, because he had one hand and a fight to run and she was the only one of the three of them he could put somewhere and leave. He put her four hundred strides up the rise on the west side, above the top of the narrow, flat under a needle-tree in the litter with a grey cloak over her. From there she had the whole hundred strides and the open ground above it, and nothing on the track could get at her.

"You count them in," he said. "Every one. Riders and anybody walking, and if a man turns round and goes back up you do not shout and you do not move, because he will be gone before you have finished shouting. You only have to know the number."

"And afterward?"

"Afterward the number is the only thing in the world that matters," Kael said. "Do not lose it."

She lay down under the tree and pulled the cloak over herself, properly, feet first, the way he had shown her. She did not ask him whether she should look away.

He went down and took his place at the bottom of the hundred strides, at the mouth, where the rise came down to the water and the track went through between them at about the width of a cart.

Valeria went up onto the rise above the flat place with the holes in it, low, thirty feet over the track.

Then there was a great deal of nothing.

It was a bright cold day with a wind coming down the fold and the water loud in it. The birds went on doing what birds do until about a quarter of an hour before, and then stopped, and that was the first he knew.

He heard the horses before he saw anything.

Then Tarim came round the bend at the top, walking, on his own, forty strides in front of the first of them.

He came down to the split rock and stopped, got down on one knee, and started doing something to his boot.

The first two horses came past him at a walk.

---

The front horse went down about eleven strides in. It went down hard, knees first and then over, and the man on it went off the front and did not get up, because the horse came over on top of him.

The second horse did not go down. It stopped. It screamed. It stood on three legs with the fourth held up off the ground, turning in a circle in a place eleven feet wide, and the men behind it could not get past it or get away from it.

There were about six seconds in there where the whole thing might have gone the other way.

Then somebody at the back of the column shouted, and the shout had *ambush* in it, and after that it was arithmetic.

They came off the horses.

They came off them the way soldiers do, fast and correctly, backs to the rise, spears up, looking at the track in front of them. It was the right thing to do. It was the thing Kael had built the entire day around. Nobody looks at ground he has not walked on. He looks at the ground where the bad thing already happened.

A voice was shouting to hold, in Ivrit, off the flat somewhere.

About half of them held.

The other half did not hold. Eight of them came down along the water side, three together and then five behind, along the strip of flattened grass where a man does not have to think about his feet, moving fast because they thought the fight was in front of them.

The first man went in to the chest.

The second man went in beside him, because he was watching the first man. The litter gave in a piece about four feet across and took him down onto the other one, and something in one of them broke with a sound that carried the whole length of the narrow.

The third stopped at the edge with his arms out.

Valeria came off the rise onto his back.

Two more went into the second hole inside the next ten seconds, because men running at a thing do not stop when the man in front of them goes down. They go round him. Going round him on that strip meant going wide, and wide was where she had put it.

That left four of them standing on the grass with her.

Kael did not see most of what she did to those four, because he was at the bottom of the hundred strides and it happened sixty strides above him, and what reached him was the shape of it and not the detail. She had a soldier's sword in her right hand and a knife in her left, and both hands had been full for two days, which they had not been since the island. She went along that strip of grass the way weather goes along a coast.

Three of them never got a spear round.

The fourth got his round and got it into the ground and she went up the shaft of it.

Kael counted her and made himself stop counting her, because men were coming down the track toward him now, three of them, and the whole of his job was arriving.

He put the thread into the blade before they could see him, and stood in the middle of the mouth, which was eight feet wide, and waited.

The first one came at him hard, because he was a boy.

Kael did not trade. He had never once been able to, and everything he had was built on not being where the blow was. He took the man's spear off its line with the flat of the blade and stepped inside it. The spear went past his ear. He put the point up under the arm where the arm had come away from the body, and the threading took it through coat and leather as if neither had been there. He got out sideways. The man went down behind him, and the spear went into the water.

The second one had watched the first one and came in low and careful.

That one took longer and cost him. He was working one-handed against a grown man with a spear in eight feet of ground and there is no clever answer to that; there is only the answer of not being there, over and over, until the other man does something with his feet. It took eleven exchanges. Kael took a cut across the top of the shoulder on the eighth. The ninth was the man's mistake, a long step onto loose stone at the water's edge. The tenth put the sword into the inside of the thigh. The eleventh was not necessary and he did it anyway.

The third man saw the second man go down and turned round and ran back up the track.

Kael let him go. The alternative was chasing a grown man uphill on legs that had given out in a yard eight nights ago, and there were only two ways out of the hundred strides. Valeria was standing in the other one.

He heard where the man got to.

That was the first minute.

The rest of it took nine more and it was not a fight, and Kael stood in the mouth for the whole of it because standing in the mouth was his job and there was nothing else in the world for him to do.

Seven men were still at the top of the narrow with the officer, and they had three ways to go and none of them was a way. Back up the track was two dead horses and fifteen strides of ground that had already taken the front of the column. The rise was a climb with a girl standing over it. That left the strip along the water, one and two at a time, into a place they had just watched eight men walk into.

They came anyway. There is nothing else a man does in a narrow, and the officer was still saying his name.

Valeria worked back up the strip toward them.

She did not hurry any of it. That was the part Kael understood best and liked least: she took the ground she wanted first and then took the man, every time, and when two came at her together she moved so that they were one behind the other and dealt with the order she had made. One of them went wide off the strip to get round her and put himself into the third hole and did not come up out of it again. Another went into the water and was carried thirty feet and stood up in the shallows on the far side, and she was there before he had got his feet under him.

By the end of it there was one man across the water, going up the far side of the fold on his hands, a long way above everything.

Kael saw him and could not do anything at all about him, which he had known would be true of somebody from the moment he chose the ground and had put down where he put things.

---

It took ten minutes altogether and then it was quiet except for the horses and the men in the holes.

Kael came up the track along the water side with the sword still in his hand and the thread out of it. He looked at what was in the flat place. Then he went on up to the top of the narrow, where the first two horses were.

The princess came down off the rise with the cloak over her arm.

She was extremely white and her mouth was doing something at the corner. She came the whole way down without stopping. She did not look at the flat place as she went past it, which he had not asked of her and did not remark on.

"Nineteen," she said.

"You are certain."

"Seventeen mounted and two walking with the horses at the back. Nineteen, and Tarim, and I did not count Tarim." She had her hands folded in front of her and they were absolutely still, which he had learned to read a month ago and had filed under the wrong heading. "Nobody went back up. Nobody went past me. I watched the top of it the whole time and there was nothing on it."

"Say the number again."

"Nineteen."

Kael went along them and counted. He got eighteen, and then he counted again from the other end and got eighteen again, and the two numbers stood there in front of him and agreed with each other about a man on a rise.

"Valeria."

She came down off the flat place. There was a great deal on her and none of it was hers.

"Eighteen," he said. "She has nineteen. One of them got across the water and went up the far side on his hands about four minutes ago, and I could not reach him."

Valeria did not ask him anything about it. She went up the fold at a run without waiting to be told, and was out of sight in about nine seconds.

It took her an hour and ten minutes.

Kael sat at the top of the narrow for the whole of it, back against the rock, arm across his chest, able to do nothing whatever about any of it. Tarim sat about fifteen feet off. Neither of them said anything for the first half hour.

"I did not know," Tarim said, at last.

"No."

"I have seen men fight. There was a year when they came up the road both ways." He had his hands hanging off his knees and he was not looking at anything. "That was not fighting."

"No," Kael said. "It was not meant to be."

When Valeria came back down she was alone and she was walking. She did not say anything. She went past them, sat down at the water and put her hands in it.

"Nineteen," Kael said.

"Nineteen," she said.

---

Four of the men in the holes were alive.

Kael went along the edge of the flat place and looked into each of them in turn, and the fourth one, who was the one with the flat accent and the list, was lying on his side in the bottom of the second hole with a leg that had gone the wrong way at the knee and no way of getting out and about an hour left in him, and he looked up at a thirteen-year-old boy standing on the edge of his grave with one arm in a sling.

Kael crouched down. It hurt to crouch. He kept the count on the man's pulse anyway, without deciding to, by watching the side of his throat, which was a thing he did now.

"I am going to ask you three things," he said, in Ivrit. "You will hear the difference in how I ask them."

"You are the islander," the man said.

"Where is the governor of Qeshet."

The man laughed and it turned into something else.

"Ishareth," he said. "He went east four days after you got out of that box. He is at Ishareth and he is buying men. He had forty left and he did not think forty was enough for three children, and he was correct, was he not."

The count stayed level the whole way through it. It went up on *buying men* and came back down, which is what a pulse does on a word a man is proud of, and it did not do the other thing anywhere.

"How many is he buying."

"As many as the letter he wrote will pay for." The man's breath went out of him and came back badly. "You will not get near that city. There is one bridge and it is his."

"That is two," Kael said. "Here is the third. What did he tell you about why."

"Why what."

"Why three children in a box. Why he did not sell you the reason." Kael leaned a little. "You have been on that road for two months and forty of you have been wondering. What did he tell you."

"He told us nothing," the man said. "He has told nobody anything since the day he shut that door. He rode at the back and he looked behind him and he told us nothing."

The count did not move on any of it.

Kael stayed there a moment longer.

"Water," the man said.

Kael gave him some.

Then he stood up and went along the other three, and asked each of them the first question and got the same city out of two of them and nothing out of the third, and when he had what he had come for he did what had to be done about the four of them, and it did not take very long, and the part of him that would have argued about it did not say anything at all.

It was afterward, walking back up to the split rock, that he noticed it had not.

He put that down where he put things and went on walking.

---

They gave Doresh the horses.

There were thirteen of them standing about in the narrow by the middle of the afternoon, two dead and two that had to be put down, and Kael would have taken three east and did not, and the reason he gave was the true one and was not the whole of it.

"Three children arrive at a city gate on cavalry horses with a governor's brand on the shoulder," he said. "The gate does not have to be clever. It only has to be able to read."

"They are evidence," Damir said. He said it the way he had said it about the iron, standing in the middle of his own yard with his son thirty feet away and neither of them looking at the other. "The same as the rest of it."

"They are also thirteen horses."

"Yes," said the headman, who had come up the fold behind him, "they are," and that was the end of the discussion. "One at a time. Down the water, in the spring, and again the spring after, where the men who buy do not ask and would not know the brand if they did. It will take four years and nobody in this fold has ever had anything that took four years."

The rest of it went into the water with stones in the coats, a long way up. The ground got put back over the holes. It rained that night, which Kael thought was the only piece of luck any of them had been handed since the box.

---

They argued about it that evening in the smith's house with the door shut, in the empire's tongue, and it was the first real argument the three of them had ever had.

"Solvenna," the princess said.

She said it the way she had ruled the rooms at Qeshet, and Kael understood that it was not a suggestion and had not been meant as one.

"There is a garrison at Solvenna and it is the empire's own ground and it is where my father's road begins. I have been in a box for two months and I have been under a floor and I have been hidden in a kitchen with a cloth on my head, and I would like very much to stop." She had her chin up. "I am not going to pretend that I would not."

"How do we get to Solvenna," Kael said.

"West."

"Say the rest of it."

"I do not know the rest of it," she said. "That is your part."

"Then I will say it." He had drawn nothing and he did not need to. "West is the road we came up. Two months of it, in the open, with wells a day apart that everything on that road has to stop at. That man's forty are on it, less nineteen, and the twenty-one that are left are between here and Qeshet or they are at Ishareth with him. There is no cover for eight days. And every one of those wells has had a man at it in the last three weeks describing your hair."

"And Ishareth."

"Six or seven days for a loaded train, so eight or nine for us. Down off the Kharoun to a river, and a city, and the last of the empire before the river." He looked at Valeria, who had said nothing. "The road east is the one road on this continent that they have already searched, because they searched it coming the other way. And a city is the only place in the world where three people can be nobody at all."

"And the man who is hunting me is in it," the princess said.

"Yes."

"You are aware that you have just made the argument for the place he is."

"I am aware of it," Kael said.

There was a silence in that room that went on a little too long.

"Say the true one," Valeria said.

Kael did not look at her. He had known that whichever of the two of them made him say it out loud, it was going to be Valeria, and she had waited until the room was quiet to do it.

"He is the only person alive who knows where my mother is," he said. "And my brother. And the old man. He put us in that box and he took us out of that house and he is the only thread there is, and there is not going to be a second one."

"Yes," Valeria said.

"It does not make the rest of it wrong."

"No," she said. "It does not. That is the difficulty."

She turned to the princess, and did the thing Kael had watched her do at a governor's table and on a beach and on the boards of a boat, which was to stand at somebody's left shoulder and say the true thing in the flattest voice in the world.

"Highness. He is right about the west road. I would go west if there were a way to go west. There is not, and I would rather be in a city than on eight days of open ground with you." She paused for exactly as long as it took to decide something. "I do not like the rest of it. I am saying so once and then I will not say it again."

The princess sat and looked at her hands.

"There is the other thing," she said. "Nobody has mentioned it and I am going to."

"Say it."

"That boy went up a road for us and his village stood in the dark for two nights and did not open its mouth, and you told him that we would get rid of them for good." She looked up. "We have got rid of nineteen. The man who sent them is buying more, six days east, and if we walk away west he comes back down this water in the spring and there is nobody here who can do anything at all about it."

Nobody said anything for a while.

"That is not why we are going," Kael said.

"No," said the princess. "But it is true, and I am not going to let us leave without it having been said in this room."

---

They went at first light with three days of bread and the water skins full and a village standing in the track.

Miresh put her hands on the princess's shoulders and said something to her, at some length. The princess did not have a tenth of it. She stood there and let the whole of it be said, and then answered in Ivrit, badly, with about six words, and Miresh put her forehead down on the top of the girl's head for a moment and let go of her.

Neshah had been carried out in a blanket and was furious about everything.

The old woman came up the track at the last minute with a linen roll and a clay pot and put them into Kael's coat herself. She told him the thing about the arm she had told him on the second morning. She told him again what she thought of ten days against six. She did not wish him luck, which she did not appear to believe in.

Damir gave him the iron.

He had dug it out from under his forge floor in the night, cleaned it, and put it in a piece of sacking: five bands, the chain, one band still shut on nothing. He put it into Kael's hands the way you hand somebody a tool.

"You said it was evidence," Kael said.

"It is."

"Then why."

Damir looked down the fold at the water going away north. Then east, up at the top of it, where the ground went back onto the Kharoun and the road ran along the south skirt for six or seven days to a river.

"Because it is not evidence here," he said. "There is nobody in this fold to show it to."

Tarim was standing at the wall and did not come over, and Kael went to him instead.

They stood there for a second.

"When it is said," Tarim said, "and somebody comes up this water with it in writing — how will I know it is not another man with a list."

Kael thought about that.

"You will not," he said. "You will have to decide."

"That is a poor answer."

"It is the only honest one I have got." He put out his right hand, which is not a thing they do in that fold, and Tarim looked at it and then took it. "You did the correct sum twice in one week. The second time you did it with a term in it that nobody in your village had. Keep doing that and you will be all right."

Then the three of them went up the fold, past the split rock and through the hundred strides, where the ground had been put back and the rain had been on it and there was nothing to see. They came up onto the top of the Kharoun in the middle of the morning and turned east.

Kael went about two hundred strides along the south skirt, then stopped and looked back, once, at the fold going down into the north with the smoke standing up out of it.

Then he stopped counting the strides, which he had been doing without noticing since the split rock, and started again from one.
