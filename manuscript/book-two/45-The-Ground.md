# Chapter Forty-Five — The Ground

Damir came to the forge door when there was grey in the sky and no colour in anything, and stood in it, and said that his son was gone.

He did not say it the way a man says a thing he has just found out. He said it the way you give a weight to somebody who is going to have to carry it, squarely, with the whole of it in the first sentence, and then he stood there and let Kael have it.

"His boots are gone and his coat is gone and the bread from the crock is gone," Damir said. "He has taken nothing else. He has not taken a mule."

Kael was still sitting on the anvil block. He got up off it, which took longer than it should have.

"When."

"I do not know when."

Kael looked him in the eyes.

"Yes, you do."

Damir put the flat of his hand on the door frame and looked at the floor of his own forge.

"After the second band," he said. "He was on the bellows for the first one and he was not there for the second."

Kael went past him into the yard, and the chase was on.

He went along the inside of the wall to the gap and down onto the track, and got low, and put his face about a hand from the ground and went along it looking sideways into what light there was, which is how you find a print in the dark: not by looking down at it but by looking across it, so that the edge of it stands up and throws a shadow the width of a hair.

There were prints all over that track. There had been horses on it two days ago and forty houses' worth of feet since.

He found what he was looking for eleven strides up, where the track went over a wet place and the wet place had a skin on it.

The skin was broken. The break had a rim on it, and the rim had gone hard.

He stayed down over it for a while.

"He went up," he said. "Not down."

"Yes," Damir said, from the wall.

"Four hours. Five." He got his knees under him and stood. "It froze after he went through it. It froze hard and it has not started to come back yet, so he was through it before the cold came down properly and that was somewhere near the middle of the night."

Valeria had come out of the house behind them.

"Five hours," she said.

"Near it."

"He's on the road by now."

"He does not know where they are. They went on west along the top and he has to find them, and he cannot ask, because a man asking after soldiers is remembered." Kael was doing it out loud because doing it out loud was faster. "He has to go to the road and go along it until he finds a place they stopped, and then he has to go on until he finds them. That is not five hours. That is a day of walking and he will not be in front of anybody before the middle of the afternoon."

"Then we have until the middle of the afternoon."

"We have until he opens his mouth," Kael said. "Which may not be the same thing."

He turned round.

Damir had not moved off the door frame. Miresh was in the house door with her hands not doing anything, which Kael had not seen before, and behind her, in the dark of the room, there was a small shape standing on the bed to see over her shoulder.

"Go and get him," Kael said, to Valeria, in the empire's tongue. "Do not hurt him. Bring him back down to me."

Valeria looked at him for about half a second, which from her was a question and an answer to it.

"He will not come."

"He will come. He will not be able to do anything else." Kael said it cold. "You cannot talk to him and he cannot talk to you, so do not try. Stand in front of him and take hold of him. He has seen you for a week and he has decided you are a girl of thirteen."

"He is wrong about that."

"Yes," Kael said. "Show him."

She nodded, and went out of the yard and up the track, and after about forty strides she stopped walking, and after that Kael could not follow her with his eyes at all.

---

He got two hours up the fold before he had to stop, and he was still stopped when they came back down to him.

The fold narrowed as it went. It had been wide and grassy at the village and it got tighter and steeper the higher it went, and the trees came back into it.

Valeria came round the bend above him with Tarim in front of her and one hand on the back of his collar.

Kael was sitting on a stone at the side of the water with his arm up on his knee.

"You can let go of him now Val."

She let go.

Tarim stood in the middle of the track and looked like somebody who has been walking all night toward a thing and has arrived somewhere else.

He did not say anything.

"You are going up to tell them we were in your father's house," Kael said.

Tarim's chin came up a little.

"Yes."

"Say the rest of it."

"There is no rest of it."

"There is." Kael did not raise his voice, because he did not have the air for it and because it would have been the wrong instrument. "You are going up because if those men find out from anybody else — from a boy at a well, from a man selling a mule at Ishareth in the spring, from anybody — then it is your house first and then every house in this fold. So it has to come from you, and it has to come now, and if it comes from you then perhaps you, and all other, may yet be saved. That is the sum."

Tarim was looking at him and had stopped looking at the ground.

"Yes," he said.

"It is the correct sum." Kael said it flatly, because it was a fact and there was no point in being anything about it. "I did it under your father's floor two days ago while that man was in your yard, and I got the same answer you got. I would give us up too, were I in your position."

Nobody said anything.

"But, there is one term in it you have wrong," Kael said, "and it is the whole of it."

"Tell me the term."

"You think you are confessing that three strangers passed through. You are not." He got his feet under him and stood up, and did it well enough. "You are going to stand in front of a man with a list and tell him that your father kept us for a week. That your mother fed us out of her pot. That an old woman came twice a day for my arm. That your father put us on his own block and cut imperial iron off three people in his own forge with his own tools, for two nights, in a village where nobody works at night, and that forty houses lay in the dark and listened to it and not one of them came out to confess."

He let that sit for a moment.

"They asked this village a question two days ago," he said. "Forty doors said no. You cannot hand us over without handing them that. There is no sentence in the world where you give us up and Doresh is not in it."

Tarim's mouth had come open somewhere in the middle of it.

"Then—" he said.

"Yes."

"Then what am I to do."

"There is one way this village is standing next week," Kael said, "and it is that not one of those men rides back up that water. Not one."

Tarim laughed. It was not a laugh; it was the noise a man makes when the thing he is being told is so far outside what is possible that his body answers for him.

"There are twenty of them," he said.

"About that."

"You are a boy with one arm."

"Yes," Kael said.

He drew the sword.

It was a plain soldier's sword off a dead man in a wood, and he could hold it one-handed and that was all he could say for it, and the light in that fold was the flat grey light before the sun comes over a rise, which is the best light in the world for what he was about to do.

He put a thread into it.

It ran the length of the blade and stayed there, white, thin, absolutely steady, from the guard to the point, and it did not flicker and it did not spread, and it made no sound whatsoever, and Kael held it for four seconds and let it go.

It cost about what a hard breath costs. He had been doing it since he was eleven and he had done it thousands of times, and it was the cheapest and smallest thing in the whole of his kit.

Tarim had gone backwards one step. He had not decided to.

"What is that," he said.

"It is the Art," Kael said. "It is what your iron was for. You have been looking at the wrong part of the story since the first night. You thought a man puts iron on children because they are children. He put it on us because of that."

Tarim looked at the sword and then at Kael's face and then at the sword again.

"You did that in my father's forge," he said slowly. "On the anvil. He said—"

"Yes."

"And the others."

Kael did not blink and did not look at Valeria, and he made the two words come out of him at exactly the weight of everything else he had said that morning, which is a harder piece of work than most of what he had ever done with a blade.

"She is stronger than I am," he said. "And so is the other one."

Half of it was true past anything he could have made Tarim understand. He had watched Valeria Vel put a grown man into a door frame and split a flagstone under one step; he had seen four Resonants hold a hillside and he had priced her against them and she had not come out badly.

The other half was a girl of fourteen with mended hands and forty words of Ivrit who had never spent a grain of anything in her life.

Tarim believed all of it. Kael watched him do it. He watched the whole of it arrive in his face.

"There is one more thing," Kael said, "and you should have it before you decide, because it is the only part of this that is worth anything to you afterward."

"Say it."

"As you now know, that silver haired girl in your mother's kitchen is the daughter of the Emperor."

The water went on going down past them.

"I know," Tarim said.

"You knew what her hair was. You did not know what it is worth." Kael came a step closer, because this part wanted to be near. "Those men offered you every roof in Doresh. She goes home, and she says a word, and what comes back up this water is more than that man could carry on a hundred horses, for you and for your father and for every house that shut its mouth for us. She will say it. I have heard her say it to people who did not deserve it."

Tarim stood there for a long time.

Behind them, a long way down the fold, one of the dogs at the near end of the village started up about something and then thought better of it.

"What do you want me to do," he said.

---

"You go up and you find them and you tell them the truth," Kael said. "All of it, in order, the way it happened, because you will have to say it more than once and a lie comes apart the second time. Your father kept us. You did not speak to them in the yard because you were frightened of what they would do to your house. You have come now because you want the money and you want us out of this fold."

"They will ask why my father did it."

"Because we made him. Because we came over his wall in the night and we were armed and there was a child of four in that house." Kael said it without any weight on it at all. "It is not true and it will not have to be. They will not care about your father. They will care about where we are."

"Where do I say you are."

"Above the village. In the fold, in the trees, in the wet ground on the east side of the water, because the boy is dying and cannot be moved and the two girls will not leave him." He watched Tarim take it. "That is why you are there and they are not. That is why you have had two days to come and only came today."

"Will they believe it."

"They will believe it because it is what they already think," Kael said. "They have three children on foot with no horses and no food and one of them with an arm that has been going bad for a week. You are not going to have to sell it. You are going to have to stand still while they buy it."

He crouched, badly, and drew in the dust at the side of the track with his right forefinger.

"Here is the narrow. You know it better than I do."

"Above the split rock."

"Above the split rock. The rise on the west and the water on the east, and about eleven feet of track between them, and it goes on like that for a hundred strides and then it opens." Kael drew the two lines and the gap between them. "You bring them down through it. Not up. Down."

"They will come down anyway. It is the only way in."

"I know. I want you to be certain of it, because if any one of them says *we will go round by the top*, you say that there is no round, and you say it once and then you stop talking about it. Yes?"

"There is no round," Tarim said.

"Good."

Kael put a mark in the dust at the bottom of the two lines.

"When you get to the split rock you stop, and you get down, and you do something to your boot. Take your time about it. Whatever they say to you, you go on doing it until you cannot hear a horse standing up."

Tarim looked at the mark for a while.

"How long do you need," he said.

"A day."

"That is not a lot."

"No," Kael said. "It is all that is needed."

Tarim rubbed the back of his neck with his hand, which was a thing his father did.

"If they come tomorrow," Tarim said, "and it does not work—"

"Then it will not have mattered what you did this morning, and nobody in that fold will ever know you went up." Kael straightened. "That is the last honest thing I have got. Take it or do not."

There was one more piece and it was not a good piece, and Kael had been holding it since the wet place on the track, and he brought it out because it had to be his and not theirs.

"You will want to take something to them," he said. "They have had a man say *I have seen them* in every yard on that road for a week. Go down to your father and tell him I said to give you one of the bands."

"He put those under the floor."

"I know what he did with them. Tell him I said."

---

They went back down and told all of it to Damir.

"If you are wrong," he said, "we are all dead, and it will be the children first, because that is how it is done."

"Yes," Kael said.

"You do not argue."

"No. That is the arithmetic. I would rather you had it than not."

The old man stood there and worked his jaw.

"Four winters ago there were two boys," he said. "I have told you about them. Nobody came looking for those two."

"I know."

"You are nothing like those two."

"No."

Damir turned round and looked down the length of the village, at the smoke going up off forty roofs into a sky with nothing in it, and Kael watched him do the sum. It did not take him very long. He was an old man in a poor place and he had been doing sums like it his whole life, and there was only one term in it that could still be moved.

"These kinds of men, men who'll take children. I have no pity for them," he said. "Doresh stands by children. We are all in this together and there is nothing to decide. Anything you need, you'll have it. And the children will go down to the bottom houses tonight and stay there."

He went out of the yard.

It went round the village in about a quarter of an hour, and after that nobody in Doresh worked and nobody in Doresh stopped moving.

---

The forge went all day.

Kael drew it in the dirt for Damir first: two pieces of rod, each bent double, each laid across the other and twisted at the middle, so that whichever way it lands one point stands up.

"I know what it is," Damir said. "I have made them. Not many."

"For what."

"Dogs. There was a year." He was already going through the rod. "How many."

"How many can you make."

"By dark? Two hundred. They are nothing to make. It is the drawing out that takes it, and I have rod."

He made them in tens on a board and Miresh carried the board out to the trough and quenched them and put them in a sack, and by noon the sack was too heavy for her and by dusk there were three sacks, and Kael counted every one of them out onto a cloth in the yard because he was going to have to know the number.

A hundred and eighty-eight.

He put them into three lots and did not explain the lots to anybody.

The child came out at some point in the afternoon and wanted to know what they were, and got told by her mother, in a voice that made her go back into the house without arguing, and that was the only time all day that Miresh spoke above the fire.

Kael went and stood in the forge door in the last of it.

"You did not ask me anything this morning," he said. "About your son."

Damir was at the anvil and did not stop.

"I did not need to."

"You have not asked me since, either."

"No."

The hammer went on for a while.

"He gave you his bed," Damir said. "Then he went up that water in the night to give you to a man with a list. Both of those are my son. I have had twenty years of him and I will get the rest of it when this is over, and I am not going to spend the day in between deciding which one he is." He turned the piece and struck it twice and dropped it in the water. "You are smart, but still, a child. When you are older you will know that there is no third thing under those two. There is just the two of them, and they are the same boy."

Kael stood in the door with his arm across his chest and did not have anything to say to that.

"Give me the iron back at the end of it," Damir said. "If there is an end of it."

---

The ground took them the rest of that day and most of the night. Valeria did the part of it that mattered.

Kael walked the hundred strides of the narrow four times before he let anybody put a hand on it. He walked it up and he walked it down, and then he walked it down again at the pace a horse walks, counting. Then he sat on the rise above it for an hour and looked at it and did nothing at all, which was the part of the work his father had never had to explain to him twice.

The track ran along the west side of the water with the rise going up on its left. For a hundred strides the rise was too steep and too loose for a horse and the water was too deep and too fast for one, and in between there was eleven feet of dust with stones in it.

It would be only him and Valeria, versus twenty. Perhaps it would've been possible, it was them two after all. But perhaps was not good enough. Twenty men would not be an easy task.

But five men, four times. That would be easy.

"Here," he said, at the top of it, where the narrow begins and where a man coming down would still be seeing the open ground he had just left. "The first lot goes here, across the whole width, for about fifteen strides. Not thick. Thin, so that the front two go down and the rest stop."

"And the rest stop," Valeria said.

"The rest stop, and they are on the wrong side of it, and they are looking at two horses screaming on the ground in front of them in a place eleven feet wide." He went on down. "Then nothing at all for sixty strides. Nothing. They will get off the horses and they will start to come down on foot along the edges, and the man in charge will be shouting at them to hold, and about half of them will not hear him, and the ones who do not hear him will come down along the water side, because the rise is a climb and the water side is a walk."

He stopped at a place where the flat between the track and the water opened out to about three strides wide, with the grass on it flattened where the winter had left it.

"There," he said. "That is where a man walks when the road stops being a road. That is the only place in a hundred strides where he does not have to think about his feet. We can trap them. I need some big holes."

Valeria looked at it.

"How many."

"How many can you do."

She did not answer for a moment, and Kael understood that she was not being difficult; she was pricing something she had not been able to price for seventy days and did not entirely have the shape of yet.

"My Art is still returning to me Kael. Three," she said. "Properly. I could do four badly and then I would be no use to you tomorrow."

"Three it is then."

She knelt down at the first place he marked and put both hands flat on the ground and left them there.

Kael did not watch the ground. He watched her. It would never be a bad decision to watch Valeria Vel working her Art.

Nothing much happened for a while. Then the grass went, all at once, over an area about the size of a door. The ground under it did not fly anywhere and did not lift. It went down and sideways at the same time, the way sand goes when you pull the bottom out of it, packing itself into the sides of the hole it was making.

When she stood up the hole was as deep as Kael was tall. The sides of it were harder than the ground around it. There was no spoil anywhere.

Valeria stood over it and breathed.

"That is one," she said.

"That is a good one."

"No. I'm almost embarrassed at my lack of power. Do not talk to me for a bit."

The second took her longer and the third took her longer than that. By the end of the third she sat down on the track with her forearms on her knees and stayed there about a quarter of an hour. Kael sat four feet away and did not talk either, and they watched the last of the light go off the top of the fold on the far side.

"Now I know you feel most of the time," she said eventually. "Having such low capacity and still remain a capable Resonant is indeed a difficult task."

"Somehow that feels like both an insult and a compliment."

"It is neither. Just a simple fact." She turned one hand over and looked at the wrist where the band had been. "Seventy days. The moment those irons were off me, I put my hand on the wall of that smith's yard and I could *feel* the whole of that wall. Every stone in it, where the weight of it went, and which one of them the whole thing is standing on. It's as if the Art was something like my eyesight, or my sense of smell, that I suddenly had lost. As if I had lost a part of the world. And perhaps a part of myself too."

Kael waited, and responded after a few seconds of thinking.

"I agree. My mother taught me that knowledge makes you see the world differently. There are many methods to understand what's around us. Some perhaps are unique to some, and those people will see a world that other wills never be able to glance at."

He looked at her.

"I wish I could see the world as you do Val," he said.

"I'm sure your world is much more interesting," said Valeria, and got up, and went to look at the third hole.

---

Later, Kael watched the princess cover the holes.

It was not a job anybody gave her. She came up the fold in the last of the light with the old woman's boy carrying a skin behind her, and looked at the three holes, and looked at the branch-litter under the needle-trees on the rise, and started carrying.

She did it badly for about an hour and then she did it well.

The trick of it — and Kael had it off his father, on a mountain, at nine years old, for rabbits — is that you do not cover a hole with what is near the hole, because the ground you took it off is then bare and the bare place is what the eye finds. You bring it from a long way and you leave the ground you took it as it was.

He told her that once and she did not need telling twice, and after that she went further up the fold each time, and came back with an armful, and knelt at the edge of the second hole laying the pieces one over another the way you tile a roof.

"How thin," she said.

"Thin enough that it will not hold a cat."

"That is not a measurement I can follow."

"No." He came over. "Lay a stick across it. If the stick holds without bending, it is too thick."

She laid a stick across it. It bent, and went in, and she got it out and started again at the edge without saying anything about it.

Damir came up at dark with the third sack and stayed to help, and knelt on the other side of the same hole from her, and they worked for a while without a word between them.

Then she pointed at the litter in her hands and said something in Ivrit.

Damir looked up.

It was two words, and they were the wrong two words, and Kael, ten strides up the track, heard exactly how wrong they were and did not say anything.

Damir said the right ones, slowly.

She said them back.

He said them again, differently. She got it the second time and put it in her mouth twice more to keep it.

"It is how it is said here," said Damir, without looking up.

"Thank you," said the princess, in Ivrit, laying another handful across the sticks. "I continue. Please teach again more."

It was not perfect. But, she had learned a lot in a few days. She might have a knack for this kind of thing, Kael thought.

---

He told her at the top of the fold, afterward, while she was washing her hands in the water and he was sitting on the bank.

"I sold something of yours this morning," he said.

She went on washing her hands.

"Tell me."

"I told Tarim that when you get home you will say a word and your father will send back to this village more than those men offered, for him and for his father and for every house that lied for us. I said you would say it. I did not ask you."

The princess sat back on her heels.

"Is that all," she said.

"It is a large thing to promise on somebody else."

"It is the only thing I have ever had," she said. "I offered it in that box when we were in chains and I offered it many more times in the past, word for word, for any petty reason. Nobody answered me in that box, tho. Perhaps the first time it has happened to me." She dried her hands on the front of her dress, which was a village dress and had been Miresh's. "This is the first time such words have been truly useful. I'm glad."

"It might not be worth anything."

"It might not be worth anything," she agreed. "But once we get back, it will be said. I want that put where you can find it later: whatever else happens, it will be said, and I shall have it written down."

She stood up.

"You should have asked me," she said. "Not because you were wrong. Because I would have liked to be the one who said it."

---

They came at the middle of the next day.

The princess had the eye. It was the most important job on that ground and Kael gave it to her. He surprised even himself. This princess was not the same one he had once seen at the top of an arena, and he knew it. 

He had one hand and a fight to run and she was the only one of the three of them he could put somewhere and leave. He put her four hundred strides up the rise on the west side, above the top of the narrow, flat under a needle-tree in the litter with a grey cloak over her. From there she had the whole hundred strides and the open ground above it, and nothing on the track could get at her.

"You count them in," he said. "Every one. Riders and anybody walking, and if a man turns round and goes back up you do not shout and you do not move, because he will be gone before you have finished shouting. You only have to know the number."

"And afterward?"

"Afterward the number is the only thing in the world that matters," Kael said. "Do not lose it."

She lay down under the tree and pulled the cloak over herself, properly, feet first, the way he had shown her.

He went down and took his place at the bottom of the hundred strides, at the mouth, where the rise came down to the water and the track went through between them at about the width of a cart.

Valeria went up onto the rise above the flat place with the holes in it, low, thirty feet over the track.

Then there was a great deal of nothing.

It was a bright cold day with a wind coming down the fold and the water loud in it. The birds went on doing what birds do until about a quarter of an hour before, and then stopped, and that was the first he knew.

He heard the horses before he saw anything.

Then Tarim came round the bend at the top, walking, on his own, forty strides in front of the first of them.

He came down to the split rock and stopped, got down on one knee, and started doing something to his boot.

The first two horses came past him at a walk.
