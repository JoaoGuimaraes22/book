# Chapter Fifty-One — The Ships

The governor of Qeshet looked at him.

He took his time over it and he did the whole of it: the hair, the mouth, the shoulders, the hands in the lap with the chain lying across them. Then the iron. Then back up to the face, where he stayed.

Sixty-two days, and this was the first of it.

"I do not know," he said.

Kael did not answer that.

Below them the yard was still going. Somebody down there was telling somebody else where to stand, twice, in Ivrit, and louder the second time. One of the five on the floor was breathing through his nose in a way that would want looking at before morning.

"You had them in your house," Kael said.

"I had them in my house."

"And then we were separated. Where are they?"

"I had them. And then I did not have them. I do not where they are."

---

He leaned forward in the chair.

The man's hands were behind him with a belt at the wrists and a second one above the elbows, and there was nothing of them to be had. So the neck. Both lamps were behind Kael, which put the light where he wanted it, on the side of the throat above the collar, and he had it inside four seconds.

It was high, and it was going to stay high. A man had hit this one twice in the body eleven minutes ago and put him down on the floor of his own room, and nothing that came out of his mouth for the rest of the night was going to bring that back down.

The way it is done is that you take forty counts of a man saying something true, so that the truth has a size, and then you hold everything after it against the size.

There was no forty in this room. There was not going to be one.

So it would have to be done with the words.

"How many men did you have on that road," Kael said.

"Fifty."

"How many of them ever came inside twenty feet of the box."

The governor did not answer straight away.

"Nine," he said. "With me."

"Yes."

The corner of the man's mouth moved. Kael knew this was truth.

---

"I need to know what happened. How many men did you put into that house where we slept?"

"Sixteen."

"How were they set?"

"Four to a door. There were four doors."

"Four came into my room," Valeria said.

It was the first thing she had said inside that building.

"Yes," the governor said.

"How many came out of that house in the end," Kael said.

"Six."

"Carrying three children."

"Carrying three children."

"So ten never left."

"Yes."

"And you did not wait for them."

"No."

---

Behind him somebody put his weight onto his other foot and left it there.

"You were in the yard," Kael said.

"I was."

"How long?"

"Until the box was loaded. Then I went."

"How long was that?"

"Longer than I had meant it to be." The governor's head went round toward the shutters and came back. "It was not going the way it had been described to me. I stood in my own yard and worked out that I was not going to get anything more out of that house, and that if I stayed I would start losing what I already had in the box. So I took what I had."

"What did it sound like?"

The man did not answer.

Kael waited, and went on waiting, and it went on not being answered. Everything else had come back in the same voice, level, in order, in the empire's tongue, out of a man who had run a city and had a number for anything you asked him. There was a shape to the way he talked, and it held all the way up to that question and then it was not there.

"I have heard people being taken," he said at last. "I have heard it more than once."

He stopped again.

"That was not what I heard."

---

Kael stopped for a breath. It seems that somehow the others resisted, or fled, or somehow fought back.

"My people. Who was still standing," Kael said. "At the end of it."

"I do not know. I was on the road."

"How far down the road."

"The road bends above the town," the governor said. "I could still hear it at the bend."

Kael had walked that town once, up from the water, in the middle of six spears, and he had every stride of it. He had the pace of a box behind four mules on a street at night, because he had lived inside that pace for two months.

Twenty-six minutes.

He did not say it out loud. Nothing in the room changed. The woman by the door moved her basket onto her other arm.

---

"What happened in Qeshet," Kael said.

"You will have to be more exact than that."

"You are an imperial governor. You told my mother that nothing would touch my brother in your house. Why would you turn on us?"

The governor did not say anything for a moment.

"I did say that. My reasons are much deeper than simple betrayal."

"My brother was a month old. I need more than some appeal to a deeper reasoning."

"Yes, I understand. It was true when I said nothing would touch him, and nothing did touch him." He worked his shoulders against the wall to get them off the bone. "There's much more to this."

"Then say it in order."

---

"A man came to me before your island was invaded," the governor said. "In the winter. Not soldiers. One man, twice, with nothing in his hands either time."

"What did he want?"

"The coast."

Kael waited.

"Not the harbour. Nothing in the harbour, nothing on the mole, nobody's cargo. He wanted my watch standing on the wall on certain nights looking at the water, and he wanted nothing about those nights in any letter going north."

"And you gave it to him?"

"I gave it to him."

"How many nights."

"Three."

"How many ships."

"Nobody told me that," the governor said. "I counted them. Thirty-one."

Kael sat still.

Somebody had counted them. Not a herald, not a report, not a soldier telling another soldier. A man on his own wall in the dark on three nights, going along a line of hulls with his mouth shut, the way Kael would have done it.

---

"You keep that city for the empire," Kael said.

"I have kept that city for the empire for seventeen years, and my father kept it before me. The name I carry was given to this family when the empire took the city. I have worn it every day of my life."

"Then why?"

"Because they were going to have Qeshet." The voice did not move at all. "That was not a thing I could arrange or prevent. What I could arrange was how it was done, and when, and to whom."

"Who's "they"?"

"They, is what that man represented. A resistance."

"A resistance? To the empire. So doomed an island full of people."

"Yes."

"There were thousands of children on that island."

"I did not ask what was on it. I did not ask what they would do," the governor said.

---

"What did you give us to make us fall asleep?" Kael said.

"I was sent a thing and told what it would do and how much of it to use. I did not ask what it was and I was not told." He looked at the floor between them. "It did not do all of it. That is why the ride to Ishareth came sooner than expected."

"Who sent it?"

"I have no name for him, or a face to give."

"That will not suffice."

"No," the governor said. "It will not."

---

He shifted against the wall again, and stopped.

"There is a thing you have not asked me," he said.

Kael did not answer.

"Qeshet has already been taken. By the same men who took your island."

The yard went on below the shutters. Somebody down there had begun putting a barrow away.

"When?"

"I do not know the day. It was set for after the island, and every other thing they set has been done on the day they set it." He said it flatly, in order, and then he stopped in the middle of it, which he had not done anywhere else. "It is done. It was done while I was on that road. I know this time they were more forgiving."

There was a lot of information to process here. The room was eerily silent besides the two people speaking.
Every question answered spawned a hundred more.

"Ask your bandit friends. They know as well as I do, that this continent was preparing for a great war, greater than any of the past, for the past years. Your island was the first spark. Qeshet was the second. The whole continent will burn."

Kael looked in the leader's eyes. He did not disagree or disavow the governor.

"Then what will happen—."

"I will not answer more today. Either finish me, or leave. My men outside should be getting suspicious of something by now," the governor interrupted.

---

Nobody answered him.

Then the man from the rope-walk stood up off the stool. He had sat behind Kael's left shoulder from the moment the belts went on and had put no part of himself into any of it.

"He is right," he said.

"About the war?"

"About the time."

He went to the shutters, put one finger on the slat and moved it down the width of an eye. He looked, and let it come back.

"A chest went out of that gate at a walk in front of fifty men who were paid to stand there and look at things. There is one of them down there doing a sum about it right now and he is not going to like where it comes out. When he does not like it he will come up this stair to be told he is wrong."

"I need the rest of tonight with him," Kael said.

"No."

"You have not asked me why."

"Say why."

"Because he is the only man alive who was standing in that yard. Because tomorrow he will be somewhere I cannot get to, and he knows that. By the middle of the day he will have worked out what he should not have said tonight, and he will never say it again." Kael did not take his eyes off the man on the floor while he said it. "There is not going to be a second one of these."

"That is all true," the man from the rope-walk said. "No."

---

"There are fourteen people of mine in this city," he said, "and not one of them came into it for you. I have spent an hour of them on your questions because you paid for it and because I wanted to hear the answers, and that is the whole of what you bought. It is finished now."

"And him?"

"He stays where he is."

Somebody by the wall said something short in a language Kael did not have.

"No," the man from the rope-walk said, without turning round. "We do not leave bodies. A body is a hunt, and a hunt has a name in it by the third week." He came away from the shutters. "That one has to get up tomorrow morning and stand in front of sixty men he is behind on paying and tell them where their money went. There is nothing he can say about tonight that does not cost him more than it costs us. He is worth more to me sitting against that wall than he is in the river, and he knows that as well as I do, which is why he offered."

The governor of Qeshet did not say anything to that. He had asked for one of two things and had been given the other one, and his face went on doing what it had been doing all night, which was nothing at all.

---

"Say it once at the gate," the man from the rope-walk said to the talker. "He has paid us for these two and he is paying us for the third, and these two are how we bring her in. Say it to the man with the board, and say it to nobody else, and do not say it twice."

"And if the board wants him to say it himself?"

"Then the board can come up here and ask him."

They put the five where a man coming in would not fall over them. One of them went round again with two fingers under each jaw and did not hurry it. The talker took the strip of shirt up off the boards and shook the dust out of it.

The governor watched him do it.

"Is she alive?" he said.

Kael was at the door with his hands in front of him and the chain hanging between them.

"Yes."

The strip went back into the man's mouth and was tied behind his head. After that there was nothing in that room but five men breathing on a floor and one man sitting up against a wall with his eyes open.

---

Down the stair with the turn in it, and out through the yard door.

Their steel was still lying on the ground inside the gate where it had gone. The two on the gate had not moved it and had not touched it, and the six of them picked it up in front of them, one at a time, unhurried, the way men do a thing they have every right to do.

Kael stood with his wrists together and watched the talker say eleven words to the man with the board.

The man with the board looked at the two children for about a second and a half. Then he made a mark on his strip of paper, because a thing that had come in had now gone out again, and that was what the paper was for.

The gate opened.

Forty-nine in the yard. He had the number before they were halfway across it.

Every man in it watched two children go out through that gate, and every man in it had been told why. It held.
