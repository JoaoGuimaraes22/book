# Chapter Fifty-One — The Ships

The governor of Qeshet looked at him.

He took his time over it and he did the whole of it: the hair, the mouth, the shoulders, the hands in the lap with the chain lying across them. Then the iron. Then back up to the face, where he stayed.

Sixty-two days, and this was the first of it.

"I do not know," he said.

Kael did not answer that.

Below them the yard was still going. Somebody down there was telling somebody else where to stand, twice, in Ivrit, and louder the second time. One of the five on the floor was breathing through his nose in a way that would want looking at before morning.

"You had them in your house," Kael said.

"I had them in my house."

"And then you did not have them."

"And then I did not have them."

---

He leaned forward in the chair.

The man's hands were behind him with a belt at the wrists and a second one above the elbows, and there was nothing of them to be had. So the neck. Both lamps were behind Kael, which put the light where he wanted it, on the side of the throat above the collar, and he had it inside four seconds.

It was high, and it was going to stay high. A man had hit this one twice in the body eleven minutes ago and put him down on the floor of his own room, and nothing that came out of his mouth for the rest of the night was going to bring that back down.

The way it is done is that you take forty counts of a man saying something true, so that the truth has a size, and then you hold everything after it against the size.

There was no forty in this room. There was not going to be one.

So it would have to be done with the words.

"How many men did you have on that road," Kael said.

"Fifty."

"How many of them ever came inside twenty feet of the box."

The governor did not answer straight away.

"Nine," he said. "With me."

"Yes."

The corner of the man's mouth moved.

"You have asked me two things you already had," he said. "Ask me one you do not."

---

"How many men did you put into that house."

"Sixteen."

"How were they set."

"Four to a door. There were four doors."

"Four came into my room," Valeria said.

It was the first thing she had said inside that building.

"Yes," the governor said.

"How many came out of it," Kael said.

"Six."

"Carrying three children."

"Carrying three children."

"So ten stayed inside."

"Yes."

"And you did not wait for them."

"No."

---

Behind him somebody put his weight onto his other foot and left it there.

"You were in the yard," Kael said.

"I was in the yard."

"How long."

"Until the box was loaded. Then I went."

"How long was that."

"Longer than I had meant it to be." The governor's head went round toward the shutters and came back. "It was not going the way it had been described to me. I stood in my own yard and worked out that I was not going to get anything more out of that house, and that if I stayed I would start losing what I already had in the box. So I took what I had."

"What did it sound like."

The man did not answer.

Kael waited, and went on waiting, and it went on not being answered. Everything else had come back in the same voice, level, in order, in the empire's tongue, out of a man who had run a city and had a number for anything you asked him. There was a shape to the way he talked, and it held all the way up to that question and then it was not there.

"I have heard a house taken," he said at last. "I have heard it more than once."

He stopped again.

"That was not what I heard."

---

"Who was still standing," Kael said. "At the end of it."

"I do not know. I was on the road."

"How far down the road."

"The road bends above the town," the governor said. "I could still hear it at the bend."

Kael had walked that town once, up from the water, in the middle of six spears, and he had every stride of it. He had the pace of a box behind four mules on a street at night, because he had lived inside that pace for two months.

Twenty-six minutes.

He did not say it out loud. Nothing in the room changed. The woman by the door moved her basket onto her other arm.
