# Chapter Forty-Three — The Water

They heard the dogs a long time before there was anything to see.

The water had been taking them down for hours by then, north off the back of the Kharoun and into the fold, and the fold had been getting wider and less steep the whole way, and the trees had gone from needle to something else and then to nothing much at all. The cold came up out of the ground here instead of down off the sky. There was grass in it. There had not been grass in two months.

Then, a long way below, one dog, and then two answering it, and then a third that was not answering anything and only wanted to hear itself.

Kael stopped and put his shoulder against a rock and stayed there.

"Dogs," Valeria said.

"Yes."

"How far."

He listened to it properly. Sound came up a fold like this and lied about the distance, and it lied in a direction he knew.

"Mile. Little more."

Valeria set the princess down on her feet and the princess stood up on them, which she had been doing since the middle of the afternoon and which Kael had not stopped counting.

"Then we should talk about it now," he said, "and not there."

---

They sat down where they were, in the grass at the side of the water, in the dark.

Kael had been building it all day, in between the strides, in the part of his head that still belonged to him. He laid it out.

"There are forty houses. There is a headman and there is a smith. We want the smith and we want a healer and we want food, and we want all three of those from people who have never seen us before, in a country where I have been for a day."

"Take the smith," Valeria said.

He had known she would say it. It was the correct thing to say.

"Go on."

"At night, in his own house, alone. He does the work or he does not, and either way we are three miles from the village before it is light and nobody knows who we were." She said it without any weight on it at all. "It is the smallest number of people. Everything else is more people."

"And the healer."

"You do not need a healer. You need the arm cut off before it kills you, and I can do that, and I would rather do it here than in a village."

"No," Kael said.

"I know," she said. "I am telling you it is on the list."

The princess had been sitting with her hands in her lap and her chin up, which was how she sat, and now she said, in the third of her voice that was all she had, "We could say we are a merchant's children."

"Say more."

"We were on the road with our father's train and it was taken. That happens. It happens constantly; my chamberlain used to complain about it in writing." She was looking at the water. "We were held and we got away."

Kael turned it over. It was not bad. It explained three children and it explained the state of them and it explained why they had nothing.

"What is your father's name."

"I—" she said, and stopped.

"What does he trade. Where does he keep his warehouse. What is the last town you came through before the road, and what does that town smell like, and who did we lose when they took us, and what did she look like." He was not being unkind about it; he did not have the strength to be anything about it. "A woman in that village will ask you what your mother is called and she will ask it kindly and it will take her about four seconds."

"I do not know what a merchant's daughter is called," the princess said.

"No."

"I have never met one." She said it quite evenly. "I have met their fathers."

"Then we do not use it."

She was silent for a moment. Then she said, "I see," and stopped talking, and did not sulk about it, and Kael filed that.

"There is a bigger problem than the story," he said.

He got his right hand up out of his lap and turned it over in front of them so that the band caught what light there was, and the eleven links came off it and swung, and he held it there.

"Every one of them dies on this. Whatever we say, they look at our wrists, and iron on a child means one thing everywhere, and everybody who sees it will already know the answer before I open my mouth." He put the hand back down. "There is no story that survives a wrist. So we do not have a story. We have what we are."

"That is not a plan," Valeria said.

"No," Kael said. "It is what is left when you take the plans away."

---

The plan he did have, and kept, was his father's.

They would come down to within four hundred strides of the near end of the village and get in above it where the water came out of the fold, and lie there through the dark and the whole of the next day, and he would learn it. Which house woke first. Where the men went in the morning and when they came back. Where the dogs slept. Which door the smith's smoke came out of. Who left the village and who came into it and how often and from which direction. That was a day's work and it was cheap and it was the only kind of work he had ever been any good at, and at the end of it he would know where to knock.

He got up to go and find the place and his legs went.

He did not fall down so much as arrive on the ground, sideways, with the grass very close to his face and the noise of the water suddenly much louder than it had been, and he lay there and worked out in what order the parts of him were going to come back.

Valeria's hand was on the back of his coat.

"Get up," she said.

"Yes."

He did not.

She waited. Then she went round in front of him and got down on her heels so that her face was where he could see it without moving his head, and she looked at him for a while.

"You will be dead by the light," she said.

"I have a day. I said a few days."

"You said that yesterday. Look at your arm."

He did not look at his arm. He had been not looking at his arm since the middle of the morning, when the line had come up past the crook of the elbow and stopped being a line and started being a country.

"If we go in blind," he said, "we go in on somebody we have not chosen."

"Yes."

"That is the whole of my father's school. Choose the ground. Do not let the ground choose you."

"I know," Valeria said. "I was in the yard for it."

She stood up.

"Your father is not here," she said, "and I am not going to carry two of you."

---

The dog came out at them at eighty strides and did its whole job and then some.

It was a brown thing with no interest in biting anybody, and it stood in the middle of the track with its feet apart and told the entire fold about them for as long as it took a door to open somewhere below, and then it kept going anyway on general principle.

The house was the last one at the top end, close in against the water, with a low wall round a yard and a shed at the side of the yard whose roof was too big for a shed. Kael got that much. There was a fire's light going somewhere behind the shed, low and banked, the colour a fire goes when nobody is feeding it, and he had that too, and it was the last piece of work he did.

The door opened and a woman came out into the yard with a lamp.

She held it up. She was perhaps forty. She was in the pale cloth of this country, wound and hung, with her head covered, and she had come out with the lamp in one hand and a piece of firewood in the other in a manner that suggested she had done it before.

Kael had four sentences ready. He had built them on the walk down and he had taken them apart twice and rebuilt them, and they were the best four sentences he had, and they were nothing at all, and he knew it, and he was going to use them anyway because a man says the thing he prepared.

The lamp came up over the wall and found them.

It found a girl in a dead man's coat with her hands in iron in front of her and a scarf that was not hers over her hair. It found another girl standing with her feet apart and a soldier's sword on her hip and iron on her wrists and nothing in her face. And it found a boy holding his left arm across his chest, grey to the lips, with three men's blood dried black down the front of him.

The woman lowered the piece of firewood.

She said something to the house behind her without turning round, one word, and the lamp came down, and she came over the wall's gap and put her hand flat on Kael's forehead the way you check a pot.

Then she took hold of his good arm and pulled it over her shoulders.

"Get in the house," she said.

She did not ask him anything.

He never used any of the four sentences. He was still holding them when the door shut behind them, and after that there was nowhere to put them, and eventually he stopped carrying them about.

---

He woke in a bed.

The first thing he did was find the iron with his right thumb, and it was there. The second was to work out where the other two were without moving his head, and Valeria was against the wall on the floor by the door, sitting up with her knees drawn and her eyes shut, which was not sleeping, and the princess was on a pallet under the window with the scarf still on her.

Only then did he let himself have the rest of it: a low room, whitewashed, warm; a shuttered window with grey in the cracks of it; a smell of ash and old milk and something like thyme; and a ceiling, which he lay and looked at, because it had been a very long time since there had been one.

His arm had been opened up and something had been done to it while he was not there.

He got it up in front of his face. It was bound from the knuckles to above the elbow in strips of clean linen, and the thumb was splinted between two flat pieces of wood, and the whole thing had been packed with something that had a smell, and it hurt in an entirely different manner than it had hurt yesterday. Yesterday it had been a pressure. Today it was a wound, which is a much better thing for an arm to be.

The last time he had woken in a bed under a stranger's roof he had woken in a box a day east with his mother gone.

He lay still until that had gone past him, and then he counted the boards in the ceiling, and there were nineteen.

---

The old woman came back at the middle of the morning and undid all of it and did it again.

She was small and dark and bent forward from the shoulders, and she worked with her mouth slightly open, and she did not talk to him while she was working, which he liked. She had a boy with her who carried the water and was sent out of the room before she started. She cut the packing away, and put the hand in a bowl of water so hot it made him lose the end of a breath, and then she got at the back of it with a blade and a folded cloth and took off what had died in the night and would not be part of him again.

Kael watched all of it.

He knew most of what she was doing. He knew the reason for the heat, which was to make the flesh give up what it was holding, and he knew what the honey was for, because his mother had put honey on him twice and had told him both times why, and he knew why she had opened the web between the thumb and the hand rather than the middle of the swelling, and he watched her make that cut and thought, correctly, that she had made it in the right place.

There was one thing he did not know.

She had a small clay pot, and out of the pot she took what looked like a wad of grey wool and was not wool, and she laid it along the wound before the linen went on.

"What is that," he said, in Ivrit.

She stopped and looked up at him.

Her face did the thing that everybody's face in that village did for the first three days, which was to be surprised at the shape of his mouth. He spoke their tongue with the coast in it, hard and forward, out of a place four hundred miles away that most of them had never seen, and to them it was a boy from the sea talking like a trader off the wharf, and every single one of them heard it and could not help remarking on it.

"You speak it," she said.

"Yes. What is that."

"Web off a rafter." She went back to work. "It shuts a small bleeding. It is not for you; yours is not bleeding. It holds the honey where I put it."

"How long has it been for you."

"What?"

"How long have you been doing this."

She thought about it while she wound the linen.

"Forty years, near it," she said. "There is nobody else."

He got a good look at her hands then, going round and round and round his arm, quick and flat and unhurried, and they were the third pair of hands he had ever watched do this, and he could price them now, and she was good.

"Ten days," she said, when she had tied it off. "You will keep it up, above your heart, and you will not use it, and you will eat, and you will not go anywhere for ten days."

"We have not got ten days."

"Then you will die on the road instead of in a bed," she said, without any particular emphasis, and packed her things and went away.

---

The house belonged to a man called Damir, who was the smith, and to his wife Miresh, who was the woman with the lamp.

They had a son called Tarim, who was grown, twenty perhaps, with his father's shoulders coming on him and his mother's face, and who had given up his own bed and slept in the shed with the mules and made nothing of it.

And they had a daughter called Neshah, who was four.

There were six of them in that house now and it was a house for five, and the food came out of one pot twice a day and the pot did not get any bigger.

Kael did the arithmetic on the second morning because he could not help it.

Bread, and a grey stew with barley in it and a little goat, and a hard sour cheese cut thin. He counted what came out of the pot and he counted what went into the six bowls, and he counted the sacks against the back wall of the room where the light got in, and he had the size of the family's whole winter inside about a minute and a half. He knew what three extra mouths took out of that. He could have said it in days.

Nobody at that table looked at what anybody else was given.

That was the thing he could not get round. He had spent his life pricing what people did, and he had never once had to price a gift. The sum came out. That was the trouble: it came out exactly, and he had it to the day, and there was nothing at the other end of it to put against the number.

Miresh put a second piece of bread in front of him on the second night.

"I have had enough," he said.

"You have not," she said, and went on with what she was doing, and after a while he ate it.

---

On the third day Valeria asked him a question.

They were in the yard. He was sitting on the wall in the sun with the arm up on his knee because the old woman had told him to and because it did in fact help, and Valeria was standing near the gap in the wall where she could see the track, which was where she had stood for some part of every hour since they arrived. Her ribs had been strapped by the old woman on the first evening. She had let a stranger put hands on her, which Kael had watched and had not remarked on, and she had said thank you afterward in the empire's tongue to a woman who did not have it.

The princess was inside. She had been inside for three days. It had taken the village half an hour to decide that, and nobody had asked her.

"Kael," Valeria said.

"Yes."

"Why are they doing this."

He looked up.

She was not looking at him. She was watching the track, and her face was doing what it did, which was nothing.

"Doing what," he said, to make her say it.

"They have been told what we are. Nobody has said it out loud in front of us but the old woman looked at my wrists and did not ask, and the headman came and looked at the three of us and went away again, and that man's son sleeps with the mules." She turned her head. "They know that we are being hunted. They know that whoever is hunting us is the empire, because who else puts iron on people. And they are feeding us out of a pot that does not have enough in it."

"Yes."

"Why."

Kael opened his mouth and found that he did not have anything in it.

He had a great many answers and every one of them was a mechanism. Because they hate the empire. Because they want something. Because the headman has calculated that we are worth more alive to somebody. Because they are frightened of what we would do if they refused. He turned each one over and each one was wrong, and he could not have said why.

"I don't know," he said.

"You always know."

"I know why people do the other thing," Kael said. "I have never once had to work out this one."

Valeria took that. She looked back at the track.

"On the mountain," she said, "my orders were that a house stands for its own. Everyone in that district understood it and nobody had to be told. It is not a cruelty, it is how a thing holds together. You stand for your own, and the more of your own you have, the more you can stand for."

"I know."

"These people have nothing," she said, "and we are not theirs."

She did not say anything after that, and Kael did not either, and neither of them got any further with it, and the sun came round the corner of the shed and onto the wall, and they sat in it.

---

The smith was a slow man with burnt forearms and one eyebrow mostly gone, and he did not come to the house much during the day, and when he did he ate and looked at his hands.

On the fourth evening he came and sat down on the wall next to Kael and asked to see the iron.

Kael put his right arm out and Damir took it in both of his and turned it over under the last of the light, close to his face, and went along the band with his thumb, and along the pin, and stopped at the pin and stayed there a long time.

"It is peened," Kael said.

"Yes."

"Both sides."

"Yes." Damir turned the wrist again. "It is very good iron. It is better than anything I have ever made and it is better than anything I will ever have to make. Whoever drew this out knew what he was doing." He put his thumbnail into one of the grooves. "And that is not rust and it is not paint that I know."

"What is it?"

"I have no idea," Damir said. He let go of the arm. "I have shod mules for twenty-two years. That is the whole of what I know about iron."

"Can you take it off."

"Yes."

Kael waited.

"I will cut the peen off the pin and drift the pin out and the band opens," Damir said. "It is not difficult work. It is slow work, because the pin is set into good iron and because there is an arm inside it, so I cannot swing. I have to tap it, and set it, and tap it. Each one is an hour, near enough." He looked out at the fold. "Five of them."

"Five hours."

"More. You cannot do five in a night. The hands go."

"Two nights."

"Two nights," Damir agreed.

Kael had been listening to the shape of the fold since the first morning, to how the water noise sat in it and how the dogs at the far end came through, and he already knew the answer to the next thing, and asked it anyway.

"Everybody will hear it."

"Everybody in Doresh will hear it," Damir said, "and everybody in Doresh will know exactly what it is, because a man does not cut iron at night for any reason there is a good reason for." He said it without heat. "There are forty houses here. Not one of them will come out."

"You are sure of that."

Damir looked at him.

"No," he said, and got up.

---

Later that evening Kael asked him about the road.

He had been waiting to. He had spent four days getting the near ground off Tarim and off the boy who carried the healer's water and off anyone who would answer: which tracks went where, how far the next water was, what was between here and the flat. He had a map now that was better than the one he had come down with and it stopped about nine miles out in every direction, which was the size of a village's world.

Damir's world was bigger. Damir's world was the road.

"East," Kael said. "Along the top. Where does it go."

"Days. Six, seven for a train that is loaded." Damir was scraping the bowl. "It stays on the south skirt the whole way because the north side is what you came down and nothing goes on the north side. Then the Kharoun stops and the ground goes down a long way, and there is a river, and at the river there is Isharéth."

"Isharéth."

"You would know it if you had been anywhere," Damir said, not unkindly. "It is the last of it. It is a city on the river and the empire holds it and past the river it does not, and everything that is going to cross goes across there, and everything that comes back comes back through there." He set the bowl down. "Half of what goes up this road is going to Isharéth. I have put shoes on it for twenty-two years."

Kael said the name over twice inside his head.

Isharéth. Six or seven days east, on a river, and the last of it.

That was where the box had been going. He had two months of east in him and no reason for any of it, and now there was a place at the end of the road with a name on it, and the number attached to the name was six or seven days.

He did not know one useful thing about it and he had it, and having it was not nothing.

---

The princess was very good with the child.

That surprised him for about a day and then stopped surprising him, because he had watched her ask to hold his brother on a boat and get the head wrong and correct it herself, and he had been filing it under the wrong thing for a month.

Neshah had decided about her on the first afternoon and there had been no appeal.

They had no language between them at all. Neshah had thirty words and all of them were Ivrit; the princess had the empire's tongue and a great deal of Vessantine formal address and nothing whatever else, and it did not appear to inconvenience either of them. Neshah brought her things and put them in her hands and took them away again and brought them back. The princess held out whatever she was given and made a small serious noise about it. Neshah climbed on her.

On the fifth day Kael came in from the yard and found the two of them on the floor by the fire with a bowl of dried beans between them, sorting them into two piles by a rule that Neshah had invented and was enforcing strictly, and the princess of the whole empire had her sleeves pushed back over the iron on her wrists and was being corrected, at some length, in a language she did not have.

Miresh was at the fire with her back to them and did not turn round.

Kael stood in the door for a second and then went and sat down where he had been sitting.

---

Tarim came down the track at a run on the sixth morning.

He came into the yard and said one word to his mother and Miresh put down what she was holding and was moving before he had finished it, and Kael was up off the wall with the count already going.

"How many?" he said.

Tarim looked at him and did not answer, because he was twenty and had run a mile and had never had to do this.

"How many," Kael said again, in Ivrit, flat, the way you take a report.

"Ten. Twelve." Tarim got his breath. "Horses. On the water road, coming down. They are at the bend."

"How long is the bend from here."

"A quarter of an hour."

"Then we have a quarter of an hour," Kael said, "and you have not seen anybody, and you have not run anywhere this morning, and you should be at the forge."

---

They put them under the floor of the storeroom.

It was not a cellar. It was the space where the boards sat up off the rock on stone piers, at the back of the house where the ground fell away, deep enough at that end for a person to lie in and not deep enough to sit. Damir had the boards up in about ninety seconds, which meant that they came up often, which was a thing Kael noticed and had no time to do anything with.

Valeria went in first and turned herself round in the dark to face the opening.

The princess went next. Miresh had her by both shoulders on the way down and said something to her, and the princess did not have a word of it and went in anyway and got flat.

Kael stood at the edge of the hole.

His body would not do it.

He had gone through the whole of it in his head on the walk across the room: the size of the space, the boards, the dark, the fact of somebody else putting a cover over him. His head had said yes to all of it, and then he was standing at the edge and none of the rest of him was going anywhere at all. He was aware of the exact temperature of his own hands.

He got down and went in.

It took about four seconds and it was the worst four seconds of the six days, and nobody saw it.

The boards went down. Damir put something on top of them that scraped, and then there were feet, and then there was a very long time.

---

The horses came into the yard at a walk.

Kael lay on his side with his cheek in the dirt and his bound arm along the length of him, and turned his good ear up, and listened.

Ten horses at least. Boots, and a lot of them, so they had come off the horses in the yard, which meant they were not in a hurry and had done this before, in other yards, that morning or the day before.

A voice. Not the coast in it and not the Kharoun in it. Ivrit off the flat somewhere, and the flat accent of a man who has been given a list.

Kael turned his head very slightly toward Valeria and found her already turned toward him.

"Talking," he breathed. "Wait."

The voice was asking Damir about his mules. It asked about his mules for a surprisingly long time, and then asked how many people were in the house, and then asked whether anybody had come down off the north side in the last week, and Damir said no in the voice of a man who is being interrupted at work, and the voice said that of course he had not, and that nobody had, and that he would like to say what it was that nobody had seen.

And then he said it.

Three of them. A boy of thirteen or fourteen, dark, an islander, who had a hand and an arm on him that would be in a bad way by now. A girl the same age, black hair, who was to be considered as dangerous as any man in this yard and was not to be approached by fewer than four, and there was a small sound in the yard when he said that.

And a girl of fourteen or fifteen.

"And you will not need me to describe her," the voice said. "You will know her when you see her, and you will know her from the far end of this village, because there is nobody in the world with hair that colour. Not fair. Not white. Silver, like a thing that has been made."

Then he named a price.

Kael did the conversion in his head out of a stupid reflex and stopped halfway through, because it did not need converting. It was not an amount of money. It was a village. It was every roof in Doresh and the ground under them and enough left to never do this again.

Then he named the other thing.

He did it in the same voice he had used about the mules, which was how Kael knew that he meant it and had not enjoyed saying it. Any house. Any house that had them, or had had them, or had fed them and let them go. He did not describe what would happen to it. He said the words *any house* and then he stopped talking, and the stopping was the whole of it.

Kael lay under the floor of that storeroom and thought, with total clarity: *they will take it.*

He was not angry about it. There was nothing in it to be angry about. He had priced the pot on the second morning and he had priced the sacks against the wall and he knew what this family had, and he had just heard a man offer them forty years of not being frightened, and he had heard the other side of it as well, and there was no arithmetic in which the answer came out any other way. He had done that sum on a bound man in a wood six days ago from the other side of it, and had got the answer he wanted, and had known exactly what he was doing while he did it.

He turned his head to the boards and waited for the light to come down.

Damir said, "There has been nobody."

---

They were three quarters of an hour in the village.

Kael counted it.

They went house by house, which he knew because he could hear the boots on the track going away from him and coming back and going further, and because the dogs at each end of Doresh took it in turns, and because a village of forty houses laid out along one water takes about that long to knock on properly if you are being thorough and are not searching, only asking.

Forty houses.

He lay in the dark and counted the doors, and he did it the way he did everything, which was completely. He got thirty-eight, and he was three short at one end because of the water noise, and he corrected for it and let it stand at forty.

Forty doors. Forty people who had been told what was in this fold, and what it was worth, and what would happen to their house.

And not one of them opened their mouth.

He had the number and there was nothing to do with it. He lay in the dark and held it.

The boots came back up the track. The horses went out of the yard at a walk, and down, and the water took the sound of them, and then there was nothing but the water.

Nobody came to lift the boards for a long time.

When they came up at last it was Miresh. She was white to the mouth. "Out. Out. Come out of there," she said, in a voice that was nothing like the one she used, and she got hold of the princess and pulled her up out of the hole and held on to her for about four seconds with her chin on the top of the scarf, and then let go and turned away and was extremely busy at the fire.

---

In the yard everybody was talking at once and nobody was saying anything.

It was the noise a place makes when it has stopped being frightened and does not have anywhere to put what it was frightened with. Three men had come up from the houses below. The headman was there, an old man with a stick and a very straight back, and he was saying the same sentence to Damir for the third time. Somebody's wife was laughing.

It went round them and got taken up and came back, high and too fast, the way it does, and there was another one under it, and then two more.

Kael stood against the wall of the shed with his arm across his chest and his back flat against the stone, and made himself stay there, and did not look at anybody's face.

It took a little under a minute.

Nobody noticed. There was nothing to notice. He had been standing in the yard and he went on standing in the yard.

Neshah came out of the house at a run because everyone was in the yard and being loud, which for a person of four is an occasion, and she went straight through the middle of the men and round the water trough and got to the princess and reached up.

The princess picked her up.

She did it one-handed and badly because of the iron, and got her onto her hip. Neshah put both hands on the scarf.

She had been pulling at it for six days. She pulled at it because it was on the head of the only person in that house who let her do whatever she wanted, and because it was blue, and because it moved.

The princess laughed and took hold of the front of it with her bound hands to keep it, and Neshah got the back of it, and it came off.

It came off all at once, and the whole of it came down after it, two months of it, unwashed and grey with dust, and the sun came round the corner of the shed and got into it, and the dust made no difference whatsoever.

It was about four seconds.

The princess had it back over her head before Kael had finished turning round. She did it fast and without any fuss and with the child still on her hip, and she said something quiet to Neshah in the empire's tongue, and Neshah laughed at her.

Kael's first thought was the right one, and he had it before he had finished moving.

*Who is standing where they could see that.*

He got the yard in one pass, which took him rather longer than it would have done a week ago. Damir, facing away, arguing. The headman, facing away. The three men from below, all facing the headman. Miresh in the doorway with her back to it. Valeria at the gap in the wall, looking down the track, because that was where Valeria looked.

And Tarim, at the door of the forge shed, twenty feet off, with a bucket in his hand.

Kael looked at him for perhaps a second and a half.

He knew Tarim now. Tarim had given him his bed and had slept six nights in the shed with the mules about it, and had carried the old woman's water up the track twice a day, and had run a mile that morning to tell them, and had done it without being asked by anybody, and had stood in the yard and been afraid while the horses were in it and had not gone anywhere.

Then the bucket went down and Tarim went into the shed after it.

Kael put it down where he put things, and went to see about the boards.

---

They did it the next night, and the night after.

Damir did the princess first, because Kael said so and Valeria did not argue. He put a stake into the block and laid the band over the horn of it so that the iron was resting on iron and there was nothing under the blow but her wrist held in his own left hand, and he set the chisel on the peened head of the pin, and he tapped it, and set it, and tapped it.

The forge was open at the front and the sound went out of it and up.

It went up the fold and hit the far side and came back, and it went down the water through the whole length of the village, one blow at a time, unmistakable, iron on iron on iron, hour after hour, in the middle of the night, in a village where nobody works at night.

Forty houses lay in the dark and listened to it.

Kael sat in the corner of the forge with his back against the wall where he could see the door and the track, and did not sleep, and listened past the hammer for the other thing, which would be feet, or a voice, or a light coming along the track. There was a great deal of time in which to listen for it. Between the blows the fold was completely quiet.

Nobody came.

He had been ready to be wrong about it. He had been ready the whole first hour, and then the second, in a way he was later not proud of, and by the third hour he had stopped being ready and had only been sitting there.

The pin went out of the princess's left band at somewhere near the fourth hour and Damir opened the band with a bar and took it off her arm.

She put her hand round her own wrist and held it.

Then Damir did the other one, which took less time because he had the measure of it now, and when it came off she sat with both hands in her lap and looked at them, and said, in the empire's tongue, to nobody, "Oh."

Valeria went the second night, and she was quicker than either girl had any right to be, because she put her arm on the horn and left it there and did not move it once in an hour, not by a hair, and Damir said afterward that he had never had anything so easy to work on in his life and had also never wanted so much to stop.

And when her second band came away Valeria stood up and walked out of the forge into the yard and did nothing at all for a while.

Kael came out after her.

She was standing in the middle of the yard with her hands down at her sides and her eyes shut and her head slightly back, and she was completely still. She was on the packed dirt where the mules turned, and there was rock two feet under it, and there was a whole fold of stone under that going down to the water and up the other side, and all of it was carrying something, and she had not been able to hear any of it for seventy days.

He did not say anything to her.

After a minute she opened her eyes.

"There is somebody's cattle," she said. "A long way down. Nine or ten of them."

"How far."

"I do not know. Far. It is not — " She stopped and started again. "It is loud. I had forgotten that it is loud."

---

His own went last, on the second night, and it was the shortest of the three, because there was only the one band left on his right wrist and because Damir was better at it than he had been.

Tarim was not in the forge that night.

He had been there the whole of the first one, on the bellows and then not needed and then sitting against the door with his arms round his knees watching his father work, and tonight he was not, and Kael noticed, and thought that a man who has been up two nights running sends his son to bed.

He held his arm on the horn and watched the chisel and did not look away, and counted the blows.

At a hundred and eleven the peen came off. Damir put the drift on the pin and hit it four times and the pin went out onto the block, and he opened the band with the bar, and the iron came off Kael's arm and he put it down on the block with the other five.

Six bands, one of them still shut on nothing, and the chain, black in the grooves, on a smith's block in a village on the north side of the Kharoun.

"That is good iron," Damir said. "I would keep it, if you have no use for it."

"It is yours," Kael said. "It is all we have."

"It is not a payment."

"No," Kael said. "But it is the only thing on this earth I have got, so take it."

Damir looked at the pile of it for a while, and then swept it off the block into a leather bucket with the side of his hand, all at once, the way you clear scale.

"It is evidence," he said. "That is what it is. I will put it under the floor of my forge and there it will stay."

Kael sat on the anvil block and rubbed his right wrist with his right hand, because his left could not do it, and the skin under where the band had been was soft and wet and paler than the rest of him, and it did not feel like his arm.

Then he put two fingers flat on the anvil.

He did not make anything of it. He did it the way he had done it at nine on a flat rock and at eleven at a bench and about eight thousand times since, which was small, and exact, and asking a very small question.

He sent a thread down into the iron.

It went.

It went the whole length of the anvil and into the block under it and stopped where the block stopped, and came back to him and told him the shape of the thing it had gone through, cold and near and entirely ordinary, and it cost about what a hard breath costs, and it was the first answer he had been given by anything in seventy days.

He sat in a smith's forge in Doresh with two fingers on an anvil and let it run for longer than he needed to, and did not think about anything at all.

The hammering had stopped. It had been the only sound in that fold for two nights and now it was gone, and the water came back up underneath where it had been, and the whole of the village was quiet, and Kael sat in the middle of it and did not count one single thing.

It was the last thing he thought that night, and he thought it comfortably, as a man thinks about weather that has been on his side.

Under a noise like that, nothing in this fold could have heard anything at all.
