# Chapter Forty-Nine — The Paper

The man with his boots off looked up at him, and then looked down, and put his boots on.

He did it the way a man does it at the end of a rest, one at a time, working the heel down with his thumb, and it took as long as that takes, and nothing else happened in that street while it was going on.

The other one had not moved at all.

He had not looked at Valeria. He had not looked up the street to see who else was standing in it. He had not looked at the boy who had just said *bridge-house* out loud in the empire's tongue in front of a house with two men on the door of it day and night.

And he had not done the thing a face does when it is handed something it was not expecting. Very often that thing is nothing. But nothing takes a moment, and the moment can be watched arriving, and Kael knew to the half-second how long it is.

Neither of them had taken it.

He put that down where he put things.

Somebody in that house had decided in advance what was to be done if a stranger came up this street and used that word, and had said it out loud to the men who sat on the door, and the men who sat on the door had been sitting on it long enough to be bored of it.

He had been in this city seven days, and had not known there was anything in it waiting for him.

The one with his boots on stood up.

"Wait," he said.

He said it in the Koine.

Kael had chosen the Koine to make a point of it and had been ready for the point to cost him something. The man handed it back with one word, went in, and shut the door behind him without any noise at all.

They waited four minutes and some. Kael counted it because it was there to be counted, and because there was nothing else in that street: nothing came up it and nothing went down it the whole time.

So he looked at the house. Plaster off it in two places, in the way plaster comes off a wall that nobody has been paid to care about in ten years. Shutters up along the whole of the upper floor in the middle of the day. Two windows below, with cloth drawn across the inside of both.

Valeria had put herself on the side the door was on. Her hand had not been near the sword since the corner and she had not taken the belt off either.

"If it goes badly in there," Kael said, without turning his head, "we run. No point in fighting these people."

"Yes."

"I have nothing else besides that for now."

"No," she said. "You have not."

The door opened.

"In."

There was one room, and in it a table, four stools, a jug and a hearth.

Bedding for two, rolled and standing on end against the wall. One pot. Ash in the hearth raked flat and left. Nothing by the door and nothing in the corners: no rope-ends, no rind, no bones, none of the small rubbish a room grows in a week whatever anybody does about it.

Two men on a door, day and night, and nothing behind them worth two men.

The third man had his shoulder against the frame of the back door. Somewhere near forty. Nothing on his belt, and not the shoulders for it either, and a face Kael could not have described to anybody an hour afterwards. When he spoke it was the Koine again, and it had nowhere in it at all. Every mouth Kael had ever listened to had a place under it. The governor's had the coast. The smith's Ivrit had the fold in it and was not the coast's, and everybody in Doresh had remarked on Kael's for the same reason.

This one had been rubbed until there was nothing left on it to hold.

"Say it again," he said.

"I have information about the bridge-house."

The man looked at him for a while.

"Whose."

"Mine."

"Who sent you."

"Nobody."

"Who else have you said it to."

"Nobody."

He asked those three, and then he did not ask a fourth.

He did not ask what the information was.

"Sit down," he said.

They sat, and nothing happened for a long time.

The man went out through the back and did not come back. One of the two off the bench sat on the end of the table with his heel hooked on the leg of it; the other stood. Neither of them said one word, to Kael or to each other. Neither of them looked at the two children for longer than a second at a time, and both of them knew where both children were for the whole of it, which Kael could tell because he was doing the same thing himself.

Sitting there, he worked out that the decision was not being taken in that house.

Somebody had gone somewhere with a sentence in his mouth and would come back with an answer, and the walk to wherever that was, out and back, was the only measurement in the room that was going to be offered to him. He took it.

Twenty-two minutes.

Then the back door opened and the man came in with two sacks in his hand.

---

"I'll need these sacks on your heads," he said. "And the steel on the table."

Kael did not move for a moment.

A sack is not a thing you put on a man you mean to kill. You do not need him blind for that. You need him blind for what comes after, which meant there was an after, and that it was somewhere else, and that the somewhere else was worth the trouble of hiding from two children.

That was the whole of the good news and it took him about a second to have it. After it there was nothing.

Blind, and unarmed, and off a street, and nobody alive knowing where either of them was except a girl in a room over a mule yard who could not come out of it to tell anyone.

"How far," he said.

"Not far."

Which was not a distance, and had not been meant to be one.

Valeria was looking at the sacks and not at the man.

"Once those are on we are two people being carried," she said. "There is nothing in that I am any use for."

"I know."

"I am telling you so that it has been said."

"It has been said."

Kael put his belt on the table.

Valeria took about two seconds longer than he did, which from the outside was nothing at all.

"I would rather carry it and not use it," she said.

"I would rather you did not carry it."

She put the sword on the table beside Kael's and stood with her hands loose.

Then the man went over both of them. He was quick about it, thorough about it, not rough about it. He took the knife out of Kael's boot and one out of Valeria's sleeve and put them with the swords. Then he pushed both their sleeves back to the elbow to look at the forearms, and stopped at the wrists, at the marks a band leaves on an arm that has been in one for seventy days. He said nothing about them. He let the sleeves down again.

"Sacks," he said.

Kael picked one up. It had held grain and it still smelled of it. He put it on first, so that there would be nothing left in that room for anybody else to have to decide about.

---

He counted from the back door.

Eleven strides of packed dirt, and a turn to the right. Forty-two on the same, and a right again. Thirty on stone, going down about one in twenty, with water running in a channel on the left the whole way, which he had heard at the gate of Qeshet and had not heard anywhere since.

Then a long one. Two hundred and sixteen. Cloth over the street for part of it, because the sun came off his shoulders and came back on again. Dung, hot oil, and the sweet rotten smell of a place where fruit is sold, and a woman shouting a price twice.

At three hundred and forty-one he crossed three strides of loose board with a soft place in the middle of the second one.

At five hundred and nine he crossed it again.

He did not say anything about that. He put it with the rest of it and went on counting, and after that he counted the turns and stopped believing the distances, because a man who walks you over the same board twice is going to walk you over it a third time if it suits him.

The hand on his arm never once pulled. It only ever weighed a little more on one side than the other. He went where it was heavier. It was a better way of moving a person than any he had been moved by.

Then the ground went soft and then it went to mud, and there was the river.

"Step down. There is a step. It is where you think it is."

The boat took him at the knees and he sat where he was put.

On water a count of strides is worth nothing at all, so he took what there was. Twelve minutes and some. Two oars, and one man on them, and he was working — the catch was hard and the recovery was slow, and no man rows like that going with a current. They went upstream. There was a bank close on his left the whole way, because the sound off the water changed when the boat came out from it and changed back when it went in again, four times.

Nobody said one word from the step down to the step up.

---

Boards, then. Fourteen strides of them, hollow underneath. A door, and the light in the sack going dark.

Hemp. Tar. And a wheel turning somewhere a long way ahead of him, slow, with a squeak in it every second turn, and feet going away and feet coming back.

The sacks came off.

He was standing at the end of a building longer than any building he had ever been inside. It had no floor except the ground and no wall on the river side except posts, and it went away from him under a low roof until it stopped being lit. Three men were walking backwards down the length of it, paying hemp out of the bundles at their waists. A fourth was at the wheel. Not one of the four turned round.

Valeria had her sleeves pulled down over the marks. She did not look at the length of the building and she did not look at the men. She looked at the door they had come in by, and then at the one in the side wall six strides from it, and then at Kael.

"Four with us," she said. "Five off the water."

"Yes."

He had twenty-two minutes of a man walking somewhere and coming back. He had twelve on the river, going up, close in.

It was not a place on a map. It was a shape on one. The shape was not large. He put it where he put things.

The door in the side wall opened.
