# Chapter Forty-Two — The Question

From the shelf he could see the whole of it, and none of it had water in it.

The sun was up now and it had taken the frost off the tops of the stone and left it in the shadows, and it lay along the low ground in long white seams where the light had not got yet. The country went away east and south in the grey backs he had seen from below, one behind another, with the thorn in the cuts between them, and the road going through the middle of the flat where the camp was. The smoke of it stood straight up in the cold. The box was a black thing at the near end with its door shut.

He looked at all of it as he had been taught to look at ground, which was to ask it one question, and the question was where three people who could not fight fifty men were going to be by the middle of the day.

Not up. Up was more of this: bare stone and thorn and a wind, and no cover a man could not see a mile of, and no water at all. West was the road they had come up, which was the road the horses would come up. South was the flat and the fifty.

He turned north.

North the back of the world dropped away on its cold side, the side the sun did not get to until late, and a long way down it there was a fold in the ground, and in the fold there was a dark line that was not stone and not thorn. He looked at it for a while to be sure. It was trees. Where the light came off them at the near edge he could see they went down into the fold and along it, following the seam in the ground the way trees follow water, because that was where the water was.

He came down off the shelf.

Valeria was where he had left her, on one knee against the cold face of the slab with the princess in the crook of the wall beside her, and she looked at him and did not ask, which was the thing she did instead of asking.

"North," he said. "Down. There are trees. Trees on ground this high mean water, and cover, and it is away from the road." He got the words out flat and in order, because his own voice had started to come from a little further off than it should, and flat was the only way he could be sure of it. "Two hours. Maybe less."

"She cannot walk two hours," Valeria said.

"No."

Valeria stood up, and got the princess up off the ground and over her shoulder in one motion, in irons, with the hand's width of chain between her wrists, and her ribs did the thing on the lift that showed in her face and nowhere else and was gone off it before she had straightened.

"Then we had better start," she said.

---

It took nearer three hours than two, because the ground on the cold side was worse than it had looked from the top, and twice they had to go along a shelf a long way to find a place to get down off it, and Kael had one hand.

He kept the count. It was the only thing he had that still worked as it was supposed to, and he put it on the strides, because a stride was a thing with an answer in it, and a man who has the count has something. Down two shelves, a hundred and forty. Along the shelf to the break, ninety. Down again. The sun came round the shoulder behind them and put their shadows out long in front of them down the slope, and it began, very slightly, to be warm on the back of his neck, and it did nothing at all for the cold that had got into him from the inside and had a different cause.

His hand he carried against his chest inside the coat and did not look at and tried not to think about, which was not possible, because it thought about itself. It had a pulse in it now that was not the pulse in his wrist. It had its own, faster, and it went with a kind of pressure, as if the hand were a thing being slowly filled, and every so often the pressure would spike and go up the arm into the shoulder and he would lose the count for a stride and have to find it again. He found it again every time. He was proud of that in a way he did not have room to look at.

The trees, when they got to them, were not the woods of the island. They were dark and small and close together, needle trees, standing in a fold with their feet in wet ground, and the wet ground had a stream in the bottom of it running cold and fast and brown over stones, and it made a noise, and the noise of it was the best thing Kael had heard in two months.

Valeria put the princess down against a trunk. The princess sat where she was put, and looked at the water, and did not go to it.

Kael went to it. He got down on his knees at the edge of it and put his right hand in and drank out of it, badly, with his face down near the surface because he could not cup a hand, and it was so cold it hurt his teeth and the front of his skull and he drank until it did.

Then he put the left hand in.

He had not meant to. He had meant to look at it first. But the cold came up off the water at it and the hand wanted the cold the way a burn wants it, and he put it in before he had decided to, and the cold went into the hot swollen ruin of it and for one moment it was the most enormous relief he had ever felt in his life, and then the moment turned over and became the other thing, and the pain came up out of the cold worse than the heat had been, white and total, and he made a sound.

Valeria was there. She had his wrist, and she was holding the hand under the water, down, not letting him pull it out.

"Leave it," she said. "The cold is good for it. Leave it in."

"It—" he said.

"I know. Leave it in. Count."

He counted. She held the wrist and he counted, and the pain settled from white down to red down to something he could stand at the edges of, and the cold began to do whatever the cold does, and after a while she let go of the wrist and he left the hand where it was of his own accord and knelt there in the wet with his eyes shut.

The princess had come across the ground on her hands and knees while he was not looking and was at the water beside him, drinking out of her own two hands in the iron, and she was doing it right, small mouthfuls with a wait between them, which was a thing somebody had taught her in the last two months, and he did not think it had been him.

He took his hand out of the water at last and looked at it in the light coming down through the trees.

It was worse than he had let himself think and better than it might have been. The back of it was not skin from the knuckles to the wrist; it had begun to try to be a scab across the whole of it and had not managed and was wet and red and going a colour at the edges he did not like, a colour that was travelling, and above the wrist there was a red line coming up the inside of the forearm that had not been there in the night. He knew what that was. His mother had taught him what that was, on a lamb, on a winter morning, with the words *this is the body deciding to spend the whole animal to save part of it, and it is nearly always wrong.*

He had, he thought, a few days. Perhaps more with water and cold and rest, which he had one of now. Perhaps a great deal fewer if it went on the way that line said it was going.

He put the hand back inside the coat.

"We have to talk about what we do," he said, "and we have to do it now, because we are going to have visitors."

---

He set the ground as his father had taught him, which was to decide where you wanted the other man to be and then make the ground tell him to go there.

There was one place to cross the stream in the fold, where a tree had come down across it years ago and the ground had built up behind it, and a man could get over there dry and could not easily get over anywhere else without going a long way up or down. On the far side of the crossing the trees stood close, and between two of the closest there was a gap you had to bend to get through, and past the gap the ground opened into a small flat clear place with the bank rising on the near side.

He put the princess flat behind the bank, in a hollow where the roots of a live tree had pulled up out of the earth, out of the line of anything, and he crouched down in front of her and made her look at him.

"They are going to come across the stream, and through that gap, one at a time, because there is no other way that is not slower," he said. "You do not move. Whatever you hear, you do not move and you do not make a sound. If it goes wrong and a man comes over this bank at you." He took the small knife from his belt, the small one off the man at the ring, and turned it and put the handle of it into her two bound hands. "You put this into him, anywhere, and you let go of it, and you get away from him. Do you understand me. Say it back."

"I put it in him," the princess said, in the third of her voice, "and I let go, and I get away."

"Yes."

She held the little knife in both hands and looked at it, and then at him.

"You are going to fall down," she said. "You are grey."

"I know what colour I am," Kael said. "Do not move."

He gave the good knife to Valeria, and Valeria took it and went and lay down in the trees on the far side of the gap, in the deep needle-dark where the sun had not come and would not, flat, with the hand's width of chain gathered up in her fists and the knife point-down out of the bottom of them, in the place she would be when a man came through the gap bent over with his head down, and she stopped moving, and once she had stopped moving Kael, who knew exactly where she was, could not find her.

He took the bank himself, on his belly, above the gap, where he could see the crossing and the gap both, with the eleven links of chain that hung off his right wrist gathered into his one good hand and nothing else. It was not much. He was not much. He knew the exact size of what he was worth in this and it was not large, and he had one thing they did not have, which was that he knew they were coming and from where.

He put his face in the needles and breathed and kept the count on the stream, on the note of it going over the stones, so that if the note changed he would have it, and he waited.

The frost went off the last of the shadows. The sun came a little further round. The red line went a little further up his arm; he could feel it go. He did not move.

Somewhere out past the near edge of the trees, up the slope, a horse put its foot on stone.

---

He heard them a long time before he saw them, which was the whole point of the ground.

Twelve. He got it off the horses first, the number of them coming down the slope above the wood, and then off the men when they came down through the near trees on foot, leading, because horses would not go into that fold and down to that stream in any order a man could use. His count had said twelve in the fold of the rock at first light and here were twelve, and he took a small cold pleasure in it that had nothing to do with anything.

They stopped at the top of the trees. There was talk, low, and it came down to him through the wood in pieces, in Ivrit, and he lay on the bank and understood every word of it, which not one of them knew a boy from an island could do.

A voice that gave orders and was used to it told five men to stay with the horses at the top and keep them quiet. That left seven. One of the seven was not a soldier; Kael could hear it in how the others talked to him and how he talked back, which was less and slower, and this one went down to the stream first, alone, ahead of the rest, and Kael put his eye to the gap in the roots and watched him come.

He came bent, slow, his eyes down, a man reading the ground and not looking for a fight, and he stopped at the crossing and crouched and looked at it for a while, and then he spoke back up the slope without turning round, and he read the three of them off the ground out loud to the man who gave orders, the way a clerk reads a list.

"Three. The boy is here." A hand at the crossing. "He is bleeding, badly, from the left hand; there is blood on every second stone. He will be slow and he will be getting slower. Two of them have no boots. One is being carried; the marks of the one carrying are deep and short, so a grown one carrying a light one." A pause. "The little one that is carried does not put her feet down at all."

Up the slope the voice that gave orders said a word Kael did not have, and then, in words he did have: "The girl is not touched. You hear me. Not marked, not touched, nothing. The two others are nothing to me. But if the girl comes back with a mark on her he will do to us what he is doing to himself out here, and I have watched him do it for two months. Bring her whole."

So that was the shape of them, and it was a good shape, and it was theirs to use.

The tracker came over the crossing.

He came over dry, on the fallen tree, and down onto the needles on the far side, and he came toward the gap between the two close trees with his head down and his eyes on the blood Kael had laid there for him, one careful stone at a time, and he bent to come through the gap.

Kael did not see the first part of it. Nobody saw the first part of it. There was a movement in the dark past the gap that was not a movement so much as the dark deciding to have a shape in it, and the tracker's head went back and his whole body arched and there was no sound at all except the small wet sound of the thing itself, and then the man was down and folded over and still, half through the gap, and the dark was flat and empty again.

The second man was three strides behind him and did not see it happen. He saw the tracker was down and he saw, he thought, that the tracker had fallen, and he came forward fast to him with his spear up, and he bent to the man on the ground, and the dark took him too, low, under the jaw, and he made one sound that was cut in half.

The third man saw a tree move.

He was further back and he was looking the right way at the wrong instant, and he saw, in the dark past the gap, one of the close trees seem to lean and come upright, and he knew, the way a soldier knows, that a tree does not do that, and he opened his mouth and shouted a name up the slope.

Kael went for him from the bank.

He came off the bank onto the man's back as the man was turning toward the shout, with the good knife in his right hand and the whole of his small weight behind it, and he put it into the side of the neck where his father had taught him a man carries the thing that matters, and the jar of it going home came back up his arm and into the ruined hand and the hand answered with a bolt of white that nearly put him out, so that he and the dying man went down into the needles together and for a moment he did not know which of them was which. He held on. He held the knife in and held the man down and the man stopped, and Kael got his knees under him and got up, swaying, one-handed, and the wood had gone to four men and a shout, and the shout was already up the slope.

They came all at once then, the last four, because there was nothing left to be quiet for.

Two went at the gap, and that was a mistake, because the gap only took one, and the first one through it bent his head to come through and Valeria came up out of the floor of the wood in front of him and took him with both fists together, point down, in under the collarbone, and rode him down with her whole body, and was up off him and had the second one as he came through behind, across the throat, the chain snapping taut between her wrists and the blade running along it, and neither of them had got a spear up in that gap where a spear was no use to anybody.

The other two came round.

They did not come through the gap. They came round the end of the bank, one and then the other, men who had understood the gap was death, and the first of them came round it straight onto Kael, who had nothing but a knife slick with another man's blood and one hand and a body that had begun, very quietly, to shut down, and the man hit him with the flat of a shoulder and put him on the ground and came down on top of him with a knee going into the middle of him and a hand, a whole hand, a working hand, closing over Kael's ruined left one where it lay in the needles and grinding it, on purpose, because he had seen it, because a man in a fight uses the thing that will make the other man stop, and Kael's whole world went to that hand and there was nothing else in it.

The princess came up off the ground behind the man.

Kael did not see her decide to. He was told about it afterward by the wood, which was that the grinding stopped, and the man's weight came off him and turned, and there was a sound out of the man that was surprise before it was pain. When Kael got his eyes to work the princess was on her knees behind the soldier with both her bound hands round the handle of the little knife and the little knife in the back of the man's thigh to the guard, and she had not let go of it, she had done exactly the opposite of what he had told her, she was holding on to it with the whole of her weight, and the man was twisting round to her with the spear-butt coming up.

He did not bring it down.

He got it up, and he had it up over a girl on her knees who had just put a knife in his leg, and he did not bring it down, and the reason he did not was that even now, even here, with the knife in him, some part of him had looked at her hair and her face and known what he was not allowed to do to it, and that half-second of a grown man not doing the thing his body was already doing was the whole of the fight.

Kael got the chain round the ankle of the leg he was standing on.

He did it from the ground, with his one hand, the eleven links whipping round the boot and his fist closing on the far end of them, and he pulled the leg out sideways with everything he had left, and the man came down. He came down onto his own planted knife, and the princess got out from under him and away, without her knife, exactly as she had been told to do the first half of and none of the second, and the man was on the ground with a knife in the back of his thigh and the fall having driven it, and he screamed.

Valeria was over him with the point of the good knife under his jaw.

"No," Kael said. He could hardly get the word out. He was on his hands and knee, one hand, one knee, in the needles beside the two of them, and the word came out of him wrong. "No. That one. Alive."

Valeria looked at him for the length of a breath. Then she took her knife away from the man's jaw and put her heel down hard on the good knee, the one he might have got up on, and something in it went, and the man stopped being able to do anything but be where he was.

The seventh was running.

He had seen the six of them go and he was going back up the slope for the horses and the five, and Valeria took three running steps and picked a fallen man's spear up off the ground on the second of them and threw it, two-handed, from the shoulder, in irons, and it took him between the shoulder-blades at a dozen strides and he went down into the needles and slid and stopped.

The wood was quiet.

Up the slope, a long way off, through the trees, a horse was unhappy about something, and a man's voice said one word to it, and then nothing.

Six men were dead in the space of forty feet, and one was on the ground alive with a knife in his leg and a knee that would not hold him, and there were five more men and twelve horses at the top of the wood who had heard a shout and a scream and then silence, and who were not going to sit up there with it for very long.

---

"How long," Valeria said. She was already at the nearest body, going through it, fast, her hands moving over the belt and inside the coat.

"Not long." Kael got himself upright somehow and made himself think past the hand, which wanted the whole of him. "They will send one back to the column and the rest will come down into the trees to look. When they find what is here, they will not come on in the dark under the trees after whatever did it. They will wait for the ten times more of them the one who rode back is bringing." He was working it as he said it and it came out right. "We have until the light goes, maybe. We do not have longer than that."

"Then we are quick," Valeria said.

They were quick. What six dead men had was more than they had had that morning by a great deal: four skins, three of them full, and Kael got the first full one against his own mouth and drank until his stomach hurt and then made himself stop, because a man who drinks it all now is a man with none tomorrow. Bread, hard, in cloth. Two cloaks that would do for the cold, and boots, boots off two of them small enough for the princess's feet and Valeria's, and Valeria sat the princess down and put a dead man's boots on her cut feet without a word and did the same for her own and said nothing about any of it. Swords, which Kael left, because a sword was not a thing he could use and not a thing they could carry three of. A short spear he kept. Nothing on any belt, on any of the six, that would open a peened iron pin: no key, no chisel, no file, no hammer. He had known there would not be. He looked anyway, on all six, with the part of his mind that was still doing arithmetic, and there was not.

The princess was on her knees by the man Valeria had kneed, with her small knife back in her hand. She had gone and pulled it out of his leg herself, which he had not seen her do. She was holding it, and she was looking at the man, and she was not looking away from him.

"Give me that," Kael said, and she gave it to him.

He got down in front of the man on the ground.

The man was perhaps thirty, brown, with a soldier's face and a soldier's beard, and he was breathing in the short hard way of a man with a wound driven deep and a knee that had gone, and he was afraid, and he was watching the three of them with the particular attention of a man who has just watched six others die and is doing arithmetic of his own.

Kael put the plan together in his head first, in order, because that was how he did everything and because it kept the fever a step behind him.

Where they were. A village. A smith, to cut the iron. A healer. In that order, because the first two you got out of a man and the last two you got out of a place, and the place was the first thing and the man on the ground was the fastest way to it.

He spoke to the man in Ivrit.

He did it low, and level, in the coast-and-stone shape of it his mother had taught him at eight for no reason he had ever been given, and he watched what it did to the man's face, which was a great deal. The man had spent two months in a column that had carried three foreign children east in a box and it had never once occurred to any of them that one of the children had every word they said.

"You are going to tell me some things," Kael said, "and then this is going to be over. Where are we."

---

The man told him a lie first.

It was a small one and a stupid one, the kind a frightened man reaches for out of habit before he has thought about it, a wrong direction and a wrong distance, and Kael knew it was a lie because he had spent the whole of a morning reading this ground with his own eyes and the man's answer did not fit the ground. He knew it the way he knew a bout was going wrong before he could have said why.

He did not argue with it. Arguing with a lie taught a man that lies were a thing you argued with.

He put the point of the little knife back into the wound the princess had made in the thigh, into it, not near it, and he leaned on it, and he kept his eyes on the man's face and his own voice exactly where it had been.

"Where are we."

The count was going the whole time. He had it on the man's pulse, which he could see in the man's throat, because his father had taught him that a body cannot lie about that; a body speeds up when it is going to lie and it does a different thing when it has decided to tell the truth, and if you know the man's own rhythm first you can read the change. So the first thing he had done, before he hurt him at all, was to take the pulse and hold it, forty counts of it, so that he would have the truth of it to measure the rest against. He was thirteen years old and he was doing it without deciding to, out of a school with no first day, and somewhere a long way under the fever a part of him watched himself do it and had nothing to say.

The man told him where they were.

He gave it up in pieces, around the knife, in a voice that had climbed most of the way to a place a voice should not go: the high ground they were on was called the Kharoun, the whole long cold back of it, and it went on east for days, and the road ran along the south skirt of it, and off the north side of it, down, half a day for a man who could walk and further for one who could not, there was a village.

"Its name."

"Doresh." The man's pulse did the thing it did when a man had stopped choosing his words. "Doresh. There is a headman and there are maybe forty houses and there is a smith because there is a smith in all of them, they shoe the mules that go up the road. Down the water. You follow the water down off the Kharoun and it takes you to Doresh, it is the only water on this side, everyone comes down to the same water—"

He was giving more than he had been asked for now, which was what they did when they had turned the corner, and Kael let him, and took the shape of it, and held the pieces against the pulse and against the ground he had read himself, and they fit. The Kharoun. Doresh, half a day north and down, on the water. A smith. It was true. He was as sure of it as he was of anything, and he had what he had come for.

He should have stopped there.

He knew, afterward, that he should have stopped there, and he had the ground and the village and the smith, and there was nothing else the man could give him that he needed, and he did not stop.

Because there was one more thing, and it was not on the list, and it had been sitting under the list the whole morning like the red line under the skin of his arm, travelling, and the fever brought it up into his mouth before the count could get in front of it.

"There was a house," Kael said. His voice had changed. He heard it change and could not put it back. "Nine, ten days east of Qeshet. A woman. A baby. An old man. You were on that road. What came out of that house."

"I was not—" the man said, "—I was on the road, I never went up to the—"

Kael took the man's own hand off the ground, the working hand, the one that had ground his in the needles, and he held it flat in his one hand against the man's own chest, and he found the thumb, and he found the joint at the base of it, which he knew, which he knew better in that moment than he knew anything else in the world, because he had made two pieces with a hinge of skin of his own in the dark not twelve hours before.

"What came out of that house."

"I don't—" the man said, and his pulse was flat, it was flat and fast and going nowhere a lie goes, and Kael read it, and the reading told him the man was telling the truth and the fever told him to do it anyway, and for one second, one second he would have back if there were any way on earth to have a second back, the fever won.

He bent the thumb.

The man screamed, and told him nothing, because there was nothing, because he had been on the road and not in the house and he had seen three carried out to a box at dawn and not one thing more, and Kael heard all of that come out of him in pieces under the pain and heard, underneath it, the flat true bottom of a man who has nothing left to give and is trying to give it anyway, and knew it for what it was.

He stopped.

He let go of the hand and he sat back on his heels in the needles and he was shaking, and he did not know how much of the shaking was the fever and how much was the other thing, and he put the count on his own breath and held it there and did not look at the princess and did not look at Valeria and looked at the man, who was weeping now, and whole in the ways that could be seen and unmade in the way that could not.

"I believe you," Kael said, in Ivrit, and it was the truth, and it was two words too late to be worth anything.

Valeria killed him.

She did it while Kael was still sitting back, without being asked and without asking, one clean movement, the good knife under the jaw and up, the way she had done it to the man at the ring, and she held her free hand flat on his chest until it stopped, and then she took her hand away and stood up.

Kael heard it happen. He did not stop her. He had the whole of the half-second it would have taken to say the word and he did not say it, and he knew, kneeling there with his ruined hand against his chest and his mother's red line climbing his arm, that he had not said it because it was the right thing to have done and because he could not have made himself do it and that both of those were true and neither of them cancelled the other.

The princess had watched the whole of it. From the first lie to the last breath, she had watched it, on her knees in a dead man's coat with her hands in iron in her lap, and she had not looked away, and now she was looking at Kael, and he made himself look back at her, because he thought he owed her that, and he could not read one thing in her face, and for once in his life it was not because there was nothing there to read.

---

They went down off the Kharoun by the water, north, into the shadow the sun had not reached all day.

They went as fast as three people in irons could go, one of them carrying a girl part of the way and the girl walking part of it, in a dead man's boots, on her own feet, ten strides and then twenty and then, once, nearly a hundred before her legs gave out and Valeria caught her. Behind them and above them, back up in the trees, men were calling names into the wood and getting no answer, and their voices got smaller as the fold took the trio down, and after a while there was only the water.

Kael carried the coat and a skin and the short spear and could carry nothing else, and he kept his feet by putting them one after another, and he kept the rest of himself by keeping the count.

He counted the princess's strides when she was walking, because it was the only number he had that went up instead of down, and because it was a thing with an answer in it, and because a man who has the count has something.

He did not count anything else.
