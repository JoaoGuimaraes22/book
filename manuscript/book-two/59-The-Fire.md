# Chapter Fifty-Nine — The Fire

The empire came into the fold with fire on the front of it, and the fire was real.

It was not Thiro's fire, the fire with no flame that took the air out of a man and left no mark. It was the other kind, the kind that is orange in the dark and eats what it touches, and it came off the empty left hand of the officer riding at the head of the torch-column and went into the coalition line at the bottom of the fold in a sheet, and men who had come four hundred miles to kill a bandit crew burned where they stood, and the fold, which had been a camp with a fire in it, became a fire with a camp in it.

Kael stood at the wheel of the far wagon with the princess up on the board behind him and had, in one glance, the whole of what there was no surviving.

Coalition at the bottom, forty of them, more, in nothing, who had come for the crew. Empire at the top, ten and an officer who was a fire, who had come for the coin and found the coalition. Crew in the middle, what was left of it, fifteen become a number he did not want to say. Three sides, and two of them wanted each other dead as much as they wanted the money, and the crew was the ground the other two were going to fight on.

"That is not a fight we are in," he said, out loud, to nobody, the way his father would have said it. "That is a fight we are under."

---

He got the princess onto the far wagon and put the reins in her hands.

"You have driven these mules to water every night for nine months," he said. "Drive them now. When Tirzah's wagon moves, you move behind it, and you do not stop for anything, and you do not look at anything, and if I am not on it you go anyway."

"Kael—"

"You go anyway." He put his good hand flat on the mule's neck, once, the animal shaking under it. "Aeliana. You have carried the water. Now carry it out."

She looked at him, and her face did a thing, and then it stopped doing it, because she was the Emperor's daughter and she had ruled on harder things than this in worse rooms, and she took the reins and set her feet.

Behind him the fire went up the fold.

---

Valeria met the first of them where the wagons stood, and the ground helped her.

She put her hand flat and took it out from under three men at once, the earth going down and sideways, sand with the bottom pulled out of it, and they dropped to the knee and could not run and she was through them before the third had understood he had stopped. She did not fight them the way she fought Kael, with a measure and a stop. She fought them the way the line of Vel was made to fight, which was to end a thing in the fewest possible motions and go to the next thing, and Kael, cutting a coalition man off the tail of the near wagon with the threading up the whole of the borrowed sword, saw her open a man from the hip to the sternum on the way past him to somewhere else and not look back to see him fall.

The threading held. His hand did not. He put it through the first man clean and the second man he had to take on the feet, not being where the spear was, the left hand trying to parry and closing on nothing, and he felt the whole of what Valeria had told him on the flat.

"Wagon," Borren's voice went across all of it. "Load and go. Now."

---

The twins had the near wagon moving.

Oran on the box with the reins and Talo throwing chests up onto it, one and then another, fifteen of them, half, and leaving the rest, and Tirzah swinging up over the board with the basket still on her, of course with the basket, and the mules leaning into it, and it started to move, slow, then less slow, out of the top of the fold onto the track.

"Go," Borren said, to them, and did not watch them go, because he was walking down the fold at Thiro.

Kael saw the whole of the next thing and could do nothing with any of it, which was the oldest feeling he had, older than the box, older than the Temple, the feeling of a boy on the ground with broken ribs watching the only man who could not lose begin to lose.

---

Thiro laughed when he saw the old man coming.

He had taken the breath out of six of the crew by then without moving toward any of them, and the coalition men behind him were burning where the empire's officer reached them, and Thiro did not seem to mind either thing, the dying of his own men or the dying of the crew, because he was looking at Valeria across the fire, and now at the old man walking at him, and he was a young man who had never in his life met a thing he could not do.

"Old man," he said, in Avestan, delighted. "Old man, you are breathing. Nobody breathes when I'm around."

And he put the volume of dead air over Borren again, thicker, wider, a killing weight of nothing, and Borren walked into it and kept his own ten feet close against himself and it did not touch him, and Kael, thirty strides off, understood that he was watching two men do the same thing to the same air and one of them was going to run out first.

"Valeria. That's your real name, is it not," Borren said. Not loud. It went to her the way his orders went, through everything. "Target his feet. I have the air."

And Valeria, who had gone into this man once on a shelf above the sea and put four inches of steel in him twice and been thrown into a door frame by the hair, went in again.

---

It took the three of them, and it took everything, and it took too long.

Borren held the air. That was the thing only Borren could do: he stood at eight feet, then six, then four, and he held his own air against Thiro's the way you hold a door against a man on the other side of it, and it cost him, Kael could see it costing him, the old man's face going grey and wet, an old man doing a young man's work.

Thiro stopped laughing.

He gave the air up, because the air was no use against the two who were inside it now, and he did the other thing, the thing that had put Valeria in a door frame, he fought, and he was fast, faster than anything Kael had seen that was not his father, all of him always moving, and he got a hand into Valeria's hair and Kael saw her head go back and saw the knife come up toward her throat and threw the only thing he had, which was himself, and the threading, into the back of Thiro's knee.

The leg went. The never-still man, made still, one leg, for half a second.

It was enough. Valeria got her feet under her with his hand still in her hair, and she did not go for his body this time, she went up, both blades, under the jaw and up, the way you finish a thing you have already learned does not die from being stabbed in the trunk, and Thiro's hand opened in her hair, and the young man who had never met the thing he could not do met it, and went down, and did not claw at his throat, because there was nothing wrong with his throat, there was a girl's blade in his brain.

He was dead before he finished falling, and it had cost them the only man who was holding anything, because Borren had spent the whole time holding the air.

---

Ambros was standing at the top of the fold by the abandoned chests, not running.

Kael saw him in a cut of the firelight, the coat buttoned to the throat, the blue at the collar the princess had sewn, and he was not running, and there was nowhere for him to run to, because the coalition he had served for the length of a winter was burning at the bottom of the fold and the empire was coming down the top of it and the crew he had eaten with for a year was dead or dying all around him, and a man who has spent everything he had on keeping something alive, and finds at the end that he has bought nothing, does not run. He stands where he is and waits for the bill.

Borren came to him.

The old man was spent, grey, moving like a man twice his own age now, the air gone out of him, and he came up the fold to Ambros through the burning of it, and Kael, twenty strides off and moving toward Borren heard the whole of it.

"They took my daughters," Ambros said. He did not say it to be forgiven. He said it the way he said everything, as a thing that had come in and a thing that had gone out. "In the winter. A man came and showed me a thing that they had, that could only have come off one of them, and he told me what would happen, and I wrote it down for him. Everything. I wrote it all down." His voice did not break. "I do not know if it kept them alive. I will never know now."

"No," Borren said. "You will not."

"I am sorry, Borren. Not to you. You were going to die anyway, all of you, the day you took that much. I am sorry to the girl. She sewed the blue."

Borren looked at him for the length of a breath, the way he had looked at Kael across a fire, the way he had looked at Tirzah.

"I know what they do," Borren said. "I know it better than you will ever know it. I have done it. But, you must understand me too." And there was a thing in that Kael did not have the room to hear then and would have for the rest of his life. "I am not going to tell you it is all right. It is not all right. But I know."

And he killed him. Not with the air, which was gone. With his hands, one on the back of the head and one under the jaw, the way the crew took a room, four seconds, quiet, close, a mercy and a sentence in the same motion, and he held the old man against his own chest while it happened the way you hold a thing you are setting down, and when it was done he let him down into the grass gently, and that was the last strength he spent on anybody but Kael.

---

"You are still here," Borren said.

Kael was there. He had not gone to the wagon. He had come the other way, up the fold, toward the old man, because there was a house on a mountain in him that he had never once stopped being outside of, and a father in a yard he had watched a general put a flat hand through, and he was not going to stand at a wheel and watch it a second time.

"Come with me," Kael said. "You are spent. I have you. I did the count on the track, there is a way east off the fold the empire has not reached, I can get you—"

"Kael."

"—I can get you to the wagon, it is nine strides, I have you, I am not—"

"Kael." Borren took his face in one hand. His hand was cold. Behind them the officer's fire came down the fold, orange, eating the dry grass, eating the dead, coming. "Listen to me, because I do not have another one of these. You cannot carry me. If you try to carry me we both stand here and the fire comes and the girls go over that lip alone with nobody who can fight, and everything I spent tonight I spent for nothing. You are the one who counts. Do the count. There is no version of this where I am on that wagon. Say it."

Kael did the count.

He did not want to do it and he did it because the man told him to and because it was true, and it came out the way it had come out on a mountain, the arithmetic clean and monstrous: the old man could not run, the fire was coming, two people carrying a third are three people who die, and the wagon needed one more sword over the lip and had exactly the time it took the fire to cross forty feet of grass to get it.

"There is no version," Kael said. His voice was not his.

"No," Borren said, and something in the grey face eased, because the boy had done the sum and got the true answer, and that was the last thing he had wanted to teach him. "Good."

---

The warehouse man died getting Kael to the wagon.

He came out of the smoke where nobody was, the man who had wanted a stone warehouse and a chair in the door of it. He got a shoulder under Kael's arm and hauled him bodily off his feet toward the lip. Six strides. Five. A coalition man who was on fire and did not know it yet came out of the burning with a spear and put it through the warehouse man's back and out the front of him. The man's face, over Kael's shoulder, did the thing a face does. He said, "Go on, then," which was a thing Borren said, and shoved Kael the last of it with the spear still in him, and Kael went over the lip and the man did not.

He never learned his name. He had learned a lock off him and a story about a warehouse and never once his name, and now there was nobody to ask.

---

Borren threw him onto the wagon with the last of the air.

Kael felt it before he understood it, a hand of wind under him that was not wind, a gust with a mind in it, the old man spending the very bottom of himself to put a boy where the boy would not put himself, and Kael came down onto the boards of the far wagon among the chests as it went over the lip, and he came up already turning, already climbing back over the chests toward the lip, toward the fold, toward the old man standing alone in the fire, and a hand closed on the back of his coat and did not let go.

"No," Valeria said.

She was on the wagon. She had the princess driving and she had Kael by the coat with both hands, her arm across him, the same arm that had been in front of him on a boat before a blow was anywhere, and she did not stop his hand this time, because his hand was not the thing. She stopped the whole of him, she put her weight on him and held him down among his father's money while he came apart.

"He is buying this," she said, into his ear, hard, flat, the truth with no comfort in it because there was none and she would not insult him with the other kind. "Do not make it for nothing. Do not."

And Kael stopped fighting her, because it was true, and looked back.

---

Borren stood in the fire at the top of the fold and did not come.

The last Kael saw of him was not a fight, because there was nothing left in him to fight with, the air spent on Thiro and on Ambros and on the gust that put a boy on a wagon. It was an old man standing between the fire and the lip with a plain soldier's sword in his hand and his one eye open, buying with his body the forty feet of grass and the thirty seconds that two wagons needed, and the empire's fire came down the fold to him, and the coalition came up it, and he stood in the place where they had to meet and made them meet on him.

On the other wagon, ahead and to the left, over the lip and gone into the dark of the track, Tirzah was looking back.

Kael saw her across the fire, on the box of the near wagon with the twins, her hand up to her mouth, looking back down into the fold at the old man standing in it, and for one second, one, the old man looked up the track at her, and Kael was between them and had both faces at once, the count giving him the two of them across two hundred feet of burning ground, and he saw the thing pass between them that the old man had never once said and now never would, and he saw her know it, at last, in the worst way a person can be told a thing, by watching the man who never said it stand still in a fire so that she could go.

She did not scream. She was not that kind. She put her hand flat on the board in front of her, as Valeria put a hand on the ground, as though there were something under it she could reach, and there was not, and the wagon took her over the rise and down and Borren was gone from her sight before he was gone from Kael's.

---

The fire closed over the fold behind them.

Kael lay among the chests with Valeria's arm still across him though he had stopped fighting it, and watched the light of it on the underside of the smoke get smaller as the track bent, and bent again, and the ground took them the way the ground had always taken them, down and away, where the road did not go.

He did the count, because he could not stop, and it was the cruelest count he had ever done.

Two wagons. Fifteen chests on each. Six people. Him, the two girls, the two brothers, the woman with the basket. Six, out of eighteen. He counted the ones who were not on the wagons, because the count did not let him not, and he had every one of them, the warehouse man who had wanted a chair, Selis who had laughed at the fire, the four of Ishareth, Talo who had caught the bread out of the air, no, Talo was on the wagon, he made himself do it again, slower, and got it right, and the getting of it right was worse.

And Borren. He put Borren at the end of it, the way you put the heaviest thing at the bottom, and he did not have a place to keep him, because his father was in the place where the dead fathers went and there was only the one place and it was full.

The wagon went down into the dark. Ahead, the other one. Above, no stars could be seen due to the smoke. Behind, the fire, getting smaller, and a man in it, standing, who had asked a boy once what he wanted, and never once who he was, and had known the whole time, and had thrown him onto a wagon with the last breath in his body, the way another man had once closed a boy's hand around a sword, and Kael understood, lying among the coins, that he had now watched it happen twice, and lived through it twice, and that both times he had lived because the man had spent himself to make it so, and that this was the thing he was, the thing that walked away from the fire on the strength of somebody else's dying, and he did not know how a person carried that, and there was no one left on the wagon he could ask.
