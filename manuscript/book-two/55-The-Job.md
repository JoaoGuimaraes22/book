# Chapter Fifty-Five — The Job

The frost came the night before the town.

He knew it was the night before because Borren had said so, which had never happened. *Tomorrow we go in somewhere and we stay.* Not the name. Not for how long. *Until the roads are roads again*, when Kael asked, and that was a season, and he lay in the tent with it and could not make it into a number he trusted.

The cold came up through the ground into his back. On his left the princess had both blankets and was still cold, he could hear it in the way she breathed. On his right, across the door, Valeria lay the way she always lay, on her back with the sword along her leg, and if she was cold there was no way of knowing.

"Do you think it is wrong," Aeliana said.

She said it into the dark, low, and he did not know for a moment which of them she meant it for.

"What," he said.

"What we do. What they do, and we carry the water for it." A pause. "I have carried water for a man who has stolen a toll-keeper's strongbox, and for a man who kept a letter for a lord who paid him to keep it, and for a woman who put a copper in a soldier's hand so that nobody would look at me. I have washed the pot they ate out of afterward. I want to know whether it is wrong. I have not been able to decide."

He thought about it, because she had asked it as a question and not as a thing she already knew, and he had never heard her do that.

"You have decided everything," he said. "Since I have known you."

"Yes."

"And not this."

"No." The blankets moved. "I have sat at that fire for two months and listened to fifteen people tell me what my father's empire is. Not to me. They do not know it is me. They say it to the fire and I am sitting at the fire. A ribbon. A cup. A house with land." She stopped, and he heard her decide not to say the rest of that one. "I was told what the empire was every day of my life by people who were paid to tell me. I had never once heard it from somebody who was not being paid. I find I cannot rule on it. It is the first thing I have not been able to rule on, and I do not like it, and I wanted to say so to somebody."

Nobody said anything for a while. The mules shifted on the line. Somewhere across the camp a man coughed and turned over.

"It is not wrong," Valeria said.

They both waited.

"I was made for one thing," she said. "The thing I was made for is lying beside me under two blankets. Where she stands, I stand, and what is done where she stands is not wrong, because if it were wrong she would not be standing there. That is the whole of it. I am sorry it is not more."

"That is not an answer," Aeliana said. "That is a wall."

"Yes."

"It would hold up anything."

"Yes," Valeria said. "That is what it is for."

Kael lay and heard it and did not say what he thought, which was that she was right that it would hold up anything, and that this was the thing about it that frightened him, and that he had no right to be frightened by it because his own was worse. He had gone up a stair a week ago and cut a man so that the man would keep his arm, and come down, and knelt in a stream, and nothing had come. He had waited for the thing that told him. It had told him, wrongly or rightly, every time there had been blood, and he had built himself around the fact that it came. He did not know what it meant that it had stopped. He did not know whether nothing was what wrong felt like, when you had done enough of it, or whether nothing was what right felt like, when you had finally done a thing cleanly, and he turned the two over in the dark and they weighed the same.

"I do not know," he said.

"You always know."

"No. I know what is in front of me. That is a different thing." He put his good hand flat on the cold ground, because it wanted something to do. "I know that a man on a stair has his arm. I do not know whether that was right. I used to. Something used to tell me and it has stopped telling me and I do not know why."

"When did it stop?"

"At the stream. After the stair."

He had not meant to say that, and he heard Valeria not move.

"Well," Aeliana said, after a time. "Then there are two of us."

---

They lay with that.

Then she said, in a different voice, "I like her."

"Who."

"Adah." She turned over toward him; he heard the blankets go. "Nobody has ever looked at Adah twice. Not once, in two months. Tirzah tells her she is slow with a knot. Selis laughs at her Ivrit. A soldier on a bridge walked straight past her because a woman was arguing about a copper. Do you know what that is? It is the most restful thing that has ever happened to me in my life. I have been looked at since the day I was born. I did not know it was a weight until it came off."

"You did the work nobody told you to do."

"Yes."

"So that nobody would be watching."

"So that nobody would be watching," she said. "And then nobody was, and I found I had done it for the other reason as well. I wanted to be a person who did it. I did not know I wanted that. I did not know I could."

Valeria said, "You have changed."

"Yes. Have you?"

"No."

"Do you want to?"

There was a long quiet on the right side of the tent.

"I do not understand how the question means anything," Valeria said at last, and it was not a refusal; she had gone and looked and come back. "There is what I am for. I am for it. If I changed I would be for something else, and then I would not be me, I would be somebody standing where I had been. You may as well ask the sword."

"The sword does not polish itself every night," Aeliana said.

Kael laughed.

He had not meant to. It came out of him in the dark, once, short, and he heard the princess turn her head toward the sound of it, and then she laughed too, low, into the blanket, and across the door Valeria let out a breath through her nose that in anybody else would have been nothing and in her was a great deal.

"He is changing," Aeliana said, when it had gone. "You said it to him. I heard you, on the slope."

"Yes."

"Into what?"

"I do not know," Valeria said. "Neither does he. I watched him walk into this camp with the road on him and did not know it was him until he was past the fire. I have known his walk for two years. Nobody has ever got past me on a walk."

"Is it a bad thing?"

"I did not say it was a thing. I said it was happening."

Kael lay between them and let them talk about him as though he were a mule on the line, and found that he did not mind it, and did not know what to do with not minding it either.

"When we walk into Solvenna," Aeliana said, "I will have to be her again."

Nobody answered that. It had not been a question.

"I do not know if I want to," she said, and then the cold got into her properly and she pulled the blankets over her head, and that was the end of the talking, and in the morning they went into the town.

---

The town had a name and he was given it and he forgot it, as he had been told he would, and he was surprised to find that he could. Some things did not stay. He had not known that about himself.

It was a town where nobody asked. He never found out whether that was the town or the winter. They were a carrier's yard at the edge of it: a long shed with the mules in it, a house behind, a gate onto a lane, and rope in the shed because the rope was real and a carrier's yard wants rope. Ambros kept the house. Kael had not known what that meant until he watched a man do it: where the food came from and how much; who slept where; what went out of the gate, what came in, whether the lane could see it. Forty-one years of a house forty miles north of the capital and the man ran eighteen thieves in a shed as though the house had never fallen down. Kael, who had grown up under a woman who ran a mountain the same way, watched him and said nothing. Once he caught the princess watching him too, from the door of the kitchen, with a look on her face he could not have priced and did not try to.

He was bad at it. He had known he would be and he had not known how bad. The trade in a town was not the walk or the voice or the lock, it was being there every morning when the woman from the next yard came to the trough, with a face she had seen before and something to say to it. He had nothing to say to it. He had never in his life been somewhere he was allowed to stay, and it turned out that staying was a thing people did with their mouths, and his had been built for other work. The princess had a hundred things to say to the woman at the trough. Valeria had none and did not need any, because a girl who carries water and never speaks is a thing every town has one of. Kael stood between the two of them at the trough with a bucket in his good hand and understood why Borren had priced this winter as a cost and not as rest.

He turned fourteen in it, somewhere. Nobody said so, because nobody knew, and he did not take it out to look at.

Borren made paper all winter in the room at the back of the house. Seals, the wax for them, hands that were not his own hand, documents that said a thing had been permitted by somebody who had never heard of it. He did not hide the work and he did not show it. Kael stood in the door of that room once, in the second month, and watched a man write out a bill of passage in the level Koine of a provincial clerk, blot it, hold it to the light, and burn it because the second *s* was his own, and start again. Then Borren looked up, and Kael went away.

---

In the spring the hand shut.

He had been told the spring and it was the spring, and one morning in the shed he found that the fingers had come round onto the rope without being asked and were holding it, and he stood and looked at that for a while. It was slow to open again. It would hold a rope. It would hold a knife, if the knife did not have to do anything clever. It would never again do what the right one did, and he knew that the way he knew the number of mules in the shed, and put it down, and went on.

The roads became roads. They went out of the town by the gate onto the lane, and nobody in the town asked where. Nobody had asked anything for three months and was not going to start.

The jobs came back the same and faster. He was not started small any more. He was the one on the stair now, whichever stair it was; the one under the window; the one who went into a room ahead of the others because he could get into a room without the room knowing. A strongbox out of an inn. A ledger out of a house whose owner had paid to have it lost. A man's own horse walked out of his own stable under a boy who said the man's name as though he were bored of it. He did the thing the job needed at the moment it needed it and he did not look for anything afterward. He had stopped looking in the winter. Whatever had come after the blood, when it came, had been a thing he waited for; and a thing you wait for that does not come is a thing you stop waiting for. That was all there was to say about it, and he did not say it.

At a river in the fourth month there was a bridge with a soldiers' post on it, and Borren stood on a hill above it and said, without looking round, "Where is your ford," and Kael told him. They crossed at it before light and were past the bridge before the man in the post had his breakfast, and neither of them said a word about it then or afterward.

The princess stayed brown. It did not come off in the winter and by the summer it was not going to. Valeria stayed Valeria.

He had every day still. He never took them out.

---

It was the ninth month when Borren sat down last at the fire and did not have a cup.

That was the first thing. Kael had never seen him come to the fire without one. The second thing was that he looked round the circle before he spoke, one face at a time, the whole way round, and Kael knew before the man opened his mouth that whatever this was he was pricing what it would cost each of them.

"Before I say anything," Borren said, "the rule."

Nobody moved.

"You know it. Nobody who knows how a thing is going to be done leaves until it is done. It has always been about the one thing, and the one thing has always been a night, or a week, or a road." He put his hands together between his knees. "This one is longer than any of you has been under it. If there is anybody at this fire who would rather be somewhere else when the year turns, get up now and go and see to the mules, and I will say nothing until you are out of hearing, and nobody will think anything of it, and you will still have your share of everything up to tonight."

He waited.

Kael watched the circle not move, one face at a time. Tirzah with the basket between her feet. Ambros with the coat buttoned. Oran with a hand on his brother's shoulder and Talo looking at the fire. The talker not talking. The princess, between Tirzah and Selis, with her sleeves down and her hands in her lap. Nobody got up. Nobody looked at anybody else to see whether they would.

"Right," Borren said.

"There is a province west of here. I will not name it. The war is going to come through it. Not this month. Before the leaves are off. The empire knows this, and has known it since the winter, and has done what the empire does when it knows a thing like that, which is write it down." He said it as though he were reading it off a page. "In the chief town of that province there is a tax-house, and under the tax-house there is a room with an iron door, and into that room has gone every coin that province has paid the Emperor for six years. None of it has gone out. It could not go out. The road it would have gone out by has had a war lying across it, and a governor who wanted to keep his head did not send six years of the Emperor's money down a road with a war on it. So it sat."

Kael had the shape of it before the man finished the sentence and did not like the shape.

"Now the war is coming to the room," Borren said. "And the empire is not going to leave six years of coin in a cellar for the men who took Qeshet. They are going to move it. West. Inside the old borders. Before the autumn." He looked at the fire. "Everything we have ever taken walked out of a gate in daylight with the paper in order. This is going to walk out of a gate in daylight with the paper in order. The difference is that this time the empire is going to do the walking."

"How many," Tirzah said.

"Wagons or men?"

"Men."

"I do not know yet. More than we have ever stood near. That is the true answer and you will have the number when I have it."

"How much," said the man who had lost a warehouse. It was the first thing Kael had heard him say since the eleven words at the first fire.

Borren did not answer that at once. He looked round the circle again.

"Enough," he said, "that nobody at this fire works again. Nobody at this fire has children who work. Enough that there is no honest men, after, because there is no reason for there to be." He let that sit. "I have been doing this for a long time. I have never once said that. I am saying it now because it is true and because you are going to be asked to do a thing for it that we have never done, and I do not want anybody doing it for a share of a strongbox."

"Who is paying us," said Ambros.

"Nobody."

That was the thing that went round the fire. Kael watched it go. Every job he had done for these people, a man somewhere had paid for. The letter in his boot had been wanted by somebody. The ledger, the document, the supplies over the hill. He had never known who and had stopped needing to, but there had always been a who, and the who was the shape of the thing: the honest men were a thing you hired. He saw fifteen people hear that for the first time in their lives nobody had hired them, and he saw what it did to their faces, and he could not have said what it was.

"The rest you will have in pieces," Borren said, "the way you have had everything. Tomorrow we go west." He stood up. "Go and hobble something."

---

He sat on after the others had gone, and Borren, who had been going, stopped.

"Say it," Borren said.

"An empire moving six years of coin moves it with soldiers."

"Yes."

"We do not leave bodies."

"No."

"Then I do not see it."

"You are not meant to see it yet." Borren looked down at him with the one eye. "You had the six at the bridge before I did. You will have this before I want you to, and when you have it you will come and tell me, in order, short, and I will tell you which parts of it are wrong. That is what you are for on this. Do you understand me?"

"Yes."

"Then there is one more thing, and I am saying it to you because you are the one who counts." He crouched, so that his face was level with the boy's, which he had never done. "I did not learn that the war is coming through that province from a soldier. Nobody in that province knows it yet. I know it because a thing was written down in the winter by a man who knew, and I had it in my hand for an hour before the man it was written to did. Do not ask me who. Do not ask me how I came to be holding it. Work out for yourself what it means that I was."

He stood, and went, and Kael sat by the fire with that.

A crew that misplaced documents for people who paid had been paid to misplace a thing that said where a war would be, and had read it first. That was one sum. A crew that was never told what a drop held had a leader who read the drops. That was another. He did not know how to make them come out to the same number, and he had a feeling, which he did not like and could not price, that the man had just told him something true in order to stop him looking for something else.

He got up and went to see to the mules. Somebody had already done it.

---

"Nine months," Aeliana said.

It was the same tent and a different dark, warm this time, with the door of it open to the summer and Valeria lying across the opening with the sky behind her.

"Nine months, and not a word, and not a sign, and not a rumour in a town that we did not go into and hear." She was lying on her back. He could hear her hands moving on the blanket, folding a corner of it and unfolding it. "I have been counting too. Not like you. I count what I have not heard. I have not heard that a princess of the empire was found dead in the south. I have not heard that a woman with a bound child was taken on the road. I have not heard that the First Master of the Temple is anywhere. I have listened in nine months of kitchens and I have heard nothing, and I have decided that nothing is a good sound, and I would like you to tell me that I am right."

"I cannot."

"I know you cannot. I would like you to anyway."

He lay with that. It was a thing she had never asked of him: to say a thing that was not true because it would help. His mother had asked it of him once, on a rock, and he had refused her, and she had held him and said nothing, and that was the one time.

"It is a good sound," he said.

"Thank you."

"It is also the sound there would be if they were dead. Nobody writes that down either."

"I know." She had stopped folding the blanket. "I am not a fool. I am the Emperor's daughter and I have never once been told a thing I did not want to hear, and I know exactly what that has made me, and I still want to hear it. Do you hope, Kael? I have watched you for nine months and I cannot tell."

He looked at the roof of the tent, which he could not see.

"I count," he said.

"That is not what I asked."

"I know." The sum had three ends and he had had them since a room over a mule yard. They were at Solvenna, or they were on the road to it, or they were somewhere no road went. He had never said the third one aloud in front of her. He did not say it now. "There is a sum with three ends and I cannot choose between them, and I have stopped trying to, because there is nothing new to put into it. That is what I do instead of hoping. I do not know if it is the same thing."

"It is not."

"No."

"Then try the other."

He did not know how. He lay there and tried, in the dark, and it was like trying to reach a thing on a shelf he could not see the height of. What came was not a feeling. What came was his brother's hand shutting on his finger and staying shut, and the weight of him, which was the weight of a lamb, and then it was a thing he had never thought before and did not know where it had come from: that a child of that age takes milk from somebody, and that somebody had been holding him every day for nine months, or nobody had.

"I hope my brother is alive," he said, "and I hope somebody is feeding him."

Nobody said anything.

"That is the whole of it," he said. "That is as far as it goes."

"It is far enough," Aeliana said, and her voice had gone somewhere he had not heard it go, and then it came back. "Valeria."

"Highness."

"I have told you."

"Yes."

"Do you hope?"

Valeria did not answer for long enough that he thought she would not.

"I was taught to be early or to be late," she said. "I was never taught what to do in the middle. I have been in the middle for nine months and I have found that I am not good at it." A pause. "I hope they are there. I do not know how to do more than that. If they are there, I will have been late, and I will bear it. If they are not, I will have been early, and I will wait. Those are the two things I know how to do."

"And if they never come?"

"Then I will have been early for a very long time."

The princess made a sound that was not a laugh and was not the other thing. Kael lay between them and had nothing to add to it, and did not try.

---

"It is my father's," Aeliana said.

He had known this was coming since the fire, and had not known which of them would say it.

"The coin. Six years of a province. It is not the governor's, it is not the province's, it is my father's, and I sat at that fire tonight and heard a man say he would take it and I did not move." Her hands had started on the blanket again. "I was waiting to see whether I would. I did not. Valeria did not."

"No," Valeria said.

"You are of the Line. It is the Emperor's coin."

"You are the Emperor's daughter and you were sitting at the fire," Valeria said. "I have told you what my measure is. It has not changed."

"And if I had stood up?"

"Then I would have stood up with you and we would have been two women walking out of a camp with six mules between us and the river, and the boy would have had to choose, and I would have been interested to see which way." She said it without any weight at all. "You did not stand up."

"No. I did not." Aeliana was quiet. "I have been trying, since the fire, to make it come out. It is his. It was paid to him by people who did not have it to pay, and I know that because I have sat at a fire with them, and I have never once in my life had to think about where a coin was before it was on my table. It is his and it is wrong to take it. It is his and it was taken. I cannot make those into one thing." She turned over. "I have ruled on everything. Since I could talk. I ruled on the road, in that room, and you all went. And I cannot rule on this and I am going to carry the water anyway, and I do not know what that makes me, and I would like one of you to tell me and I know you will not."

"You are the one who asked whether it was wrong," Kael said. "In the autumn. Nobody else at that fire has asked. Not once. Not in nine months."

"Is that an answer?"

"No. I do not have one. It is a thing I have." He turned his head toward her voice. "I have watched fifteen people who were robbed by your father's empire take a little of it back, one wagon at a time, for nine months, and I have helped them, and nothing in me has said a word about it either way. You asked. I would rather be in a tent with somebody who asked."

She did not say anything to that for a while.

"When we walk into Solvenna," she said.

"Yes."

"I will have to be her. There is no other way in. A princess of the empire walks into the first city of the old borders and says her name and her father's machine does the rest, and then the machine has me again, and Adah is a girl who carried water for a summer and is not anywhere." She said it very steadily. "I have been trying to decide whether I want that. I have decided that I have never been asked what I want. I have been asked what I will have. Those are different questions and I did not know it until this year, and I do not have an answer to the first one, and I am fifteen years old."

"You will be what you are," Valeria said.

"So will you."

"Yes."

"And you do not mind."

"I do not know what minding would be for."

Aeliana laughed, then, properly, and it went out through the open door of the tent into the camp and somebody at the fire turned their head, and she put her hand over her mouth and laughed behind it, and it was a long time before she stopped.

"And you," she said, when she had. "Who walks into Solvenna? Lucan Drusane is on a letter. The Ghost is on a bed sheet on an island that is not there. Kael is at a fire, with nothing after it. Which one goes through the gate?"

He thought about it honestly, because she had.

"I do not know," he said.

"That is not like you."

"It is exactly like me. I have never known. I have wanted to see over the sea since I was four years old and I could not have told you why then and I cannot tell you now." He stopped, and found there was more, and let it come. "I know who I am walking toward. I know their names and I have counted them every day for nine months. I do not know who is doing the walking. I thought I would find out on the road. I have not. Perhaps you find out at the gate. Perhaps you find out when the people you are walking toward look at you and tell you who arrived."

"And if they are not there to tell you?"

"Then I will find out some other way," he said, "and I would rather not."

The fire outside had gone down to nothing. Valeria's shape lay across the door with the stars behind it and had not moved the whole time, and he knew she had heard every word and would never repeat one of them, and that if anybody ever asked her what was said in that tent she would say that nothing was.

"Kael," Aeliana said.

"Yes."

"Thank you for hoping. I know it cost you."

He did not answer that. He lay with his shut hand on his chest and felt it hold nothing, and the summer came in through the door, and after a while the princess slept, and then he did.
