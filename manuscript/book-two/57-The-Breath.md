# Chapter Fifty-Seven — The Breath

The shutter was shut.

He saw it from the lane with the wood on his shoulder and had the whole afternoon go over in his head in the time it took to take a step. Two pieces at the bottom of a fire in a closed room, the room stays closed, eighteen men breathe it and do not wake. That was the other side of the margin. That was the side he was not allowed to be on.

He went in through the yard gate bored at the soldier, the soldier bored back, and crossed with his eyes on the ground and had it: three wagons under the shed roof, wheels on all three now, greased, canvas folded on the seats. Ready. The empire was ready to walk. He had four days. The empire had a made-up mind.

The long room took him with the wood. Eleven men. Three asleep. The fire low and red. He knelt at the hearth and stacked, piece by piece. The two that were not wood went in at the bottom, under the others, where the coals were hottest and would take slow; his hands did not stop over them. Then he stood, and there was the shutter, high on the yard wall, closed against the cold.

He could not open it and stay a boy stacking wood.

"That'll smoke," he said, in the Koine with the town in it, to nobody, to the room. "Green in it." He coughed, once, not much. A soldier at the table looked up. Kael went to the window the way a boy goes who has decided a thing is his fault, and put his hand up to the shutter and worked it, and it came open a hand and stuck. "There," he said. "It'll clear."

"Leave it," the soldier said. "It's cold."

"It's your eyes." He shrugged, and left it, open a hand, and picked up the empty cloth the wood had been in. Nobody closed it. A shutter open a hand on a cold evening is a thing a soldier means to get to and does not, because a boy has just made it his idea to leave it, and a man does not get up to undo a boy.

He went out across the yard and did not look at the shutter and had it in the count anyway, open, one hand.

---

The bell went four.

The change came out of the long room, six, and six went in. The two on the square were relieved, and the two on the lane. Kael was at the well in the square with a cup, drinking, a boy who had carried wood. He watched the six who came off the doors go into the long room where a fire was taking two pieces of his mother's herb at the bottom of it, and the door shut behind them, and he counted the room again from the outside. Eighteen, now. The evening ones. Two hours of a shut room and a fire and a shutter open one hand.

He put the cup back on its hook.

Across the square a woman shook a cloth from a window. Two men stood with a horse between them. The bell-tower threw its shadow the length of the square and the leaves hung dark and full on the trees, and nobody in the town looked at the boy at the well, and the boy at the well had just started the clock on all of it.

He walked out by the east gate slowly, and went up the cut, and told Borren the shutter was open a hand.

"Then it is tonight," Borren said.

---

At full dark he lay on the lip of the cut with the count on three things and could not spend it on any of them.

The chimney of the long room. He watched the smoke off it, which he could not really see, and told himself he could tell it had changed, and knew he was lying, because smoke is smoke in the dark and the herb did not colour it. Under the smoke, the room. Eighteen men going slowly down into it and not knowing, or eighteen men wide awake and one of them saying to another that his head hurt. He had no way to know which. He had put a thing into a room and walked away from it, and now the room was two hours of not knowing with a wall around it.

And the lane gate.

Every night since the first, a man had come out of the lane gate an hour into the dark and gone twenty strides down the lane to the wall of a garden and pissed against it and gone back in. One man. Off the count of the six, out of the yard, alone, in the dark, for as long as it takes. Kael had him every night. Tonight he would come out into a plan.

"He is the one I cannot see how to do," Kael had said, on the slope. "He is outside the room. The room is asleep and he is standing in a lane with his back to a wall."

"Then he is yours," Borren had said. "You wanted to know what you were for tonight. You are for the man in the lane and the man at the bottom of the stair. Nobody else."

He lay on the lip and watched the yard and the count ran and there was nothing to do with it but hold it, and holding it was the worst thing there was, and then the small door of the lane gate opened and a shape came out of it, and the waiting was over, which was better.

---

Nine of them went down into the town in the dark, not together.

White and blue on seven. Borren in an officer's coat, the eye-patch a dark oval under an officer's cap, and something in the way he wore it that Kael had never seen on him, a man used to being obeyed by men he did not know. Ambros buttoned to the throat, the blue at his collar sitting exactly, a man of the capital who had come a long way and did not think much of the road. The talker. The warehouse man with the wax against his chest. Oran. Two more. Tirzah in her own clothes with the basket, off to one side, because the basket did not go in at the gate, the basket waited near it.

Kael went first and alone, along the lane, a boy in the dark on an errand, and the man from the lane gate was against the garden wall twenty strides down with his back to the yard and his hands busy.

He did not fight him.

He came up out of the dark on the man's blind side, all at once, off where the thing was not looking, the way his father had taught him, and he had the man's own knife out of the man's own belt before the man had finished turning, and the point of it under the ear where the man could feel exactly what it was.

"The officer wants you," Kael said, in the Koine, bored, a boy sent to fetch. "In the room. Now. Don't do your belt up, he said now."

It was a stupid thing to say and it was the right stupid thing, because a man with a knife under his ear and his breeches open does not think, he obeys, and the two sentences did not agree with each other, which was why they worked. The man went back up the lane ahead of him with the knife at his kidney, in through the small gate, across the yard. Kael opened the door of the long room and put him into it: the dark, the low fire, the eighteen asleep on the floor, the smell. The man had one half-second to understand the shape of it, and in that half-second Talo took him.

Nineteen in the room, and none of them a sound.

---

The gate opened to the empire's colours.

Kael was not at the gate. He was already through it, along the wall in the dark of the yard, but he heard it, because a plan is a thing you hear when you cannot see it: Ambros's voice in the capital's Koine, level, tired, faintly put upon, and the two men on the lane gate going from bored to straight-backed in the space of a sentence. The advance. A day early, which an advance is. The paper. The seal. A pause while a man who could read read a thing written to be read, and then the gate, and boots on the yard stones, unhurried, the boots of men who had every right to be there.

Then the officer came out of the tax-house door with a lamp, and his left hand was empty.

Kael watched him from the dark along the wall. The man crossed the yard to Ambros and Borren and held the lamp up and looked at their faces, one and then the other, and took the paper Ambros held out and read it, and Kael's whole body was the count. The man read it twice. Then he folded it, and put it inside his own coat, and did not give it back.

That was wrong.

Kael had it and could not say what it was. A man reads an order and hands it back; the order is not his, it is the escort's, it is the paper the escort carries down the road to the next post. This man kept it. He filed the wrongness the way he filed everything, in the place where the whys went that he could not open, and held still, because there was nothing to do with it, and the officer was already talking.

"You are early," the officer said.

"We are early," Ambros said, in the voice, as though early were a thing lesser men did to him. "There is a war four days west of your wall, Commander. I would rather be early to a thing than late to it. My people will want to see the chests tonight and count them, and I will want to sleep in a bed that is not a wagon, and in the morning the escort proper comes to your gate and we are gone before the town is up and you have your yard back. You have had this coin under your floor for six years. You will not miss the last night of it."

The officer looked at him a moment.

"You have come with nine men," he said.

"I have come with nine men and an order with the Chancellery's seal on it. The forty are a day behind me on the road, eating your province's bread. Would you like to send a rider to check the seal? Solvenna is five months that way." Ambros let it sit. "Or would you like your yard back."

---

"I will stand your doors tonight," Ambros said.

It was the thing Kael had not seen coming and Borren had, and it was the whole of the trade in one sentence, and Kael stood against the wall in the dark and watched a man hand over his own house because a tired man in a good coat offered to carry a weight for him.

"My men have slept in a wagon for a week," Ambros said. "They will stand a wall tonight and be glad of the ground under them. Send your watch in. Let them sleep in a bed for the last night they will have this coin to guard. In the morning it is not your problem any more." He said it kindly, which was the cruelest way to say it. "You have earned a night off the wall, Commander. Take it."

The officer took it.

He would tell himself, if he ever thought about it again, that he had thought about it, but he did not think about it, because a man does not examine a gift that lands in the exact shape of the thing he has wanted for a month, which was to be rid of it. He gave an order. The six on the doors came off the doors. Six men who had been going to stand a wall all night in the cold walked across the yard toward the warmth of the long room and the beds in it, glad, off duty, and Kael counted them in through the door, one, two, three, four, five, six, into a room where nineteen men lay on the floor in the dark not breathing loud enough to hear, and the door shut behind them.

Twenty-four in the room now. Only six of them still had the wit to lie down on purpose.

Nobody in that yard knew what had just happened except the boy against the wall, and the boy against the wall had a moment, just one, where the size of it went through him: that this was the thing, that a whole building full of armed men had just been walked into the ground by fifteen people with a needle and a wax key and a herb, that his mother's hand on a mountain and his father's blind-side and a woman's basket and a steward's voice had all been made for exactly this and he was standing in the middle of it. Then it passed, because there was a stair, and the man who could burn the building was walking toward it with a lamp.

---

Down.

The stair was stone and turned once and the lamp threw their shadows huge on the wall. The officer first, with the key on his belt and the lamp in his right hand. Ambros at his shoulder. Behind them the warehouse man, who was going to count, with nothing in his hands that a man would look at twice. Kael last, with the second lamp, a boy to hold a light, his left hand holding it because the right had to be free and nobody in that stairwell knew why a lamp-boy would want a hand free. Borren did not come down. Borren was in the yard with the doors, being the officer of an escort, because a plan is a thing that has to keep looking like itself in two places at once.

Four of them, and a wall of stone, and one door.

It was iron, set in stone, with two locks a hand apart, and the officer put the lamp on a bracket and took the key off his belt and put it in the top lock and turned it, and the bolt went back with a sound like a cough. His own key. His own hand. Ambros made an approving sound, the sound of a man of the capital watching a thing be done correctly.

The bottom lock was the tax-master's, and the tax-master was asleep in a bed across the town, and the warehouse man had spent three months and a block of wax on the key that was going to open it, and he stepped up now with the key in his hand and put it in the lock and turned it.

It did not turn.

---

It went a third of the way and stopped.

Kael saw the man's face not change, which was the tell, because a face that does not change is a face working. The warehouse man drew the key out a hair and pushed it back and turned again, gently, feeling for the ward, and it went the third of the way and stopped, and the officer, who was a hand away with a lamp, said, "Is there a difficulty."

"It sticks," the warehouse man said, in the Koine, without a country in it, mild. "Your tax-master keeps a dirty lock, Commander. They all do." He worked it. "A moment."

Kael held the lamp and held the count and did the arithmetic he did not want to do. The change was at first light. First light was, he had it exactly, and it was hours off yet, but hours were not the thing. The thing was that a wax key does not fail slowly. It either turns or it is wrong, and a wrong key gets more wrong the more you work it, because the wax gives, and the man had one key and no second try, and behind them up the stair was a yard held by six people pretending to be an escort, and a town that would wake, and eight chests short of everything, no chests at all, if this door did not open.

"How long has it been sticking," the officer said. To Kael. Because Kael was a local boy with a lamp, and local boys know the building.

Kael had not been asked a thing and now he had been asked a thing, in the empire's tongue, by a man with an empty left hand a foot from his face, and the answer had to be a boy's answer and it had to be true enough to survive the next question and the one after it.

"Since the winter," he said, bored, a boy who had carried wood into this yard for a month. "The master swears at it every quarter-day. He says the cold gets in the works." He shrugged, one shoulder, the lamp not moving. "He kicks it."

The officer looked at him a half-second longer than he needed to.

Then the warehouse man said, "Ah," very softly, the way a man says it when a thing gives, and the key went the rest of the way, and the bottom bolt drew back.

---

The door came open on the dark and the officer took the lamp off the bracket and stepped into it, into a stone room stacked with the Emperor's coin, and that was where he had to stop being what he was, and he did not know it, and he had one breath left in which he was still the most dangerous thing in the building.

He never spent it.

Borren was not in the yard. Borren had come down the stair behind Kael on feet that made no sound at all, a whole man of him that Kael had not heard arrive, and as the officer stepped through the door Borren's forearm went across the man's throat from behind and his other hand took the wrist of the empty left hand, the Art hand, and pinned it to the man's own chest, and the warehouse man had the right wrist, and the two of them bent him back over nothing with his feet coming off the floor.

The officer's left hand opened against his own chest to spend a thread into the men holding him.

And Kael put the iron on it.

He had it out of the front of his shirt already, the band open in his good hand. He closed it over the wrist and the black in the grooves, one-handed, getting the weight where he wanted it before he asked the hand for anything. The band shut. The pin was already through. He leaned the whole of himself on it, and it seated.

The officer's face.

Kael was a foot away with the lamp and he watched it happen, watched a man reach inside himself for the thing that had always been there, the thing he had been raised to keep a hand free for, and find nothing. Find a hand. Find the world gone flat and ordinary and a stone room and three men and a boy and no answer anywhere in his own arm. Kael had felt that in a box east of Qeshet, the smallest thread he had getting no answer for the first time in his life. He had never watched it land on somebody else, and it landed all at once and from underneath, and the man made a sound that was not a word, and Borren's hand came off the throat and over the mouth, and the sound stayed inside it.

Kael looked for the thing that came.

The count came. Twenty-four in the room above, six of them awake an hour ago and not now. One on the stair. This one. Thirty-eight chests, and the arithmetic of two men to a chest and a stair with a turn in it. That came. The other thing did not, and he did not have the time to kneel in a stream and look for it, and he found that he was glad, and did not look at the gladness either.

"Iron," the warehouse man said, and Kael gave him the second band, and the officer's hands were behind him, and it was done, and nobody had died, and the empire's fire was a man on his knees in his own cellar breathing through his nose.

"Right," Borren said, quietly. "The chests. And keep your count up."

---

Two men to a chest, up a stair that turned once.

Kael counted them out. Thirty-eight chests in the room, small, iron-strapped, heavier than a thing that size had any business being. Six years of a province, going up a stair on the shoulders of thieves in the empire's colours. He stood at the turn with the lamp, counted, priced each one going past, held the whole of it against the dawn.

Thirty went.

Three wagons hold thirty, loaded right, and thirty was what three wagons held, and eight were left in the stone room because a wagon that breaks an axle eleven miles down a road at first light is worse than eight chests the Emperor gets to keep. Borren made the call without a word, a hand flat, no more, that meant stop. Kael stopped counting them up and started counting them down: out into the yard, onto the wagons, canvas over, roped, the mules backed into the traces in the dark by men who did it every day of their lives.

Above them the town slept. The tax-master slept in his bed. The eighteen and the six slept the sleep that had been chosen for them, and the two who had walked into it awake slept beside them now. In the long room a man breathed wrong, once, and the boy left to watch them dragged him by the collar to the door and the open shutter and the cold air, because the rule was the rule, and the man breathed, and stayed alive to guard a coin that was already on a wagon.

The bell in the tower went, some hour, and nobody counted it but Kael, and Kael did not need it, because he had the dawn already, and the dawn was coming.

---

Oran came off the wall.

He found Kael at the wagons in the last of the dark, and he did not run, because you do not run in a yard you are pretending to own, but he came fast, and low, and he said it in the Koine with the Avestan under it where the word would not come:

"Horses. On the west road. Coming."

Kael had it before the man finished. The escort. The real one. Early too, the way an advance is early, because everyone on this road was early, because a war made men early. Horses on the west road in the last of the dark meant the escort's own advance, nine men and an officer, coming to a town gate that was shut until first light, and they would not batter a town gate in the dark, they would camp outside it and wait for it to open, which was the one thing that saved them, and the one thing that damned them, because the gate opened at first light and so did the crew's wagons.

"How far," Kael said.

"Close. They will be at the gate before it is light."

"Then we go at light," Kael said. "With them. We are the advance. They are the advance. Two advances do not happen." He turned it over even as he said it, fast, the way you turn a thing over when there is no time to turn it over and you do it anyway. "They will stop outside a shut gate. We go out of it the moment it opens, three wagons, before they have got off their horses and stretched, and they will see the escort's advance leaving as they arrive, and it will be wrong, and it will take them a while to know how it is wrong, because a thing that is wrong in the right uniform takes a while."

"And if it does not take them a while."

"Then we are on the box of three wagons full of the Emperor's money in front of nine men on horses," Kael said, "and it takes them exactly no time at all. Tell Borren. Now."

---

First light came the way it comes, without asking.

The town gate opened at the first grey because a town gate opens at first grey, and three wagons rolled out through it, unhurried, the empire's colours on the seats, an officer riding at the head with a patch under his cap and a boy on the last box with the count running so hard it was the only thing in him.

Outside the gate, off the road, nine men and an officer stood with their horses in the grey, stiff from a cold night on the ground, watching the escort's advance leave with the coin.

The two officers looked at each other.

Borren lifted a hand off the rein, a hand's width, the greeting of a man who has been up all night and is glad to be going home. The other officer, cold, tired, a night on the ground behind him and a day of counting chests ahead, half-lifted his own hand back before his face had finished deciding whether the thing in front of him made sense.

That was the half-second. Kael had it. The distance between a hand going up because a hand went up, and a mind catching up to what the hand had done. It was the same half-second the two men on a bench had not spent at the rope-walk. It was the whole of the trade. The wagons rolled, and the officer's hand was up, and by the time his face had finished the wagons were past, and by the time his mouth opened the first wagon was turning off the road onto the track that went where the road did not, and a track is a slow thing to turn a wagon onto and a slow thing to follow a wagon down, and the last thing Kael saw of the west road was the man swinging up onto his horse, and by then it did not matter, because the ground had them, and the ground was the crew's.

He did not take the count off the nine until the track had bent twice and the road was gone.

---

The wagons came over the lip of the fold in the full light of the morning, and the camp was standing.

Not making itself. Standing. Packed and roped and on the line, the way it had been left, eighteen people in a fold of ground with nothing in it, waiting, and now nine of them coming back up out of the valley with three wagons and six years of a province, and the whole of it able to walk the moment the wheels were over the lip.

Nobody cheered. Kael came over the lip on the last box and saw the camp see the wagons, and saw what it did to them, which was not a sound. Selis stood up from the mules. Talo, who did not talk, put his hand flat on the empty blanket beside him and left it there. And Valeria was already moving, one half-step off the princess's shoulder as she had been for nine months and a lifetime, coming down toward the wagons to see the boy come off the box with his own eyes, because a report is a thing you get and this was a thing you saw.

The princess stood where she was. She had carried the water. The mules that were going to carry her father's coin the length of the world were watered and rested and in the traces, and she stood at the head of the line with her sleeves over her wrists and watched three wagons of it come over the lip, and Kael, getting down off the box with his shut hand held the way he held it, could not have said what was on her face, and did not try to.

"Borren," Tirzah said, from the line. She had gone up ahead somehow, the basket already on a mule. She was not looking at the wagons. She was looking east, back down the fold, at the way they had all come in.

Kael turned.

Nothing. The fold, the grass, the lip, the morning. He put the count on it anyway, because she had, and Tirzah asked the best questions in any room, and a woman who looks the wrong way when three wagons of money come in is a woman who has heard something the money is louder than.

"What," Borren said.

"I do not know yet," Tirzah said. "That is what I do not like about it."
