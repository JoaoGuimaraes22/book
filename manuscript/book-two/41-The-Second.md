# Chapter Forty-One — The Second

The door came open and the cold came in with it.

The light behind them was the grey that comes before the sun. It was full of the smoke of a camp awake a quarter of an hour, and it fell across the straw and stopped at Kael's knees.

Three of them, in the order they had used for sixty-two days.

The first stayed down on the ground outside with the bar in both hands and his head at the level of the floor. The second came up over the sill and in, bent under the roof, and went across without looking at anything, and got down on one knee at the ring behind Valeria Vel and put his hand on her rope and closed it. Kesir came last, with the cups.

Kael lay in the shape he had lain in all night. The empty band was over the ruin of his left hand and the eleven links lay in the straw where chain lies when it is doing nothing.

The man at the ring was watching Valeria. He had watched Valeria every morning since the first one.

Then the princess stood up.

She got her feet under her, and her hand went to the boards once and came off them, and she came up off the floor of that box with her back straight and her chin level, and she stood.

The man in the doorway said something in Ivrit that was not a word.

Kesir looked at the floor.

Kael went.

He came up off the straw and across in one movement and took the crouching man by the hair with his right hand and pulled the head back and round, and the throat came up under the jaw, and he put his teeth into it.

He had thought about the front of it. It was the side. He got the side, high, where the skin is thin over what runs there, and he bit and turned his head the way a dog turns its head, and it opened.

It was hot. It went over his face and down inside the neck of his shirt and it kept coming. Under it there was a small sound of a man trying to use a throat that would not do it any more.

The body went rigid and then it went everywhere at once. Kael let go of the hair. He was underneath most of a grown man and the arm he might have braced with was a bag of broken things at the end of his own wrist, and he did not try. He got his right hand down the man's side and found the belt and found the sheath and had the knife out of it and turned and put it into Valeria's two hands.

She had them up and open before it arrived.

---

Kesir was still looking at the floor when Kael came off the dying man and went low into him.

He got an arm round the ankle and his shoulder into the back of the knee and put all the weight he had left through the outside of it. Nine years of it. The joint went the way a joint goes when it is asked to bend somewhere it does not bend, and Kesir came down across the doorway and the cups went with him, and one of them struck the sill and rang.

The man on the ground outside had the bar up over his shoulder.

He was not looking at Kael. He had a fistful of the princess's sleeve in his left hand and he was holding on to it, and he had begun to shout, and the front of the shout was still the noise a man makes at a girl who has stood up when she has been told not to.

Valeria came off the wall with three feet of rope trailing.

She had cut it in one draw against the ring's own bracket. She had cut the ankles the same way while she was rising. She could not swing her arms apart further than the width of a hand, so she did not: she went in with both hands together in front of her, the knife point-down out of the bottom of her fists, and she went past the boy on the floor and up onto the sill and off it.

The bar came round and caught her across the ribs on her way past and she made no sound about it at all.

Then she was off the sill and out of the frame of the door and gone out of the light.

---

Kesir got up onto one hand.

He was making noise. It was not a shout. It was the sound of a man with a knee behind him and no air, coming up out of him in pieces, and it was going to become a shout in three or four seconds.

Kael got to him. He put the flat of his left hand over the mouth.

The pain went up the arm and into the shoulder and into the jaw and turned the box white at the edges. He did not take the hand off. The hand did not close, because it could not; it lay across the man's face, wet and open and useless, and Kesir got his teeth into it, and that was very nearly the end of the thing, and Kael put his forehead down on the back of his own wrist and leaned and held.

He got the rest of it in pieces, sideways, past his own arm, out through the open door.

The bar going up and away out of the top of the frame. A sound off it that was not wood on wood, and then the bar on the ground. Feet losing the frost, twice. A man's weight going down flat, which is its own sound and a short one. Then a great deal of small movement very close to the ground, and then not even that.

Nine. He had counted it without meaning to.

He was still holding the mouth when Valeria came back over the sill.

She did not ask him to move. She got the knife in under the arm, up, twice, and put her free hand flat on the man's chest and kept it there until it stopped, and then she took Kael's left wrist and lifted it away, and looked at what was on the palm of it, and put it down against his chest.

"Do not use it again," she said.

The camp made a sound like a camp. Somebody laughed at something a long way off. A mule was being argued with. Nobody came.

---

The princess was standing where the man had let go of her sleeve.

She had not moved. There was a great deal of blood on the outside of her arm and none of it was hers.

Valeria cut her rope, and her ankles, and then went past her without a word and out onto the ground.

Kael made himself do the next thing in the order he had put it in, four hundred times, on his back in the dark.

Boots. The man who had gone down in the doorway was nearest and his feet were nearest to Kael's own size, and Kael got them off him with one hand and his teeth in the laces and put them on. They were still warm inside. He did not look at that for very long.

Then the belts. Two knives, a small one and a good one. A skin, half full, off the man at the ring. A steel and a stub of char cloth in a leather bag on the same belt, and Kael took the whole bag. A coat off the doorway man, which was the biggest, and he put it over his arm.

The man at the ring had been the one who was kind about the mules.

Kael had that in his hands for about a second and put it down where he put things, and went on.

He got the water skin's strap over his own head. There was nothing else in that box worth the time.

He stood in the door and looked out at ground he could walk on. The air had nothing in it. No straw, no oil, no lantern, none of the other thing. It was only cold, and it went into him and hurt, and he took as much of it as he had time for.

Valeria was under the box on one knee looking along the length of the camp.

"Now," she said.

Kael went down off the sill. It was further than it had looked from inside and he had no hand to put down, and he went onto his side on the frost and got up.

The princess came down into Valeria's arms and could not stand when she was put on her feet, and stood anyway, holding a fistful of Valeria's sleeve the way the dead man had held hers.

Then the door, and the bar.

Kael had it out loud before he had decided to say it. "The bar. Put the bar on."

Valeria looked at him for a quarter of a second and then went and did it, and it took her two tries, and the second one seated it, and from ten strides away there was a black box with its door shut, which was what there had been every morning for two months.

She was coming back round the end of it when a man got up off the near fire and started toward them.

Kael took a fistful of the princess's sleeve and got her down into the dark under the box, in between the wheels, and Valeria came in against the boards on her other side with the knife flat along her forearm and stopped moving.

The man came all the way. He came round the corner of the box and stood with his back against the boards four feet from them and did what a man does at the side of a wagon at the beginning of a morning, and looked out at the light coming onto the hills while he did it. His feet were the width of a hand from the princess's.

She did not make a sound.

He finished, and said something to nobody in Ivrit about the cold, and went back to the fire.

Kael lay under the axle with his face turned into the frost and his hand held against his chest, and let three men's blood go cold on him, and did not move until Valeria did.

---

Behind the box there was nothing, and there had never been anything.

There was no reason to go behind it. The door was on the fire side. In sixty-two days Kael had never once heard a man's feet on the blind side of that box except when a wheel wanted looking at, and he had heard everything.

They went round the end of it, put it between themselves and the camp, and stopped in its shadow. Kael took the ground.

Frost, and a great deal of it, laid white and even over dust. It held a boot like wax. Away to the left the ground was churned to mud and refrozen where the animals had been walked, and it went out that way toward the water and the pickets and the men who would be at both.

To the right the ground rose and went to stone. Grey stone, coming through in shelves, running back up to the shoulder they had come down off yesterday. It took nothing at all.

It went uphill. It went back the way they had come. It went away from the water.

"Right," Kael said.

Valeria did not ask. She got down, put the princess over her shoulder and stood up with her, in irons, with a hand's width of chain between her wrists. Her ribs did something on the way up that showed in her face and nowhere else.

They went out of the shadow of the box and across eleven strides of frost. Kael went last and did what he could with his feet in the marks the other two had made, and then they were on the stone.

---

The camp did not stop.

He listened to it the whole way up. It was the third time in his life that his hearing had been the only thing standing between him and the end of everything, and he found that he was quite calm, and he did not look at that either.

A pot. Somebody putting wood on. Two men going past on the far side with a load. The mules. A voice he knew saying the same three words it said every morning about the near-side leader, and Kael thought, distinctly, *Rashim is going to be the one who opens it*, and put that down too.

The stone went up in shelves and each shelf was a place where a head coming over the top of it could be seen from below, and he took them one at a time, flat, and sent the other two up while he watched the camp, and then went up himself.

On the fourth shelf the princess's arm came off Valeria's back and hung, and Valeria stopped and got her back on and did not put her down.

Kael had the coat and the skin and could carry nothing else. He knew the exact size of that. He went up in front with his working hand on the rock, hauled, put a foot where the foot went, did it again. The boots were somebody else's and too big and he went over twice. The second time he put the left hand out to stop himself. He did not do it a third time.

The light came up along the road below them while they were on the fifth shelf and turned the whole of that country the colour it had been at Qeshet. There was no part of the ground they were lying on that could not be seen from the flat, by anybody down there with a reason to look up.

Nobody had a reason to look up.

On the sixth there was thorn, in a line, growing out of a seam where water had been at some season, and it was the first cover of any kind.

They went into it and lay down.

Below them the camp had spread itself out on the flat and put its smoke straight up in the cold: the box at the near end with its door shut, the fires, the picket line, the mules, the men, and the road going away east through the middle of all of it with the light coming along it. Fifty men. Kael counted the fires and got it again and it was still fifty.

Nothing was moving that was not moving for its own reasons.

He put his face down in the frost and let his breathing go.

---

They went for another hour before the sun properly came, and by then they were over the shoulder and the camp was a thing on the other side of a hill.

Kael stopped them in a fold of the stone where a slab had come away from the seam behind it, and got the princess down out of Valeria's arms, and sat her against the cold face of it.

Then he untied the cloth from behind her head.

It came away stiff. He worked the knot with his right hand and his teeth and got it off and put it in the fold of his elbow, and she brought her hands up in the iron and touched the corners of her own mouth, both sides, carefully, the way you touch a thing to find out how much of it there is.

Her mouth had gone at the corners. It was cracked into the flesh on both sides and it had been cracked for a long time and healed and cracked again.

She did not say anything.

Kael put the skin against her hands and she got it up and drank, and stopped, and drank again, and gave it back with about half of it gone and did not apologise for the amount, and he took it and gave it to Valeria.

Valeria drank two mouthfuls and stopped, and gave it to him, and he drank two and stopped, and it was almost nothing, and there was no water in front of them anywhere that he knew of.

He looked at his hand in the light.

It was the first he had seen of it. The back of it was not skin any more from the knuckles to the wrist. The thumb sat out at an angle that had nothing to do with a thumb. The whole of it had gone the colour of a plum and it was fat with something and it was hot enough that he could feel it against his own chest through his shirt, and there were the marks of Kesir's teeth across the palm.

He put it back inside the coat. He put the coat round the princess.

"How many," Valeria said.

He had done it on the way up, on the shelves, between one and the next.

"Not fifty." He got himself round to where he could see her. "He has been looking down the road behind him every afternoon for two months. He is not going to take fifty men off that road and send them the other way for us." He turned it once more and it came out the same. "Ten. Twelve. Mounted, and they will bring somebody who can read ground, and they will be behind us before the middle of the day."

"Twelve," Valeria said.

"Twelve."

She looked out at the country for a while. It went away in front of them in long grey backs, one behind another, with the thorn in the low places, and nothing on any of it that was moving.

Then she held her hands up in front of her, without any particular emphasis, and let him look at the iron, and put them down again.

Neither of them said anything about it.

Kael got his feet under him. There was a shelf above them that would show him the whole of the ground east and south, and the sun was going to be up on it in a few minutes, and after that they were going to have to be somewhere else.

"Lucan," the princess said.

It came out of her broken and about a third of the size of her voice, and it stopped him with one hand on the rock.

"I did not do what you told me," she said.

He looked at her. She was sitting in another man's coat with her hands in iron in her lap and her hair down her back with two months in it, and she was looking straight back at him, and she was waiting.

"No," Kael said. "You didn't."

He went up onto the shelf.
