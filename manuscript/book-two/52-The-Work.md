# Chapter Fifty-Two — The Work

The lamp burned itself out while they were talking, and nobody lit another.

The city was as quiet as that city got. A mule shifted against a rail somewhere under them, and further off a dog answered another dog and gave it up. Kael sat with his back against the door and did the sum out loud, in the dark, for the two people it belonged to.

"Qeshet is taken. So nobody we are looking for is in Qeshet. A taken city gets counted, street by street, and anybody in it who could walk would have walked. If any of them came out of that house and got loose, they moved."

"Moved where," Aeliana said.

"That is the question."

For a while nobody offered anything. The dark was good for this. Nobody had to do anything with their face.

"When a party is broken, you do not search for it," Valeria said. "You go where the party was going. It is the only place on earth that everybody in it knows. I was taught it before I was taught anything with an edge on it. If you are separated, you are not lost. You are early or you are late."

"And where our party was going was said out loud," Kael said. "At that table, with the eight of us sitting round it. You do not go to the capital from the south. You go to Solvenna, and from Solvenna to the capital."

"Also, there is the letter," Kael continued. "Every name at that table was written down for the capital."

"By the man who had us in a box before morning," Aeliana said. "I would not put weight on that letter. If my father's people have any of them, it reaches the capital without it. Everything they touch does."

"So, either they are already at Solvenna," Kael said, "or they are on the way to it, or they are somewhere no road goes. Two of those can be walked, and they are the same walk."

Nobody said anything about the third one.

---

"Now the rest of it," he said. "Thirteen months, for people who can use the road. We cannot use the road. We won't have enough coin for thirteen months, so we'd need to survive without its use, or find other ways, which would slow us down. And we can't trust anyone, not empire, or otherwise, until we finally reach Solvenna. There it's old empire borders, not susceptible to the coalition's manipulations, deeply loyal to the empire. People who'll help the you two undoubtedly."

"You have already built something," Aeliana said. "I have been listening to you build it. Say it."

"As far as we now, the south coast is in chaos. That means every road south and east of Solvenna must be filled with people we won't know if we can trust, until it's too late."

Kael looked at the princess' eyes.

"That old bandit guy I talked about earlier, said that fifteen of his people will leave this city tomorrow, going to Solvenna. They are not the empire's and they are not the coalition's. Moving things through a war without being looked at is their whole trade." He let that sit for a breath. "Nobody counts three children inside eighteen people."

"I feel as you are suggesting we join some thieves?" responded Aeliana.

"The *honest men*, they call them." Valeria said.

"These people are more than simple thieves Aeliana."

"The ones who blinded you twice rather than let you learn a walk," Valeria said. "You are talking about carrying the two of us up to the place they did not show you, in daylight, uninvited, the morning after their job. What I asked you at that door I am asking again. If they say no, do they let us walk out."

"Last time I did not know. This time I have heard the man's own policy. They do not leave bodies. A body is a hunt, and a hunt has a name in it by the third week."

"That was a rule about a man they need alive."

"It is a rule about what things cost. Everything I watched that man do was priced before he did it." Kael turned his wrist in the dark, where the band had sat. "If we go to where they are now, it tells him his blindfold failed, and a crew is not safe around people who know too much and bring nothing. That is true and I have counted it. It is also one fact with two readings, because the thing that makes us dangerous to him is the thing that makes us worth having."

Kael took a breath.

"Furthermore, what are, realistically, our options?"

"We go alone: thirteen months is the price for a man who can use the road. For three who cannot be seen, feeding ourselves around a war, trusting nobody and checking everything — I cannot put a top on it. Two years. More.

"We find the empire: a garrison, a loyal house, somebody in the right colours. The last imperial hand we took put us in a box, and with the next one we would not know until the door was shut. I have no way to test a loyalist from outside his walls. Nobody does.

"Or the honest men. I will not pretend I know the inside of their leader off two days. But what he wants is plain, his people come first, and he answers to nobody on either side of this war. Worst case, as I read him: he does not kill three children on his bank. He tells us to fend for ourselves, and we have spent a walk. And he is gone tomorrow. Of the three roads it is the cheapest in time, and it is the only one that is not there the day after."

"And if you have priced him wrong?"

"Then our lives will pay a heavy price." He looked at the princess. "But hers will always be the heaviest price to pay. So this decision, is one we make together."

---

The bed frame ticked as Aeliana sat back against the wall. When she spoke it was not quickly.

"Tell me again what he said about me. The words."

"He said: there were three of you, there are still three of you. He said he had found out most of why we cannot be seen and would do us the courtesy of not saying the rest of it out loud. Then he said, *get her off this river*."

"A man worked out what I am worth," she said, "and what he did with it was lower his voice." A cart went along somewhere beyond the yards and faded before she went on. "I have spent months now learning exactly how frightened to be of strangers who know things about me. I am telling you I know the size of that fear to the grain. Of him it is small."

"It is still a risk."

"Everything left to us is a risk. You have not yet let us down Kael." The frame ticked again. "And I can see in Valeria's eyes, she believes it is the right choice too. Whatever would be my ruling, I'm sure she'd find a way to convince me eventually."

"I would try, your Highness."

"Then I have ruled. We go."

They had until tomorrow, so they had today. Kael sat against the door a while longer, listening to the two of them breathe their way down into sleep, and then there was light in the shutter seams and he had not noticed the night go.

---

Zahir was under the long shed with a mule's near hind in his lap, and took the news of their going the way he took everything, at length and to nobody in particular. The road east was good this month, he said, and the road west was full of soldiers, and there had been a crowd at the bridge-house since first light. He said all of it with a hoof in his hand, in the voice of a man discussing weather.

Kael filed it.

They did not leave together. He went first with the sacking on his back, and the bag inside his coat where the fifth knife had ridden. The girls came after, spaced well behind, and they came as what they had decided in the room: two sisters on an errand, the older in front and not waiting, the younger carrying the water skin and looking at what there was to look at.

At the bottom of the stair Aeliana stopped for the length of one breath, as though the street were cold water and she was choosing how to enter it. Then she walked. Nine days in a room over a mule yard, and she walked down the middle of a foreign city with her sleeves pushed back, and the only person who looked at her twice was a woman selling onions, who looked because she stood too straight, and looked away again when she stopped.

Valeria did not once turn her head toward her. Kael, forty strides in front, could read what that not-turning cost off the set of her shoulders.

---

He had never seen a stride of the way, and he had the whole of it.

Eleven strides and a right. Forty-two and a right. Thirty on stone with water running in a channel under it. He had kept the turns and thrown the distances away, because the distances had been lies: the same three strides of loose board had come at three hundred and forty-one and again at five hundred and nine, and a walk that crosses its own board twice is a walk being made longer than it is. Under the sack it had all come apart into a shape. On his own feet, in daylight, the shape sat down onto the town and turned into streets.

They passed the end of the street that did not go anywhere. The bench outside the long low house was empty, and the door stood the way doors stand when nothing behind them is worth a bar.

Past the last houses the bank was boards over mud, and then the boards gave out and it was just the bank, with the river going by on their right, brown at the edge and shouldering along in the middle. Kael stopped where a runnel came down and let the girls close up, because from here there was nobody left to count them.

The wind came round off the water. There was tar in it, and hemp, and under those the wet-rope smell that is both of them together and neither.

"There," he said.

The building sat along the bank like something that had been laid down rather than built, longer than anything else on that river, open to the water on posts. Two boats rode against the posts, low, and a third stood off with a man leaning on a sweep in it, doing nothing, the way men do nothing when they are watching a bank.

---

A man came off the near end of the walk before they were within fifty strides, wiping his hands on a cloth that did not make them cleaner. He was of this city; the face said so, and the way he stood in the path without seeming to block it said more.

"You are on the wrong bank for whatever it is," he said in the Koine.

"I am not," Kael said. "I am here to see a man."

"There is no man here. There is rope."

"I have no name to ask for." There was no point pretending otherwise, so he said the true thing. "The man I sold a bridge-house to."

The cloth stopped moving.

Behind the man, down the length of the walk, the work began to stop. It did not stop all at once. It stopped one pair of hands at a time, the way frost comes up a window, until the wheel at the far end turned its last turn and squeaked and did not squeak again, and the whole long building stood there holding rope and looking at three children on a bank.

The man from the rope-walk came up the boards unhurried, a coil over one shoulder, and set the coil down on a post as if that had always been where it went.

"The last thing I said to you," he said, "was that you would not see any of us again."

"Yes."

"It was true when I said it." The one eye went along the three of them, and stopped on Kael. "Say, how did you find your way back here?"

Kael grinned.

"That's a secret. If you want to know more, it'll cost you."

There was a silence of a particular kind. These *games* somehow made him feel something he could not describe.

"I put six turns into that walk that it did not need," the man said.

"I counted eight."

He laughed then, once, the second time Kael had ever heard it, and picked the coil back up off the post as though it had answered a question for him.

"Come up off the bank," he said.

---

The small room off the walk had been a room full of paper. The paper was gone. The two seals were gone, and the third one, and the column of figures, and what was left was a table, two stools, and the stone that had weighted the piles sitting on bare wood.

The man from the rope-walk sat, and looked at the three of them standing, and did not offer anything.

"Say what you want."

"Passage to Solvenna," Kael said. "All three of us. Inside the crew, for the whole of the road."

"And what have you got."

"Me. You said I am useful for one thing. Between here and Solvenna, I am for whatever you point me at. Any work the road asks, any job, whatever needs doing. You have seen what my hands are worth and you have seen what my eyes are worth, and you had the second of those priced before I did." He kept his voice at the pace of a man reading a list. "Her, you have seen too. She stays with the third, in the camp, off the jobs. And if the road ever comes at your people, you have watched what stands up when that happens, because you cleared a room once to watch it."

"And the third."

"Stays with the camp. She is not part of any work and she never will be."

"That is what I get. Say the price."

"My share. Whatever the work brings, I do not want my part of it. Keep it. That is the price of the two of them staying back."

The man looked at him for a long moment.

"And her keep, and her keep, and yours. Bread three times a day for thirteen months for three people who by your own account bring me one pair of hands and a half." It was not said unkindly. It was said the way he said everything, as a figure already carried in a column. "Why would I put that under my boats."

"You told me to get her off this river," Kael said. "There is one set of people on it going the whole way to Solvenna who cannot be seen doing anything. I am looking at them."

"I did say that." He turned his head and looked out at the water for a moment, and came back. "I said it meaning a boat south, to a town nobody has heard of."

"She does not want a town nobody has heard of. Her road is the same as mine."

"And you," the man said, to Valeria, straight at her.

"I am with them," Valeria said.

He took that without anything moving in his face, and this time he did not look away from her while she said it.

"And you." To Aeliana, last. "Is it your own choice, to be standing on my bank."

"It is," she said. "Entirely."

---

The man from the rope-walk got up, moved the stone an inch to the left for no reason Kael could read, and sat back down.

"No," he said.

Kael did not move.

"Not to the shape of it. To the shape of it, no." He put one finger on the table. "You are not giving me your share. A man on a job who is owed nothing is a man with nothing under his feet, and nobody works next to that man twice. My people will know what you are paid, because that is how they will know what you are." The finger moved once, as though striking a line through something. "Their bread comes out of your share. What is left of it after their bread is yours, and you will take it, and if there is nothing left then you have been paid what the road was worth that month. That is the shape."

"Is that not the same shape?"

"It is not." He sat back. "Now the part you have not priced. Thirteen months of doing what you are told. By people you met three days ago, who will not explain themselves, on roads you will not be shown twice. In that room I watched a grown man say your sentence back your way because you were right. You are very good at being right. On my road, being right is not a rank."

"I know what obeying is. I was raised in it."

"You were raised in it, sure thing. This time, you will be doing it for strangers, with your family on the far side of every day I spend getting there slowly and safely and boringly, and there will be evenings when the fastest thing in you is certain I am wrong. I am telling you now, so that it has been said in this room: some of those evenings I will be wrong. You will do it anyway."

Kael heard Valeria not saying anything at all, which was its own sound.

"You know our one rule," the man said. "Nobody who knows how a thing is going to be done leaves until it is done. From first light the thing being done is eighteen people getting to Solvenna, and it is being done for thirteen months, and you will know the whole of it every day you are inside it. Do that sum."

"Nobody leaves," Kael said.

"Nobody leaves. Not at the first town, not at the worst one. There is no door in this that opens partway along." The one eye stayed on him. "You have spent three days deciding things and one night deciding this. You can have until I stand up."

"I do not need it," Kael said. "Done."

"Done," the man said, and stood up, and it was not until he was up that Kael understood that the standing was the signature.

---

At the door of the small room he stopped and turned back, and for a moment he looked at the three of them together, the way a man looks at a load he has agreed to carry and is now allowed to think about.

"You came up a bank to a place you were never shown," he said, "to ask for a man you had no name for. What did you ask for, at the end of the walk?"

"The man I sold a bridge-house to."

"That will not do for thirteen months."

He said the next thing the way he had said everything else, flat, in order, a figure off a sheet.

"Borren."

Kael took it and put it where he kept the numbers that mattered.

"It was given to me," the man said. "I did not choose it, which makes it rare in this company. Nobody outside this crew has ever had it. Work out for yourself what I have just told you."

Then he went out onto the walk, where the coils were going down into the second boat one at a time, and looked back at Kael, and gave him the first of it.

"There is rope to carry."
