# Chapter Sixty-One — The Inn

The war came through the province before the leaves were off, as the man had said it would, and it did not come through them.

They were on the tracks by then, west and a little south, six people and four mules and two wagons that were not going to see the end of it, and the war went by below them on the roads the way weather goes by in the next valley. A column, once, in the grey before a morning, going east the way they had come: Kael had it from a cut at four hundred strides and did the count on it without wanting to, and it came out at something over a thousand and a train behind, and none of the six said anything, and they did not move until it was gone. A town, once, that they had been going to go round anyway, with the smoke standing up off it straight in the still air for a whole day. He looked at it from a hill with Tirzah beside him, and she looked at it for a long time, and then she said, "Not that one, then," and named another for the morning, and that was the whole of what anybody said about it.

The leaves came off. The nights went cold in the way that means it, and then the days did.

It worked. That was the thing he had not expected, and he turned it over more than once in the dark and could not find where it should not have. Six people who had all been under one man for a year, and the man dead, and it went on making itself the way the camp had always made itself, one pair of hands at a time, nobody telling anybody anything. Talo dug the fires. Oran had the mules and had them so well that they came to him without being fetched. Tirzah named the town, the night before, once, and went into it with her basket and came out with bread and with the shape of the next three days, and nobody asked her how. Aeliana carried the water and did Selis's work in the traces and had begun to talk to Tirzah in Ivrit in the evenings, badly and without embarrassment, and Tirzah answered her in it and corrected nothing, which was not how Kael had been taught a language and was, he saw, how a person learned one. Valeria was where she was.

And Kael counted, and said what he had, in order, short, and nobody told him which parts were wrong.

That was the part he could not get used to. For a year there had been a man who took what Kael brought him and turned it over and handed it back with a piece of it changed, and Kael had hated that on the evenings the piece was the one he had been sure of and had come to need it the way you need a wall to put your back to, and now he brought the sum to the fire and gave it and Tirzah nodded and Oran nodded and it was done, and it was the sum, and if there was a place in it that was wrong nobody at that fire was going to find it before the road did. He had wanted to be the one deciding since he was nine years old. He had it now. It was worse.

He said so to Valeria, once, on a watch, because it was the kind of thing you could say to Valeria and have it go nowhere.

"You said that on the island," she said. "Not in those words."

"When."

"On a wall. About your father." She did not look round. "You said you had been taught to think and never once been allowed to decide, and that you would like to be, and I said you would not like it when it came. I do not think you heard me. You were looking at something else."

He had no memory of it. He had the count on every board of a storeroom floor and he did not have that. "What was I looking at."

"Me," Valeria said, and went on watching the dark.

---

The wagons died in the fourth week, one and then the other, on a stretch of track that went up more than the mules had left in them, and the six stood in the cold and looked at thirty chests with the Emperor's mark on them sitting on the ground in a wood, and Tirzah said, "Then we do it his way," and nobody asked which.

The water was two days off. She knew where. She had it off the board she carried in her head, a place on a river that was not on any road, where a man kept boats and had kept them for the old man for six years and had never once asked what went in them, and she walked in ahead of the rest of them with the basket and the seller's walk and came back with two hulls and a boy to bring them down, and the mules went to the man for the price of the boy, and that was the end of the mules. Oran stood with them a while before he let the boy take them. He did not say anything. Talo went and stood with him.

Thirty chests into two boats. The water took them where the tracks had not, faster and with nothing on it, down and then west, with the banks going by in the cold and the frost on the boards in the mornings, and the year turned on the water somewhere, because there came a morning when the light was a different light, low and long and coming from the wrong place, and Kael knew the season the way he knew everything, off the angle of a thing, and did not say so. He was fifteen, or near it. Nobody knew when he had been born but a woman who was not on that water and a man who was dead, and he did not know either, so it was not a thing anybody could have said.

Oran sang on the water. Once, in the sixth week, low, with the oars shipped and the boat going with the current, and it was not the song from the last fire, it was a different one, and Talo in the bow did not turn round and his mouth moved the whole way through it. Aeliana had her head down over the Ivrit she was working at with her lips and stopped working at it. Tirzah, in the stern, looked at the water going past. Nobody said anything when it finished, and Kael found that he had not been counting anything for the length of it, and put that down beside the other time, and did not take either out.

---

Solvenna was where the water went.

They saw it first from a bend, two days above it, in the last of an afternoon: the river opening out ahead of them into a width he had no number for, and on the far side of that width, low, in the cold haze, a line that was not the bank. Tirzah put a hand up and the boy leaned on the steering oar and they went in under trees and tied up and did not go on. The city was on the page in his head off a governor's mouth in a bed, and a dead man's mouth at a fire, and now it was a line in a haze, and none of the three were the same thing.

"Not in daylight," Tirzah said. "Not from the water. A city like that one watches its water." She looked at Kael. "You will want to see it first. Go and see it. Take the girl who does not fall over."

He went with Valeria, on foot, along the bank and up onto a rise where the trees stopped, and lay in the frost and looked at the first city of the old borders with the count running whether he wanted it or not.

It was not Ishareth. He had known it would not be and he had not known how. Ishareth had been a city that had been there before anybody, and had been walked round and out of, with the empire's one thing stuck to the side of it like a thing stuck to a wall. This had been put here. The wall went round it in one line with the corners where somebody had decided the corners went, and the wall was the colour of no ground he had walked on, pale, cut, carried from somewhere else, and inside it the roofs did not pile on each other the way roofs did where people had built as they needed to; they went in lines, and the lines went to a place, and at the place there was something with a dome on it that took the last of the light and held it after everything round it had gone dark. There was white and blue on the wall. Not one band of it on one man. It was on the wall, the way it had been on the tax-house, the way it had been on the twelve who had died on a mountain for the girl lying in a boat two miles behind him, and it was on the gate, and the gate was three gates, one in the wall and two in the towers either side of it, and men went in and out of it in the last of the light in numbers he could count and did.

"Say it," Valeria said.

"It is theirs. All of it. Nobody was here before them and there is nothing in it that was not put there on purpose." He had the river gate too, downstream, with the boats going in under a chain that went up for them and came down after. "The water gate has a chain. The land gate has the list. There are more men on this wall than on the whole of Ishareth's, and they are not standing about."

"And."

"And it does not matter, for us. We are three people on a road with nothing on us. It does not matter what a gate is looking for if you are not it."

"You said that at Ishareth."

"I was right at Ishareth."

Valeria was quiet for a while. The dome gave up the light.

"It is going to matter for her," she said.

"Yes."

"When."

"When she says so. Not before." He looked at the gate a while longer. "She is going to walk in through that as nobody. I would put money on it."

"You have not got any money."

"I have a chest of it in a hole in the ground, in a week."

"That is not yours."

"No," Kael said. "It is not."

---

They put it in the ground on the third night, above the city, in a wood on the far side of the river from the road, where the ground went up steep enough that nobody would ever have a reason to farm it, and the digging was Valeria's, and it was the worst thing he had ever watched her do.

She had told him what it would cost. She had said it flat, the night before, as a fact about the work: *Thirty chests is three holes. Three holes properly is two nights, and I will not be any use to anybody the day between.* And she put both hands on the frozen ground in the dark and the grass went, and the earth went down and sideways with no sound, packing itself into the sides, the way sand goes when the bottom is pulled out of it, and it took an hour for the first, and she sat down after it with her back against a tree and her hands in her lap and did not speak, and Aeliana held the lamp with its shutter down to a slit and did not speak either, and Tirzah and the twins carried the chests up from the water on their backs one at a time and put them in.

Ten in a hole. Then the second, and the sitting down was longer, and her face in the slit of the lamp was a face he had seen on an old man in a fold holding his own air against another man's, grey, wet, a young woman doing a young woman's work and the work being too big for one night anyway. He wanted to say stop. He did not say it, because they had thirty chests on a bank above the Emperor's own city and a boy with two boats who had been paid to forget and would not forget that.

"Say something," Valeria said, from the tree, with her eyes shut. "You are standing there having a thought."

"Do the third tomorrow."

"No."

"Then let me—"

"You cannot. You have said so yourself, on a mountain. You are two fingers wide." She opened her eyes. "The Emperor's daughter is holding a lamp so that a Vel can put her father's money in the ground where her father cannot get it. I have been thinking about that for an hour, sitting here, and I have decided it is the funniest thing I have ever done, and I am going to finish it. Move."

She did the third. She did not get up afterward, and Tirzah put her own cloak over her where she sat, and they filled the holes in with their hands, and Kael had every one of the thirty chests by its place and the place of every tree round it, and the angle off the dome when the dome came up out of the haze at first light, and there was nothing to mark it with and nothing needed to be.

---

They parted at the boats, before the light, the way the crew had done everything.

There was no speech in it. Tirzah had said the shape at a fire in a cut a season ago and the shape had held, and this was the door in it, and she opened it the way the old man had, by standing up. The twins had one of the boats loaded with what three people took out of thirty chests, which was less than the boy at the boats would have believed if he had been allowed to see it, and Tirzah had her basket, and the girls had the two sewn coats with the collars cut off them and what a woman could carry through a gate without being looked at twice, and Kael had what he had.

Tirzah looked at the three of them, one at a time, as the old man would have.

She did not say anything about the old man. She had said it once, at a fire, and she had said she would not say it again, and she did not. She put her hand flat on Aeliana's covered head, once, and took it away. She looked at Valeria, who looked back, and something went between them that Kael did not have and did not try for. Then she looked at him.

"You have the count on that ground," she said.

"Yes."

"Then it is yours. Nobody is coming back for it. That is the joke about us, still." She almost smiled. "Go and find your people, Kael. If they are there. If they are not, then you know what to do, because he taught you, and I watched him do it."

"I do not know what to do."

"No. You do not know that you know. Neither did I." She picked up the basket. "Do not come and find us. That is the rule and it is the only one I have left."

Oran came past him last, to the boat, and stopped, and said, in Avestan, low, the tongue he had had before he had any other, "Go well, brother of nobody."

And Kael said, in it, "Go well. Both of you," and watched the boy hear it, and watched him put it together, a summer and an autumn and a year of a face doing nothing at a fire while a valley boy reached for a word under his Koine and did not find it, and Oran opened his mouth and then shut it and then laughed, once, short, and shook his head, and got into the boat. Talo, who did not talk, looked at Kael for a long moment, and nodded, and took the oar.

The boats went up the water into the dark, and the sound of the oars went with them, and then that went too.

Three, on a bank.

He had the count on every one of the eighteen, still, in order, and he did it once more, standing there, and put the three living at the end of it, above the water, with the dead below, and then he made himself stop, because the girls were waiting and there was a gate.

---

They went in through the land gate in the middle of the morning, on the road, with a train.

It was the thing the old man had done with every gate he had ever met and the thing Kael had done at the first gate on this continent that had ever let him through, and he did it now for the last time, in the empire's own country, because there was no other way to do it and because it worked: the last halt before the walls, a train of forty animals with a man short-handed, the hoof named before anything was asked for, and the girls four animals back with their heads down. The road came in off the plain between fields that had been fields for a very long time, with the stones of them piled in straight walls, and the wall of the city came up out of the flat with the gate in it, and the gate was as he had had it from the rise: the list, and the men with it, and white and blue on all of them, and no face on that gate that was not the empire's.

He walked past the list at the head of forty animals and nobody looked at him, because nobody on any gate in the world looks at the boy at the head, and the count gave him the men on it and what each was doing and he let it, and then he was through, and under the arch, and out into a street that was straight.

That was the thing. He had known it and had not known how it would be. The street went away in front of him without one bend in it to a place he could see from where he stood, and the walls either side were cut stone all the way, pale, with the doors in them the same size as each other and the same distance apart, and the people in it were the empire's people, in the empire's cloth, going about the empire's morning, and there was more white and blue in the first hundred strides of it than he had seen in his whole life put together, and none of it was looking at him.

He stopped inside the gate with the train going past him, because the man had paid him to the gate and no further, and waited. Four animals. Then the girls, one behind the other, with their heads down, and the second one came level with him and did not stop and did not look at him, and said, low, in Ivrit, as she went past, "Nobody looked."

"No."

"I was Adah," Aeliana said, to the street ahead of her, in the flat priced voice she had off Valeria and did not know she had. "For one more gate. I decided that on the water. I wanted one more." A breath. "It is done now. The next door I go through in this city I go through as what I am. But that one was mine."

He had known it. He had put money that was not his on it. It was still a thing to hear said, in a straight street, by a girl with her head down, and he found he could not answer it and did not try.

Valeria came past a half-step behind her and did not turn her head.

---

He chose the inn the way the crew would have, and then found he had chosen it for a reason the crew would not have had.

He walked the three of them off the straight street into the ones behind it, where the city had been allowed to be a city, and it was there, in the third street, that he found what he was looking for without knowing until he saw it that he had been looking for it: a yard with a gate onto the street, a well, a long low house on two sides of the yard with rooms over, and a man in the gate with nothing to do who did not look at faces. It was the kind of place a train stopped at. It was the kind of place the crew had walked into in a hundred towns, where a stranger was a stranger and the price of the not-looking was in the price of the bread. He had it in four seconds off the man at the gate and the state of the well and the way the animals in the yard were tied.

And then he stood in the street a moment longer, with the girls behind him, and had the other reason, and turned it over, and it was this: that it was the place you went to if you were early, or late. If you had come to a city to wait for somebody and had no idea what door they would come through, you went to the place a stranger goes. You went where the party was going. Valeria had said it in the dark in a room over a mule yard, off her family's teaching, before she had been taught anything with an edge: *you go where the party was going.* And Kael, who had never once in his life gone where a party was going, had walked into the first city of the empire's old borders and gone straight to the one yard in it where a person who did not want to be seen would sit down and wait.

He did not say that. He did not know if it was a sum or a hope, and the two weighed the same, and it was cold.

"Here," he said.

The man at the gate wanted coin and a number, and Kael gave him both in the Koine with a town in it, and the man did not look at him, and they went in.

---

The room was at the end of the long house, up an outside stair, with one window that opened onto the yard and a door with a bar. He put the sacking down inside it, and Valeria walked the boards and put her hand flat on them and said, "Two below. Sleeping. One above, at the far end, not moving," and took her hand away. Aeliana sat down on the edge of the bed and took the scarf off, in a room with a barred door, and shook her head once so that the hair came down, and it did the thing it did, and she looked at the wall and said nothing.

Kael went to the window.

The yard was below him, with the well in the middle of it and the animals along one side, and the gate onto the street at the far end, and it was the middle of a cold morning, and there were people in it. A boy drawing water. Two men with a cart. The man at the gate, with nothing to do. He had the count on all of them without meaning to, because a window was a place you stood and counted, and it was a habit older than the box, and it ran, and he let it run, and it gave him the yard, and the yard was a yard.

Then a door opened in the long house across from him, on the ground, at the far end, and people came out of it.

He had them before he had them. That was the only way he could say it afterward, and he did not say it to anybody: the count gave him five bodies coming out of a door into a yard, and gave him the order they came in, and the distance between them, and what each one carried, and it had all of that before it had a face, because faces came last, they always had, it was the walk and the weight and where the hands went, and the walk of the first one out of that door was a walk he had grown up inside of. A woman, not tall, in the cloth of the country, with a child on her hip. An old man beside her, tall, gone thin, with a stoop that had not been there once and was there now as weight, walking crooked, the way a man walks who has been put back together more than once by somebody who knew how. Two behind: a young man with an arm he did not use, talking, steadily, to nobody, correct in every particular, Kael would have bet his life on it, and a small young woman beside him who was not listening, with a sword on her, in the empire's own city, and her head turning once across the yard the way a person's head turns who is counting the exits and has counted them already.

And the child. Kael had the child last, because the child was on her hip and was a shape, and the shape had a dark head, and across the eyes of it, tied behind, was a strip of cloth.

He had all of it. He had it whole, the way he had always had everything, and it went into him and stayed, and he stood at a window with his good hand on the sill and did nothing at all with any of it, because there was nothing in him that knew what a person did with this, and the part of him that would have known had been built by the woman crossing the yard below him, and she had not built that.

She was leaving. That was the next thing that came. They were walking toward the gate, all five of them, at a walk, in the middle of a morning, the way people leave a place they have decided to leave, and she had the child on her hip and the old man beside her and they were going to go out through that gate into a straight street, and he had counted them for two years, in the dark, every night, three ends to the sum and no way to choose, and the sum had come out, in a yard, and was walking away from him at a walk.

He did not decide anything.

He was on the stair. He did not have the door. He did not have the stair either, afterward, not one board of it, which was a thing he would think about for a long time, that the boy who had every board of a storeroom floor at six years old did not have the stair he came down on the one morning that mattered; he had the yard, and the cold, and the frost on the stones of it, and five people at the gate with their backs to him, and he did not run, because he had been taught at four years old that you do not run in a place where people can see you, and it was the last lesson she had ever taught him that still held.

He walked across the yard. He walked the way the crew walked, weight in the front of the foot, going nowhere because he was already there. The boy with the bucket looked at him and looked away. The man at the gate did not look at him at all.

He stopped ten strides off. He had a voice and he did not know where it was.

"Mother," he said.

It was not loud. It was the word in the Koine, because that was the tongue in his mouth, and it went across ten strides of a cold yard the way his father's orders had gone across a fold, through everything, and the woman with the child on her hip stopped walking.

She did not turn round. Not at once. He watched her not turn round, and he had the whole of what that was, because he had watched her be still his whole life and this was not that stillness, this was the other one, the one she had had at the door of a house on a mountain when a boy came home late, the stillness that is a person holding themselves together with both hands from the inside, and the old man beside her had stopped too, and had turned his head, and had seen the boy in the yard, and did not say anything, and put one hand on her shoulder, very lightly, and took it away.

Then she turned round.

*I have been asked, since, what she said to me, and what I said to her, and I have never been able to answer, because I do not think either of us said anything for a very long time. I know what her face did. I have it still. I have had it every night since, in the order it happened, and it has never once got smaller.*

She looked at him across ten strides. Her face was her face, the one that passed as village until she was still, and it was still now, and her hair had three marks of white in it that he had watched go in, and her eyes were her eyes, dark, hidden by will, and they went over him the way they had gone over him at every door he had ever come through, the whole of him, once, fast, the way the count went, and stopped on the left hand.

He saw them stop. He saw her have it. He had watched her price a thing a thousand times and had never once been the thing, and he was now, and she priced the shut hand at ten strides in the time it took him to breathe, and he saw the price arrive in her face and saw her not let it, and then she did let it, because the concealment was off her, it had been off her in a bed at Qeshet and it was off her here, and her face came apart, once, all of it, in a yard, in daylight, in front of a boy with a bucket, and she said his name.

"Kael."

Not a question. She had never in her life said his name as a question.

He did not remember crossing the ten strides. He had the yard in every detail and he did not have that. He had her hand on the side of his face, the herder's hand, hard, with the fingers going into his hair the way they had gone into it when he was four and had a fever, and he had the child between them on her hip, warm, heavy, a great deal heavier than a lamb, turning its bound face toward the sound of him, and he had his own good hand come up and take hold of the front of her cloth the way it had taken hold of a governor's, a fistful, and hold on, and his own face doing a thing he had not given it leave to do, and he had the sound. He made a sound this time. He did not know he had one. It came out of him into the front of her shoulder where he had put his face and it was not a word in any of the four languages she had given him, and she held the back of his head with her hand and let it come, and said nothing, because there was nothing in any of the four for it either.

"I have you," she said, at last, into his hair, in Avestan, the tongue she went slower in than anything. "I have you. I have you. I did not know. I did not know. I have you."

"You are alive."

"Yes."

"He is alive."

"Yes. He is alive. He is heavy, is what he is. Feel."

He felt. He put his good hand on the child's back, on the cloth of him, and the child was hot through it, and breathing, and the breathing made a shape that Kael had once counted on a mountain until it did, and it did now, and the child's hand came round from wherever it had been and found the hand on its back and shut on one finger of it, and held on, and did not let go.

"Somebody has been feeding him," Kael said, into her shoulder. It was not what he had meant to say. He had not meant to say anything.

"Yes," his mother said. "Somebody has."

---

The old man had come back across the yard while that happened, and had stood off, at three strides, with his hands folded in his sleeves and his crooked back to the gate and his dark eyes on the street beyond it, giving out nothing, the way he stood at everything. The two behind him had stopped where they were. Aurelian had stopped talking. That was how Kael knew, later, what it had been, because Aurelian had never once in four years stopped talking for anything that was not a wall.

Kael took his face out of his mother's shoulder. He did not let go of her cloth.

"First Master," he said.

"Kael." Severin looked at him, once, the whole of him, the way he looked at a page. His eyes stayed on the hand a fraction longer than on anything else and gave nothing about it. "You are late."

"Yes."

"By some months. I had it at eleven, from the south coast, for three people on foot who could not be seen. It is coming up on twenty." He tilted his head. "You will tell me the difference at some point, in order, and I will want the parts in between. Not today."

"No."

"Not today," the old man said again, and something in the courteous, unhurried face that gave out nothing gave out something, for the length of a breath, and was put away. "Your mother has been at a window for four months. I have been on a bench. Corvane has been talking. Sedrane has counted every door in this city twice, and there are a great many doors." He looked past Kael's shoulder. "And you did not come alone."

Kael turned his head.

They were in the yard. He had not heard them come down and he should have; he had the count on the stair he had come down himself and had not had one board of it, and he had not had two people come down it after him either, and there they were, six strides off, and had been for some time. Valeria, half a step off the princess's shoulder, in the place she stood. And the princess, with her head covered, in the road's brown, with her hands at her sides and the upright back that had come back to her in a box and never gone again, looking at the old man.

Severin looked at her. Then, in the middle of an inn yard in the first city of the empire's old borders, with a boy drawing water twenty strides off, he did not go to his knee, because a man of his years going to his knee in a yard is a thing people look at, and instead he inclined his head, exactly as far as a First Master inclines it to the blood, and held it there a beat longer than the form asked, and straightened.

"Highness," he said, quietly.

"First Master." Her voice was the ruling voice, and it did not shake, and he watched her make it not shake. "You are alive."

"So far."

"I am glad of it." She said it plainly, as a thing that was true. Then, because she was who she was, and could not help it, and had never once been contradicted before a boy on a boat had done it: "I have a great deal to say to you about that house. Not here."

"No, Highness. Not here." The old man's mouth did something at the corner that was very nearly the other thing. "You have been well, I think. You have gone brown."

"I have watered mules," the princess said. "For a summer. I was extremely good at it."

Behind the old man, Aurelian Corvane made a sound that was not a laugh and was going to be one in about a breath, and Elarine Sedrane put her hand flat on his good arm without looking at him and the sound stopped. She was looking at Valeria. She had been looking at Valeria since she came down the stair, and Valeria, who looked at nothing she had not decided to look at, had looked back, once, and looked away, and looked back.

"Val," Elarine said.

"Sedrane."

"You are thin."

"I have been walking."

"Yes. I can see that." Elarine's dark eyes went over her the way they went over everything, pricing before they blinked, and stopped, and the neat small face that was forgettable at a glance did a thing Kael had seen it do once, on a mountain, over a wound, and not since. "I am glad," she said, in a voice that had four years of *Sedrane* in it and was, for once, not billing anybody for them. "I did not think I was going to get to be. I had priced it at about one in three."

"You were wrong."

"Yes. I was wrong. It is the best I have ever been wrong." Elarine looked at Kael. She did not say anything to him. She looked at him for a while, at the hand, at the face, at whatever was on it, and she nodded, once, the way she had nodded across a Temple yard when a thing had been proved, and turned back to the old man.

Aurelian had not stopped looking at Kael. The arm he did not use was held the way a man holds a thing he has decided is all right and is not, and the tall body that had been built a year ahead of its age was thinner than it had been, and the face was the same face, dark gold, high, the face the bench had behaved differently in the radius of, and there was nothing in it now that Kael could have called doctrine.

"You look terrible," Aurelian said.

"Yes."

"I mean it. You look like a thing that has been dragged. What happened to your hand."

"A band."

"A—" Aurelian stopped. He looked at the hand. He was not a stupid boy; he had never been that; and he had been in a house with a barred door for a night once and knew what a band was and what it was for and what it did to a person's Art, and he looked at the hand a little longer and Kael watched him not ask, which was not a thing Aurelian Corvane had ever managed on the island, not once, not about anything. "Right," he said. "Later. You will tell it later. In order. I will want the whole of it." And then, because it was still him: "I have been in this city four months and I have been in every yard in it. I told them you would come to this one. I said, he will count the city and he will come to the yard where a person is not looked at, because that is what he does, he counts, and Sedrane said that was not a method, and I said it was the only method he has ever had, and here you are."

"Yes," Kael said. "Here I am."

"I was right."

"You were right."

"I would like that written down somewhere," Aurelian said, and his voice did a thing at the end of it that he covered by turning his head, and Elarine put her hand on his good arm again, and left it there.

---

They went in. Not to the far room; to the one the old man had, at the end of the long house, with a door that barred and a shutter that shut, and it was a small room and there were eight people in it, and Kael stood inside the door with his back against it, because that was where he stood, and had the count on eight.

Eight.

It came out whole. It was the first sum in two years that had, and he stood there and let it, and did not do anything with it, and did not need to.

His mother sat on the edge of the bed with the child on her knee and took the strip off its eyes.

She did it without looking at anybody, the way she did a thing that was hers, and the child blinked in the shuttered light and turned its head, and its eyes were red. Both of them. Not the red of a thing that has been hurt. The red of a thing that is red, the way a stone is grey, all the way through, with the light in them, and they went round the room the way a child's eyes go, at everything, and stopped on Kael, at the door, and stayed.

Nobody in the room was surprised by anything. He saw that. He had seen it on a mountain and he saw it here, the princess not moving, the old man not looking up, Elarine's eyes going once to the child and away as if it were a fact she already held. Only Aurelian, who had been in the house and not in the room, went still in a way that was new to him, and looked at the child, and then at Kael's mother, and then, carefully, at nothing.

"He knows you," his mother said. "He does not know how he knows. He was a month old. But he has stopped, and he does not stop for anybody but the old man. Come here."

He came. He crossed the room and sat on the floor in front of the bed, because it put him at the child's height, and the child looked at him with the red eyes and put out a hand and took hold of his face by the jaw, hard, with the whole hand, the way a child takes hold of a thing to find out what it is, and Kael let it, and did not move, and the child said something that was not a word in any language and was, he understood, about him.

"He talks," Kael said.

"Not yet. He is deciding which one to do it in." His mother's hand came down on the top of Kael's head and stayed there. "I have been giving him all four. Your master says it is too many. I have said that it was not too many for you."

"It was not."

"No." Her hand did not move. "Show me the other one."

He had known she would. He gave her the left hand, palm up, on her knee beside the child, and she looked at it in the shuttered light for a long time, and turned it over, and back, and put her thumb into the palm of it where the bite had gone through and pressed, and watched his face while she did it, and he did not let his face do anything, and she saw him not let it.

"Who did this."

"I did."

"With what."

"The other one."

She was quiet.

"It was a band," he said. "Painted. On a road, in a box, going east. The Art did not answer in it, and there were fifty men, and I needed one hand out, and there was only one way." He looked at his own hand in hers. "It holds a rope now. It holds a knife if the knife does not have to do anything clever. It came round on its own in the spring. I did not think it would do that. It does not do what the other one does, and I do not think it is going to."

"No," his mother said. "It is not." She did not soften it. She had never once softened anything. "It will do more than it does now. Not that. I can work on it and it will be better than it is, and you will have most of a hand, and it will not be the hand it was, and you will fight round it for the rest of your life." She closed her own hand over it. "You did the sum, and the sum came out at a hand. I taught you to do that. I did not think I would have to look at it."

"It came out," Kael said. "I am here."

"Yes." Her voice went, once, on the one word, and came back. "You are here. I have watched the road for four months, from a window, and I have watched it for two years before that from nowhere, from a bed, from a box the old man got me out of, from the back of a wagon going north with a child that could not be seen, and I did the sum every night the way you were doing it, I know you were doing it, I know exactly the sum you were doing, because I taught it to you and it has three ends, and I could not choose, and every night I chose the one I could bear and knew that I was choosing it." Her hand was on his head again. "There are no enemies. There are no allies. I said that to you in a bed and you looked at me as though I had said nothing. This. This is what I meant. This room. That is all there is and it is enough, and I have it, and I did not think I would."

Kael put his forehead down on her knee, beside the child, and stayed there, and the child put its hand in his hair, and pulled, and he let it, and the room was quiet, and nobody in it said anything, because there was nothing in any language for it, and they were all people who knew that.

Behind him, low, the princess said something to the old man, and the old man answered her, and it was about the house, and it could wait.

Valeria stood inside the door where he had stood, with her back to it, and looked at eight people in a room, and did not move, and did not say anything, and Kael, with his face on his mother's knee, did not see her; but he had the count on her, still, the way he had it on everything, and he had that she was there, and that she had put herself where she stood, and that she was not going anywhere, and that was the last thing he counted that day, and then he stopped.
