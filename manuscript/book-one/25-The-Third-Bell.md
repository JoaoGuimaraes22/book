# Chapter Twenty-Five — The Third Bell

The lamp was still burning when he came up the last of the hill.

He was three hours past *Evening* and he had known it since the cistern, and he came in through the door with the whole of it ready — where he had been, who with, what had been said, in the order she liked it — and stopped, because his mother was at the table with her hand flat on a sheet of paper and his father was sitting down.

His father did not sit down when he came in. In eleven years his father had not once been sitting when he came in.

"Sit," Neris said. "Eat first. You are grey."

"I ate at the Temple."

"You ate bread in a hole in the ground with three children and I would not give the smallest coin for any of it." She pushed the plate across the table with one finger, side-on in the chair, reaching with the one arm. "Eat, and then tell me all of it, and then I am going to stop asking you questions and you are going to go to bed."

He ate. It was the goat, and the last of the winter roots, and there was one lamp lit because the second one was for work.

Then he told them.

He told the bout first, because that was the order it had happened in. His father took him through the second column and the fourth without a word wasted.

"You went inside a column."

"Yes."

His father looked at him for a moment. "Good," he said, and nothing else.

Kael went on to the arm, and then to the corridor.

At the words *sends for you*, nothing happened in that room at all.

He knew what that meant. He had learned in the autumn exactly what it meant when a name landed on his parents and nothing came back off them, and he kept going, and gave it to them whole: the two men in grey, the stairs, the arms taken, the room, the food, the woman behind the chair, what the princess had said and what he had said back. He did not soften the part where he had corrected her. He did not soften the part where he had asked about the table, though he heard how it sounded coming out of his own mouth at his own table with one lamp on it.

His mother asked three questions.

Where were the guards standing when the door was open. Whether anyone had written anything down while he was in the room. And what, exactly, the woman behind the chair had been wearing.

He answered them. She listened the way she listened to birds.

And then she stopped.

Kael waited for the fourth question, and the fifth, and the sightline reconstruction and the order of it and *their words, not yours*.

His mother put her hand flat on the table.

"That is enough of that for tonight."

"There's more."

"There is always more. It will be there in the morning." She was not looking at him quite. "You have been up since dark and you have been cut and you fight at the third bell. It will keep."

Kael sat and could not work out what had just happened to him.

His father got up, went to the fire, came back with the pot, and poured. The smell of it came up between them. It was the mint she grew by the step, and he had drunk it after every hurt of his entire life.

"The judge of the games was at that arena," Neris said, to nobody. "There were four hundred of them. It is one island and there is one week left of it."

"Yes," said Vask.

"Then we will do the rest tomorrow."

"Yes."

She turned the cup round on the table with two fingers.

"He beat a house today," she said.

"He did."

"In front of all of them."

"Yes," said Vask.

That was the whole of it. Nobody looked at Kael while it was said and nobody said one word to him about it, and he sat in his own chair with his hands round a cup of mint and understood, without any of the equipment he would have needed to say it out loud, that his mother had asked three questions instead of thirty and that his father was sitting down.

"Your sibling is very close now," Neris said.

"How close?"

"Days. A week." She put her hand on the top of it, which she had never done in front of him, and left it there. "He has been sideways since the turn and last night he was not, so he has decided something."

"You keep saying he."

"I do," his mother agreed. "I have no evidence whatever. Do not write it down."

Kael laughed — properly, once, out of the middle of him — and his father made the small sound in his nose that was as far as his father went, and for about four seconds the three of them sat in a one-room house on a mountain with a lamp and a pot of mint between them, and that was all that was happening anywhere.

"Everything is by the door," Vask said, after a while.

Kael looked. Two bundles, canvas, roped. He had walked past them coming in and had counted them and had not let the count arrive anywhere.

"The books," said Neris. "The salt. The instruments. It has been ready since the second day of the games."

"And when it starts," Kael said, "the master comes."

"The master comes," said Neris.

Nobody in that room said anything else about it, because there was nothing else to say about it. It had been decided in the autumn and paid for over a winter and it was the one part of the whole arrangement that had never needed discussing.

Kael went up to the loft when he was told to, and slept the way he had slept before every important morning of his life, which was badly and then all at once.

---

They were both at the door.

That was the thing. His father was at the door because his father was always at the door, up before him, out at the block before there was anything to see by. His mother was at the door too, with a shawl round her and one hand on the frame and the whole weight of her carried forward, at an hour she had no business being out of a chair, on a stone step in a spring wind.

Neither of them said anything about that either.

His father checked the arm. He did it the way Ruthen had done it and a good deal faster, thumb well below the cut, one turn of the cloth tightened.

"It will open in the first two minutes and then it will stop," he said. "Do not look at it."

"No."

"Sedrane."

"Yes."

"You've said she has been reading you twice a week for two years and she was writing yesterday while everyone else was shouting." His father let go of the arm. "So she has got everything you did to the Drusane boy, and she is not going to give you any of it. What has she not got?"

Kael stood on the step in the cold with the mountain going grey behind the house and found that he had the answer and had had it since the cistern.

"Everything I've learned about fire," he said. "She taught me all of it herself."

His father looked at him for a moment.

"Go on, then," he said.

And Neris took Kael's face in both hands, which she had done perhaps eleven times in his life. She looked at it. She said nothing. Then she let go.

"Evening," said Vask.

"Evening," said Kael, and went down the hill.
