# Chapter Twenty-Eight — Turning Point

The road climbed.

It went up off the headland between two walls of dry stone, and it was full, and everything on it was going the same way they were. A man carrying a woman. Two men carrying a bench with somebody on it, which was slow, and which they kept doing. A girl of about ten with a fistful of another girl's sleeve, going at a speed the second girl could not do, so that the second girl came along behind her at an angle, half off her feet, and neither of them made any sound at all.

Kael went up it beside Valeria Vel with the fog closing behind them and the noise of the arena coming apart at their backs, and he did the only thing he knew how to do with a road, which was read it.

He did not like what it said.

The people thinned. That was the first of it. A road that is emptying from the front is emptying because something at the front is taking people off it.

Then the second thing, which was that nobody was coming back down.

"Soldiers ahead," he said. "Theirs."

"Yes."

"You haven't looked."

"No. You have." She had not slowed. "Say it when you have it. Not when you have finished checking it."

Four men came out of a side lane forty strides up, unhurried, with things in their hands that they had not brought with them.

They were not a formation. They had been in a house.

Valeria did not stop, did not speak, did not change her pace by any amount he could measure. Kael, who had spent hundreds of afternoons learning the exact contents of that girl's body, watched her put her weight down on the flagstone at the mouth of the lane, and felt the stone answer under his own boot from three strides away.

Then she was among them.

In the yard there had always been a shape to it — a beginning, a middle, a moment where you could see her decide. There was none of that.

She went in at a speed that had nothing to do with her legs.

The first one was still turning. She put the right-hand blade in under his jaw and out the other side of his neck, and what came out after the steel came out under pressure, and went up the dry-stone wall in a long arc, and then a shorter one, and then a shorter one, keeping time with something inside him that had not been told yet.

The second got his arm up, which was correct, and which is the only thing there is to do. The blade took the forearm off through both bones without changing speed and went on into his face.

The third was stepping back with his mouth open. She arrived at him instead of at where he had been, and put both blades in under the ribs on either side at once, and lifted, and a grown man came up off his heels with his boots off the road. Then she let go of him. He sat down in the ruts with the whole of himself in his lap and his hands out on either side of it, not touching it.

The fourth ran two strides. She took one. She put a blade through his back at the height of the heart and he ran three more before his legs were told.

That was the thing the yard had never shown him and had never once suggested. The first was on his knees with both hands up at his neck trying to hold it shut, and he was doing it wrong, and there was no right way to do it. The second was face down and still moving. The one in the ruts sat looking at his lap. And somewhere in it somebody was making a high sound with no words anywhere in it, over and over on one note, and Kael could not tell which of the four it was.

Valeria went back four strides and put a blade in at the base of the second one's skull, and the moving stopped, and she did not look at what she had done while she was doing it.

Three seconds. Perhaps four. The noise went on a good deal longer than that.

The smell came up off the road with the steam. It was iron and it was the inside of things and it was, underneath, a smell Kael had known since he was eight years old, because his mother had taught him what is under a hide on the table by the door with the light coming in, and a man opened all the way down is the same warm room as anything else opened all the way down. He had never once put those two facts beside each other. They went beside each other now and would not be separated again.

There was nothing in any of it that looked like skill. That was the part Kael could not get past afterward. Skill was what she did to him on the sea court, where every exchange had a joke in it somewhere and both of them were pretending the pretending was not there. This looked like a woman going down a row of beans.

His hands had come up. He noticed that they had come up. By the time they had, there was nothing anywhere on that road to put them on.

Valeria stepped over the second man, because he was in the way of where she was going, and reset her feet, and looked up the lane and then back down the road, in that order, and her breathing was not different.

Kael had watched thirty men do exactly that in a gateway an hour ago and had thought, standing on the sand with his hands empty, *it had a school.*

"Kael."

He looked at her.

"That was four," Valeria said. "There will be more of them and they will not always be four. When I go, come with me. Do not wait to be told which one is yours."

He had a great many things arriving at once and no order to put them in, and what came out of his mouth was the smallest of them.

"You didn't ask them anything."

Valeria Vel looked at him for about a second.

"No," she said.

---

They went up.

She set the pace and it was a hard one, and he matched it without effort and would have matched a harder one, and there was a piece of him that noticed he was pleased about that and filed the fact with a certain amount of distaste.

"Their colours," he said.

"You saw them."

"Green at the shoulder. A cord. Not all of them had it — one in three, one in four."

"Officers' men. Not officers." She said it the way she said the result of a thing she had checked twice. "That green is eastern. The dye comes out of the east and it is expensive and nobody west of the middle sea wastes it on a cord. Those are eastern troops."

"Coalition."

"Coalition is a word for eleven of them at once. Those are eastern." She went around a cart that was on its side with nothing under it. "It matters. Eastern means they came a long way and they did not come to hold anything, because you do not hold a thing at that distance. They came to do a job and go."

Kael turned it over.

For four years a fat man in ink-stained cuffs had taught the reduction of fortified towns to boys who would never reduce one, and Kael had read every book on that shelf because it was on that shelf. None of it had ever once been about anybody he could see.

"They speak the downhill tongue," he said.

Valeria's head came round.

"You understood them."

"All of it. They were saying where the left was and that they were behind."

She was quiet for four strides.

"Then you will walk on my left," she said, "and you will tell me every word you hear, and you will not wait until you have decided whether it is important."

That was the whole of it.

She did not ask him. She did not slow down and turn and put the question in the middle of the road where he would have had to stand in it — *and how does a boy off a mountain come to have the enemy's language in his mouth?* She took the fact, and she priced it, and she put it to work, and went on up the hill.

"Valeria."

"Say it."

"How far is it, from the wall to the mountain road."

The pause was not long. It was the length of a person deciding to answer the question that had been asked instead of the one underneath it.

"Four miles. Perhaps five, the way you would have to go now."

"And the wall. How long does it hold."

"I have no idea."

"You said days."

"I said it on the sand to a boy who was not standing in his own head, and I have not been given one new fact since." Valeria went up over the broken part of the road where the ruts crossed and did not offer him a hand and did not look to see whether he needed one. "Nobody was told this was coming. There is no plan for it. What I have is that it is cut stone, and it is high, and it stands on ground with nothing near it worth standing on, and every armed man left alive on this island is going to arrive at it before noon, because there is nowhere else on this island to arrive at."

"Your orders name it."

"My orders name it because somebody once sat in a room and wrote down the only defensible place on a small island, and then went to his dinner. That is the whole of what an order like that is. It does not know what day it is." She did not look round. "I know what you are doing. Stop."

"I'm not doing anything."

"You are working out whether I was right, and you cannot, because you do not have enough, and you are going to go on not having enough all the way up this hill." She said it flatly and without unkindness, which was worse. "I told you they would help you to the mountain and I said I was sure of it. I am not sure of it. I said it because you were standing on that sand and you were not there."

Kael said nothing.

*I'm sure of it* was not a fact. He had known that when she said it. He had known it the way he knew a claim was untested the moment it left anybody's mouth, because his mother had built the machine that knew it and had been running the machine on him since he was four years old, and he had heard the flaw in the sentence and had taken the sentence anyway.

He had wanted to be told. It was the only time in his life he could remember wanting to be told.

They came up onto the flat and the fog thinned and there was a lane going off to the right between two low walls, and at the mouth of it there had been a post.

---

He had seen these lights from the mountain every night of his life.

He had counted them in the dark from a doorway with his father's hand on the top of his head. He had come down past them on the lawful road twice a day for years, high above, within sight and sound, never through, and he had looked away from them so many thousands of times that looking away had stopped being a thing he did and had become a thing he was.

He was in them now.

The lane was narrow. It went down, then up. Smoke came along it at knee height and then lifted, and there was fire in three of the roofs on the left, one on the right; one of the three was going to take the next along the row, and nobody was going to stop it.

He saw a man sitting up against a doorframe with both hands in his lap, holding the place where his stomach was. Kael's mother had taught him what sits under a hand in that position, and how long it takes. He knew going past at a run that the man had somewhere between one hour and four, and that nothing anyone did would change it.

He saw a woman face down across her own step with the back of her head opened. That one had been quick. It had come from behind, and she had not known.

He saw a man on his side with his leg off above the knee, a belt round the stump that somebody had put there properly — wound, turned, tied — the man grey and shaking and alive, done by somebody who knew how. Kael went past him, and the arithmetic went with him: *that one lives if he is carried in the next hour.* There was nobody to carry him.

That was the part. Not the leg, not the head. He went past the man who could be saved, and the machine gave him the number, and the number was useless, and his legs went on doing what they had been told. Somewhere very far back, a faculty nobody in his family had ever built or trained or mentioned once put out a hand and touched him, and was ignored.

He saw the man with the coil of rope.

He was in the ditch on the right with his hat still on. Yesterday he had stepped into Kael's path on the road down to the arena, and Kael's whole body had gone to work before his head had any say in it, and what the man had done was take the hat off.

Then the boy of about six.

He was not sure at first. Then he was, because of the sleeve. A boy of about six had taken hold of his mother's skirt on that road and pulled it twice and pointed at Kael with his whole arm. His mother had looked. The man beside her had looked. Then the cart had stopped, and the whole road had found out what was on it, and he had been the first person on this island ever to point at Kael for a reason that was not a warning.

He was under the wall on the left. There was a great deal of blood.

Kael avoided his eyes from staying too long on that thing no longer the shape of a boy, and moved past him.

---

They came up out of the lane into the wider part where the wells were. There were between fifteen and twenty of them working the houses on both sides. 

"There's no way around this. Kael, are you ready?" Valeria said.

Kael nodded.

He went with her.

Here is what the next part was, and there is no arrangement of it that makes it into anything.

Valeria took the right and the middle. Kael took her left, the wall, the doorways. 

One of them saw them come out of the lane and did not stop what he was doing.

*Two strays,* he said, over his shoulder. *Off the left.*

*How small?*

*Small.*

Kael heard every word of it.

*Please,* he said. *Let us pass.*

It came out in his mother's accent, with the ends of the words where she had put them.

The nearest man turned round to see what had spoken.

Valeria opened his throat.

He was faster than every single one of them. It was not enough. There were exactly fifteen of them. His count had never stopped.

He got his blades up under one and turned it, and the man behind that one had a hand in his coat and hauled, and Kael went off his feet.

He was on the stones. There was a boot coming, and behind the boot a second man with a hooked thing raised, and there was no roll that came out of that and no lane to take.

He put earth into his own forearm along a line about two fingers wide, and let the hook come down on it.

It went through his sleeve, pierced the skin but did not go into the bone. 

The man looked at his hook. Then at the arm. Then at Kael's face.

*Resonants,* he said.

He did not shout it. He said it the way a man says a thing he has just worked out and would rather not have. Then he said it again with the whole of his chest, and it went up that lane and round the corner of it, and at the far end of it men stopped what they were doing to people and turned round.

Two of them went backward before they went forward.

He came up off the stones inside the man's arms, and put his short blade through the top of his thigh and turned it and took it out.

It came out ahead of the blood by about half a second. Then the blood came, and it did not run, it pumped, and it went across the stones in a sheet that was already wider than a man when Kael's second foot left the ground.

His right-hand blade was now carrying lightning.

He learned in the first ten seconds what that does to a man. It went in under the ribs of the one with the hook's friend and the man's whole body shut at once — every muscle he owned pulling in the same instant — so that he clamped down on the steel like a door closing and Kael had to put a boot in his hip and haul to get it back out of him. The man was still standing when the blade came free. He went on standing for a moment after that.

Kael put the thread back down the arm and into the blade and went at the next one, and kept that edge off their steel, and never once let it touch theirs.

Then Valeria stopped moving.

She did that perhaps four times in the whole of that morning, and each time it was worse than the moving. She put both hands down at her sides and set her feet and the lane came up.

It came up in a column the thickness of a man's chest — packed earth and old stone and four hundred years of people walking on it — and it took the one standing over the well through the middle of him and did not stop where he stopped. It carried him up to the height of the roofs and left him there.

Then the next one, three strides further on, and the next after that, walking up that lane ahead of her in a line. The ones that did not catch a man threw him into a wall, and the wall did the rest.

Kael had spent eight weeks of his life learning to put a thread into two fingers' width of his own forearm.

She was rearranging the road.

He priced it while he worked, because he could not stop pricing things, and the price was honest and he did not enjoy any part of arriving at it: there was no version of him, at any age, with any amount of study, that did that. It was not a technique. It was a purse the size of a house with eleven generations of drilling wrapped round it, and she had been born holding it, and she was thirteen, and she was not yet halfway down it.

She had been to the bottom of her purse exactly once in her life, in the autumn, on a sea court, because a boy with no capacity at all had asked her to go and find out what it did on the way down.

She had told him what it was like on the way down, afterward, in the flat voice she kept for facts, and he had written all of it in the blank book with everything else he was ever given. So Kael was the one person on that island who could stand in a lane and watch the Line of Vel spend and know roughly where her floor was.

It was further off than he had feared and nearer than they needed. It was not a purse you crossed an island on.

---

The one who killed him came out of a doorway.

There is nothing else to say about him. He was not the biggest of them and he was not the best of them and Kael, who could have priced him at forty strides, did not price him at all, because he came out of a doorway at two strides with a short-hafted thing in his hand, on Kael's blind side, while Kael was turning back for the girl.

Both his blades were wrong. One was out and low and away and the other was caught up in a man's coat behind him, and the distance was not a distance, it was a doorway, and it was knife distance, and the knife was where his father had put it four years ago.

*Not on the belt.*

His hand went into the coat.

He did not decide anything. There was no moment in it where a boy stood on a lane and weighed one thing against another thing, and afterward, for a long time, he would go looking for that moment the way he went looking for everything, and find nothing where it should have been.

The lesson was four years old and it had never once changed.

*Not the body. The carrying.*

He went to the body.

He put it in under the arm, where his mother had shown him the pump sits, on a table, with a hare, when he was eight years old and had asked why the blood came out of that one in a jet and out of the others in a run.

It came out in a jet.

It went up inside his own sleeve to the elbow and it was hot in a way he had not been ready for, hotter than the inside of a person has any business being, and it kept coming, and the man's weight came down onto the knife and finished the last of it for him.

It was not like the yard.

A man is heavy. He is heavy in a way a waster and a sack and a goat are not, because he is heavy and he is also *holding on*. Then he stops holding on, and becomes heavy in a completely different way, and the change happens in the hand. There was a man on the end of Kael's arm, and then there was a weight on the end of it, and there was no moment between the two, no line, nothing to count.

The man's face was eight inches from his face. He was young. Not as young as Kael and not old, and there was surprise on him, which Kael could read perfectly, and nothing else on him at all, which Kael could also read perfectly and had no use for.

Kael's own face was doing something.

He could not tell what. He went for it the way an old man had told him to go for it on that white sand less than a day ago — *you watch it, the way you watch everything else* — and he reached for his own face and there was nothing there to reach for. He could not feel it. He had no idea what his mouth was doing. It might have been doing anything.

He was very sure of only one thing, which was that there was nothing in him anywhere that was enjoying this.

Then the weight went off the knife and down, and Kael took two steps back into the lane and stood there with the thing in his hand.

"KAEL."

He turned round.

Everything was where it had been. The well, the slab, the girl, the fifteen or twenty who were now nine or ten. No time had passed. The whole of it — the doorway, the weight, the arithmetic he would be doing for the rest of his life — had cost about four seconds, and the world had not waited even that long. Two of them were coming down the lane at Valeria's back and she had her hands full.

He went.

---

After the first one they were not hard.

That was the thing, and he turned it over a great deal afterward, in the flat unhurried way he turned over everything, and never found any part of it that was difficult. The first one had taken a doorway and a blind side and a hand going into a coat without permission. The second one took a step and a half. He did the second one from behind, correctly, with the short blade, in the place he had been taught to put it when he was eleven years old on a mountain in the dark by a man who had said *here, and then here, and do not be anywhere near his hands when you do it*, and it worked exactly as described, because everything that man ever told him worked exactly as described.

He killed the third one going past, with the left blade, across the front of the throat, and did not break stride, and did not see him land.

He put the fourth one under the slab.

He came round the well and took the fifth one's spear at the shaft and went up it, which was the column — the same solve as the column, the thing he had done to a Drusane in front of eleven thousand people two days ago. The man had no more idea what to do about it than Lucan Drusane had. But there is no yard-master standing at the end of a lane, and there is no rule there about the shoulders, and Kael went up that shaft and put the point of the right-hand blade in through the man's eye and most of the way to the back of his head, and had it out and was turning before the spear had finished coming down.

He noticed that one less than he had noticed the second.

That was all he ever had to say about it afterward, and it was the part that frightened him.

And the cold voice counted.

It had counted at the settlement when he was nine and it had counted two, and it counted now, and it did not care what it was counting, and it was going to be there when he stopped and it was going to have a number.

He did not stop.

*There is a thing nobody tells you about the first one, which is that afterward you will go looking for the moment you decided, and you will not find it. I have looked. I looked for years, the way my mother taught me to look at a thing until it gives up what it is, and there is nothing in that doorway at all except a hand going into a coat. They built that hand. They built it in the dark, on a mountain, one stroke at a time, out of love, and it did precisely what it had been built to do, and it did it without me. I have never decided whether that was a thing done to me or a thing given to me. I have stopped expecting to.*

---

They came out at the top of the village with nine or ten behind them and nobody following, because there was nothing left in that lane to follow with.

The cart was still where it had been on the morning of the round of eight. It was on its side now with nothing under it, and the bed sheet was still nailed along the rail with a rope through the hem so the wind would not take it.

The wind had not taken it. Nothing else had either.

He did not need to read it. He had read it going away down the hill yesterday morning and had walked the last of that hill with his ears hot. He went past it without turning his head, with his father's knife in his hand.

Valeria went about thirty strides up the open road and then stopped, on purpose, in the middle of the ground where she could see three ways, and stood, and breathed, and did not sit down.

Kael stood beside her with his hands hanging and the knife still in the right one.

He looked at it.

There was a great deal on it, and it had gone up over the guard onto his fingers and dried in the creases where his fingers folded, and it was not going to come out of there without water and time, and there was going to be no water and no time.

His father had taken this knife off the wall in the autumn and turned it and put it into Kael's hand hilt first and said that the knife was the smallest part of it. He had not said what the largest part was. Kael had assumed, in the way you assume a thing at twelve, that the largest part was carrying it properly.

He put it away, inside the coat, where it went.

"Sit if you are going to," said Valeria.

"I'm not."

"Your hands are shaking."

"I know."

"That is the third thing," she said. "The first two are yours. The third one is the drink and the sugar and you have had none of either since before it was light. Do not build anything out of it."

He looked at her.

She had blood to the elbow on both arms, a cut on her jaw, her hair out of its binding on one side. She was thirteen years old. She stood in the middle of a crossroads on the island where she had spent one year of her life, breathing, in the manner of a person who has done a piece of work and expects to do more before noon.

"How many is it for you," Kael said.

"Today?"

"Ever."

Valeria Vel looked out along the road toward the water, where the fog had gone and the smoke had come, and Kael watched her decide, without hurry, that he had asked her a real question and was owed the real answer.

"I do not have a number," she said. "There was no first one. My house does not do it that way. They take you to see it before you can do it, so that when you can do it there is nothing about it left to find out." She put her hair back off her face with the back of a wrist because the hand was not fit for it. "There were four years of watching before I was ever given anything sharp. Executions, mostly. My father stood behind me for the first of those with both hands on my shoulders so I could not turn my head, and afterward he told me I had done well, and I had not done anything at all."

She said it exactly as she said everything, priced and set down, and it was not until three days later, in a boat, that Kael understood he had been told something enormous.

"They did it right," she said. "It is the correct way to raise a Vel. I would not change any of it."

Kael's mother had taught him what a lie is shaped like from the inside. He stood in the road and could not tell, and knew he could not tell, and understood for the first time in his life that this was going to keep happening to him for as long as he lived.

"You were sick on your feet," Valeria said. "In the lane. You did not stop moving and you did not stop being useful and I do not believe you noticed you were doing it. That is all right. It is what it is supposed to do the first time." She turned. "It will not do it the second time and you will notice *that* instead, and it will be worse. Come on."

---

They came up the last of it and the fog was gone off the high ground and the wall was there.

It was where she had said it was and it was what she had said it was, which he had not entirely believed of any wall, because he had lived his whole life on an island where the only things people built were made of the wrong stone and fell down. This one was a mile up from the water on the flat shoulder of the ground with nothing near it that could be stood on, and it was cut stone and it was high, and there were men on the top of it who had been put there by somebody who knew where to put men.

It went round the whole of the district in a shape you could not see the end of from the road, and there was one gate on this side, and there were a very great number of people in front of it.

Kael stopped on the road and looked at it and did the thing he did.

He read the ground in front of that gate the way he had read a Drusane's guard and an air column and a girl's lead foot, and everything he was he got from two people who had made him for exactly this, and what the ground told him was that the gate was open and that it was letting people through at a rate that had nothing whatever to do with how many people were standing in front of it.

He filed that. He did not take it any further, because behind him the island was on fire and in front of him was the only wall on it that had ever been built to be a wall, and because there was a road on the far side of this district that went four miles to the mountain road, and because he had a number now, and it was six, and he was going to have to carry it up that mountain and set it down in front of his mother.

"There," said Valeria Vel, who had been right about all of it. "I told you."

"You did," said Kael.

They went down toward the gate.
