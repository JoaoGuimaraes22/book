# Chapter Nineteen — What We Take

The door opened when he was still four strides off it, which meant his mother had been at the window and not at the fire, and had been there long enough to know his walk before she could have seen his face.

Behind her, in the middle of the room, his father was standing.

Vask did not stand in the middle of the room. He sat at the end of the table with his back to the wall and his hands where they went, or he was outside, or he was asleep. Forty minutes past Evening he was standing in the middle of his own floor with nothing in his hands at all, and the fire was banked low, and the bar was leaning against the frame where it went when a door was going to be opened again soon.

Kael came in and put his back against the door and shut it with his weight.

"I was followed," he said.

His mother did not say anything. She went past him and put the bar down and it went into the stone the way it always went into the stone, and the sound was the same sound, and everything about the room changed anyway.

"From where," his father said.

"I don't know where he picked me up. I had him at the second bend above the village, at about a hundred and fifty, walking."

"Sit."

Kael sat. His mother did not. She stayed at the door with her hand still flat on the bar, and his father came and put one hand on the back of the chair opposite and left it there.

"Go."

He gave it to them in order, and it came out of him fast and complete, because it was the one part of the day he had a shape for.

"A man on the road at the hour men come off the water. Wool cap, wool coat, the colour of every coat on this island. My build for height and heavier through the chest. Boots not made on the island — the sole was too flat and he set them down whole, so he came off a ship or he came off a horse. He never closed and he never fell back. A hundred and fifty at the bend, a hundred and fifty at the top of the rise."

"You tested it."

"Twice. A lace at the top of the rise, count of forty, and then again at the fold. Same number both times. He was matching me and he wasn't good enough to make it look like anything else, but it didn't matter, because it was one road and he knew it went one place."

"Where did you lose him."

"The fold under the goat line, where the walker behind you loses the top of your head for four seconds. I used three of them. Up the bank on the outsides of my feet and down in the scree, eleven feet above the road."

"How long did you hold it."

"The lamps at the water and a hundred after."

Something happened in his father's face that was not an expression. Kael had been reading that face his whole life and he had four categories for it and this was not in any of them.

"He came under me at the same pace he'd kept for two miles," Kael said. "He didn't slow at the fold. He didn't look up. He went on to the split rock and I don't know what he did there, because I was not there. I came home over the shoulder and down the goat line onto the path from the side that has no road on it. It added forty minutes. That's why I'm late."

"Face," his mother said, from the door.

"Never saw it. I had the top of his cap and his shoulders and his walk."

"You have the walk."

"I have the walk."

"Then you will give it to me tomorrow in the yard," Vask said, "at speed, four times, and I will tell you what he does for a living." He took his hand off the chair. "Anything on him."

"Nothing. No badge, no colour, no ring, no dog. Nothing on him anywhere that meant one single thing."

His mother came off the door then and sat down across from him and put her hands flat on the table, and began.

Where did you first have him. What was the light doing. What did you change. What did he do when you changed it. How many people on the road behind him and how many in front. Was there a boat off the point when you came over the shoulder and how many oars. Where was the sun. They came the way they always came, in an order that never varied no matter what the thing was that had happened, and he answered all of them, and somewhere in the middle of it he noticed for the second time in his life that the order did not change when the subject did, and that he had a name for that in the yard and none for it at this table.

At the end of it she sat back.

"He was not good," she said.

"No."

"How good were you."

Kael thought about the answer to that for slightly too long, and gave the true one, because it was his mother.

"Better than I needed to be."

"Yes," Neris said. "That is the part I am going to think about."

---

"Now the rest of it," Vask said.

Kael put his hands on the table because he did not know what else to do with them.

"The tournament lists went up this morning," he said. "In earned order, off the standings. Vel, Corvane, two of the senior cohort, Sedrane." He stopped, and made himself say it at the same speed as the rest. "And me. Four lines down."

The fire moved in its hole and nobody said anything at all.

"I didn't enter," Kael said. "I want that said first. I have not gone near it. I have not asked anyone, I have not written anything, I have not stood in front of one person on that island and let them think I wanted it." That last one was not true and he heard it arrive and let it stand. "Somebody entered me."

"Who."

"There's a provision in the games' law. A judge of the games can stand for a competitor who has no house." He kept his eyes on his father, because he had learned four years ago that the reading was better there and he was going to need every grain of it. "It's small and it's old and it gets used twice in a generation and it's for a boy off a farm whose father can't write his name. A judge used it. It's sealed and it's lodged in the capital and it went nine days ago, which is before the standings closed, which means whoever wrote it knew where I was going to fall on that sheet before I did."

"The First Master," his mother said.

"Spent this morning finding out what he could do about it. Nothing." Kael heard his own voice do something and put it back. "He said the Temple is his and the tournament is not. He can refuse me a room and refuse me the yard and he cannot refuse me the games, and he can't take a name off a document that isn't his, and if he writes to the capital he makes a small thing large."

His father sat down.

It was the first time in the whole of it that either of them had done anything Kael could price, and what it priced at frightened him more than the road had.

"The name in the second column," Neris said. "You have it."

"Halden."

He was watching them both when he said it. He had decided on the road that he was going to, and he had spent eleven years being taught by these two people how to watch a face while it received something, and he had spent the last year having the last of it beaten into him at the sea court by a girl who could price a room of six hundred in one pass.

He got nothing.

Not stillness — stillness would have been an answer, and he would have taken it. He got two people hearing a word they had never heard before and putting it down in the place where you put a word you have never heard before. His mother's eyes went very slightly up and to the side, the way they went when she was going through a shelf. His father waited for her.

"No," Neris said.

"No," said Vask.

And that was the worst thing that had happened all day, and it had taken four seconds, and neither of them knew they had done it.

"Who is he," his mother said.

"A judge of the games."

It came out of him level and unhurried and exactly true, and it was a wall with a coat on it, and there was a great deal standing behind it: two years of corridors, and a warmth that ran level for everybody, and a man who talked about the capital the way Oss had once talked about the Temple, and a drawer he had shut on all of it when he was eleven and had not opened until this morning, when it came open by itself.

He was good at holes.

"He arrived two springs early with a warrant that was correct," Kael said. "The First Master looked at it twice today and both times it was still correct. He isn't one of the great houses. He isn't anyone's cousin." He stopped, and then gave them the rest of it, because the rest of it was the part that mattered and he was not going to keep that back too. "The master said: *he is the empire, Kael. Not a house.* He said the empire isn't one thing that wants one thing, it's ten thousand men in ten thousand rooms, and any one of them with a seal and a reason can reach into another man's school and take a boy off a wall, and there's no door to knock on, because it was all done correctly."

Vask looked at Neris.

"And there is one more thing," Kael said, "and I think it is the worst one, and I have been carrying it since the third bell."

"Give it."

"He told me about the spring. The exhibition. The thing I did to the Vel girl's arm." He had told them that himself, in this room, at this table, in the summer, and had been audited for it and told the rest was correct, and he had gone up that hill every day since inside the answer four hundred people had agreed on. "He said everybody on the island decided inside a quarter of an hour that the sun had been on the sea, and that the agreement was not luck, because four hundred people wanted an easy answer and were handed one and took it. And then he said he was not going to ask me what it was, and that he had chosen every day since the spring not to say any of this to me, and that he had been wrong." Kael looked at his hands. "And then he said: *somebody in that colonnade did not need an easy answer. He had a hard one already.* And nine days ago a man wrote my name onto a sheet in the capital."

His mother stood up.

She did not go to the window. That was the thing he noticed, and he noticed it because he had cracked the window pattern when he was nine years old and had been quietly checking it ever since like a man testing a rail. She went to the door and put her hand on the bar that was already down, and stood with her back to the room, and looked at the wood.

"Say the judge's sentence again," she said. "The one at the rail. Not the master's."

"*What a pity it would be, not to showcase the empire's finest.*"

"And he asked you for nothing."

"Nothing at all. He said so twice and he priced what he gets out loud without being asked — a judge who stood for a boy who wins is a judge who was right in public once, carried the rest of his life. He said it wasn't a small thing and he wasn't going to pretend it was nothing." Kael turned in the chair. "He was kind about it. That's the part I can't get past. There was no cruelty in it anywhere."

"No," his mother said. "There would not be."

---

"How long," Kael said.

Neither of them asked him how long what.

"How long have you known somebody would come."

"Eleven years," his father said.

"And you have watched the sea every night of my life for a thing you knew was going to come up a road."

"Yes."

"Then say the rest of it." He had not planned any of this and it was coming out of him now in the order it had been stacked in since the second bend, which was no order at all. "You told me at this table that it was a weight and it kills. You told me this house was alive because nobody had ever looked at it. You told me I'd have all of it whole one day and to pray the day never came. Well, a man walked behind me to within two miles of this door tonight, so I'd say the day has some opinions of its own." His voice went and he let it go. "You told me once I wouldn't ask the next hundred questions that night. It's been a year and a half. I've kept every one of them. I want them now."

"Ask," Neris said.

So he asked them, and they let him ask, and that was the cruelest arrangement anyone had ever made with him, because they let him get all the way to the end.

Who is hunting us. Who are you. What did you do. What is the bad employer. Whose was the sword that made the seam in your shoulder and where were you standing when it did. Why does a woman who was born in a village know four languages and the order to ask questions in. What is my name. Not what am I called. What is my *name.*

His father let the whole of it run out and did not once look away from him, which was worse than any of the answers would have been.

"We are hunted," Vask said.

"By who."

"That is not tonight."

"Then what is."

"That you are hunted because you are ours," his mother said, from the door. "That is the whole of it, and it is not a piece of the answer that I am handing you to make you quiet. It is the load-bearing part. If you learn nothing else in this house before you leave it, learn that: nobody in the world is looking for a herder's son on a hill. They would be looking for you because you came out of us."

"Then tell me what you *are*—"

"No," she said.

"Why."

"Because the day you know it, you are carrying it," Neris said, "and a boy who is carrying it walks differently, and answers differently, and holds a door differently, and every one of those is a sentence somebody can read. You have spent this evening telling me how a man on a road gave himself away by the way he set his boots down. You are asking me to put something in you that you cannot set down."

Kael sat with that.

"That's the same answer," he said. "That's the same answer you gave me last year in a different coat."

"Yes."

"It was a bad answer then."

"Yes," his mother said. "It is still the only true one."

His father had not moved through any of it. Now he put both forearms on the table, the way he did in the yard when he was about to explain a thing once and only once, and the tally-marks came up out of the sleeve and lay there where they had lain in front of Kael for twelve years.

"I will give you three sentences," Vask said, "and they are not the history, and you will not get the history from me tonight or on any night I can foresee, and you are going to hate me for the size of them. Listen anyway."

"I'm listening."

"We were owned. We stopped being owned. People who own things do not forget."

Kael counted eleven before anybody in the room moved.

"That's three sentences," he said.

"It is."

"There's about forty years missing out of the middle of it."

"There is."

"Are you going to tell me any of it. Ever."

Vask thought about that for long enough that Kael understood, distantly and with a certain amount of horror, that his father was not managing him. He was actually thinking about it.

"I do not know," he said.

---

After that, they stopped talking to him and started talking about him, and did not send him out.

Kael would think about that for years afterward and never entirely settle what it had been. They had put him out of rooms his whole life for smaller things than this; there had been a night in the summer he was nine when the whole world had turned on a conversation held behind a wall with a boy on the other side of it declining to listen. Tonight they left him at the table, and built it in front of him in the flat voice they used for weather and salt and the price of a goat, and never once asked him what he thought.

He could not decide, then or later, whether it was the first time they had ever treated him as a man, or the last time they had ever treated him as furniture. Possibly both. It was the family trade.

"Not a winter crossing," Neris said.

"No."

"Not the archipelago at all. Every hull between here and the shelf is known to somebody and half of them are known to the master."

"The master is not the problem."

"The master is not the problem tonight," she said.

"How long can you sit still."

"Until the spring." She said it exactly the way she had said *no* about a name an hour before, without one grain on it. "After that I cannot travel and neither can it."

Kael's head came up.

He knew. He knew before he had assembled a single piece of it, the way you know a step is missing before your foot finds out, and then the pieces came up all at once and in the wrong order, the way they always did.

She had not eaten breakfast at this table since — he did not know. He could not put a date on it, and he could always put a date on things. The goat had been going down to the lower pens with his father since before midsummer and he had filed it as his father wanting the walk. Twice in the autumn he had come in late off the mountain to a dark room and a banked fire and his mother asleep, and his mother did not sleep before he was in the house, she had not done it once in eleven years, and he had walked past both of those nights and thought *she is tired* and gone up the ladder.

Three months. Call it three months. He ran it twice and got three months both times.

He sat at his own table in the house he had lived in his whole life and did the arithmetic on his mother's body in front of her, and nobody helped him, and nobody said the word, and when he got to the end of it he looked up and she was already looking back at him and had been for some time.

"Yes," Neris said.

"How long have you known."

"Long enough to have stopped counting it in weeks."

"Does the master know."

"The master arranged the boat," she said.

Kael put that beside the rest and found that it fit and that he did not like the shape of the whole. He had spent eleven weeks getting to fourteen strides up a stone stair. He had spent nine days building a rug the size of a boot out of a floor. He had spent an autumn taking a girl to the bottom of her purse and writing the numbers in a book, and he had been at the top of every column in a Temple full of the empire's children and three lines off the top of the boards, and in the whole of it he had not once come into this room and looked at his mother.

Nobody said that to him. That was the thing. Not one word of it was said to him, then or after, by either of them. His father did not look at him and his mother did not soften anything, and the flat voice went on over the top of it about hulls and about weather in the spring, and he was left to sit in his own chair holding it with both hands, and it was the heaviest thing anyone gave him that year.

"The birth here," Vask said.

"Here. Not the village. A woman from the village is thirty women from the village by the second day."

"Then it is the master."

"It is the master," Neris said. "Nature is the mending art. It always has been — everything a healer does badly with a hot iron and a needle, the art does properly, and there are perhaps four people on this island who can do it properly, and one of them has been coming up this hill for twelve years and eats our honey."

Kael thought about a fern standing up out of a pot on a windowsill across a whole room, and about a finger going back to one column on a page the way a tongue goes back to a tooth, and did not say anything, because there was nothing to say and because his mouth had gone somewhere else.

"Then after," his father said.

"Then immediately after. As soon as it can be carried and not one day past."

"How long is that."

"Some weeks. He will tell us." She sat down at last, on the bench by the door, which was not where she sat. "The boat has been under the scree on the north shore since before the summer, dry, with the master's man looking at it twice a month. It is a working hull and it is nobody's and there is no paper on it anywhere. We go from our own side of this mountain in the dark and we are past the shelf before the village has its shutters open."

"Where."

"That is not tonight either," Neris said.

"Will I be allowed to hold it," Kael said.

He heard it come out of his own mouth and could not think where it had come from. It was not operational. It was not a count and it did not price anything and nobody in the room needed it, and he was twelve years old and three lines off the top of the boards of a Temple full of the empire's children, and he had asked it in the voice he had asked things in when he was five.

His mother looked at him for some time.

"Yes."

"When."

"When it is dry and it is breathing and I say so." Something came up into her face that had not been anywhere in it since he came through the door, and stood there a moment, and went. She put her hands back on her knees. "You will be careful with the head."

"I know about heads."

"You know about heads," his mother agreed, and that was the whole of it, and it was the only warm thing that was said at that table all night.

Kael did the sum before either of them offered it to him, and he did it in the way he did everything, which was quickly and completely and without being asked.

Autumn now. The turn, and the cold term, and the storm-weeks, and the turn toward spring. The child in the spring. Some weeks after that.

Six months. Call it six months, and be honest about it, because a count you hurry is a wish.

Six months of the hour after the yard. Six months of a cistern court with four people in it, and a bench nobody sat on, and a girl who stood up in a doorway and could not decide. Six months, and then a dry hull under the scree on a shore he had never been told about, and after that the whole of the rest of his life somewhere else with these three people and no one at all.

Nobody in that room had said the word *friends*, because nobody in that room had ever used it about him, including him.

"The games are in the spring," said his mother, to his father, in the same voice she had used about the hull.

"Yes."

"Then he will not be there to stand in them."

"No," Vask said.

Kael sat perfectly still, with his hands flat on his own table, and did not say one word.

He had spent four years learning what it cost to be seen and he had built a very good coat, and he found now that the best work he had ever done with it was being done at his own table, on the two people who had cut it for him, over a sheet of paper nailed to a wall six miles away with his name four lines down it in a clerk's flat hand.

---

They went on for a while about what went.

It was a short list and they built it in front of him and it was mostly not things. The books went, because the books were the master's and could go back to the master. The salt went, because salt was money anywhere there was weather. The slate did not go. The loom did not go, and his mother said that out loud and then said nothing else about it, and Kael discovered that he was watching his mother price a loom and looking away from it at the same time.

His father crossed the room in the middle of it, thinking, with his thumb moving on his fingers the way it did when he was counting something that was not in the room, and stopped at the foot of the loft ladder, and put his boot flat down on the third board and stood on it.

Kael had counted around that board since he was nine years old.

He had learned it on a night when three adults were arguing in drill-voices about a knot and the argument had stopped because a board creaked under a boy who should have been asleep, and he had spent the following year learning the exact width of it in the dark, and had come down over it on the night he crossed the mountain and had never once put his weight on it since, because it creaked, because it was old, because it was a board.

His father stood on it with his whole weight and it did not creak.

It moved. It went down perhaps the thickness of a fingernail, all of it at once, from end to end, the way a thing moves when it is lying on top of something instead of being fixed into it, and when Vask's weight came off it, it came back up all at once too, and in the black seam at its edge, for about as long as it takes to blink, there was a line of iron.

A board that is nailed down does not move at one end. A board that moves in one piece is not a board. It is a lid.

His father took his boot off it and went on counting on his fingers and said something to his mother about the goat, and his mother answered him about the goat, and neither of them looked at the floor, and there was no reason in the world why either of them should have.

Kael sat at the table with his hands flat on it and did not ask.

He had asked a hundred questions that night and had been given three sentences and a shape, and he had watched two people he loved go through their whole shelf for a name and come back with nothing, and he had held two things back from them in one evening without deciding to either time. He was twelve years old and he had lived every night of his life eight feet above a locked thing under his own floor and had been told it was a board that creaked, and had believed it, the way he had once believed that the sea would freeze.

He put it where it went. He did not take it out again for a long time.

Later, in the loft, with the two voices going below him and the fire down to its coals and the bar across the door, he lay on his back in the dark and ran the count again, because he could not stop running it and because it was the only part of the whole day he could hold in one piece.

The turn. The cold term. The storm-weeks. The turn toward spring.

Six months, and it came out the same every time, and it was the only honest thing anybody had given him since the first bell.
