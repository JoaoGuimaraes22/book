# Chapter Fifteen — The Hours

The road home was long enough to fight the match eleven more times, and he fought it eleven more times.

He fought it along the coast shoulder with his right arm hanging dead and swinging whenever he turned. He fought it at the bend above the village, where the roofs were putting on their early lamps and the water had gone the colour of a slate nobody had written on yet. Every time, he found the place where it had gone wrong. Every time, the place moved. Reach was not the answer; he had taken longer arms apart and enjoyed it. Speed was not the answer, or not by itself, because speed could be spent early and made to arrive at the wrong address.

By the split rock he had the shape of it, and the shape was worse than any of the answers he had been trying on.

She had not been faster than his thinking. She had been faster than his body. His thinking had been the whole of his fortune for three years, and tonight it had arrived on time, at the right place, with the right answer in its hand, and had watched the answer be too slow to spend. There was no lever in that. There was nothing heavy anywhere in it to be tricked.

He went up through the last of the light with his heart going like a boy running, and he was not running.

His mother had the lamp lit and the slate out.

"Your arm," she said, before he was through the door.

"It comes back. It was the shoulder." He sat down without taking his boots off, which he had not done since he was nine. "There's a girl."

And he gave them all of it.

It came out of him in the wrong order and he could not make it come out in the right one. Vel, eleven generations, sent a year early to learn the ground. The twin wasters and the yard swallowing its own sound. Three exchanges for the spear, three for the longsword, six for Aurelian Corvane, and the way she had been absent from every one of them, standing in a fight the way the sea stands in an argument with a swimmer. *Who is next.* The whole yard three deep with nothing to spend. Himself, walking out into the measure with the coat's arithmetic left standing behind him half-summed.

He told them the duel the way his father had taught him to give an account, and the account ran away with him anyway. The herder's shuffle offered and declined. The seam between the flagstones taken and answered, not late, not corrected — waiting. His fifth exchange belonging to her from inside his second. Somebody reading him, at speed, answer for answer, for the first time in his life.

He told them about the thread without slowing down, because the thread was in the middle of the fight and the fight was what he was telling.

"—and there was nothing plain left that would get there in time, so it went into the waster. The stitch. It held through her parry and the wood stopped and the edge didn't, and I cut her arm."

The fire ate what it was given.

He heard the room change and could not have said which of them changed it. His father had not moved. His mother had not moved. The slate lay where it lay with nothing on it.

"Go on," his father said.

So he went on, and the going-on was worse, because now he could hear himself. The First Master rising on the steps. *No,* twice, the way you decline bread. The ground taking her weight differently, a settling, like a wall sitting down onto its course, and nothing leaving her body — nothing, he said, twice, because that was the part his eye had kept. A flagstone split under one step. Eleven exchanges, counted. The twelfth arriving through his best parry as though his best parry were a curtain.

"And she stopped it a finger from my face," he said. "She had all of it in hand. All of it, at that speed." He looked at the table. "I didn't stop mine."

Nobody said anything for a while. The lamp did what lamps do.

Then his mother said, "How many were in the yard."

"Three deep on all four sides. The young cohorts and both senior ones. Say four hundred."

"Where was the sun."

He had to think, and thinking about it put him back in it, standing in the drawn measure with the light where it had been. "Behind the west colonnade. Low. The barrels had shadows out to about a stride."

"So it was in your eyes and not in hers."

"For the first half. We changed ends at the reset."

"Who was on the colonnade."

"The First Master. The visiting officials, five of them, and their clerks. The judge." He said the word *judge* the way he would have said *barrel*, and nothing in his face did anything, because a great deal in him had been built to make sure nothing in his face did anything. "The masters not standing the yard."

"Where was your hand when the thread was in the wood."

"Low. Under the bind, on the inside line. It would have been behind the waster from three sides out of four."

"And the fourth side."

"The colonnade."

His mother nodded once, as though he had passed something, which he had not. "Who was nearest to you when it happened."

"Sedrane, by the racks. Corvane behind her. The rest of the line, eight or nine of them close enough."

"What did they say. Their words. Not yours."

"Wet light," Kael said. "A splinter off the waster. Somebody at the back said luck, because somebody always says luck." He heard how it sounded, laid out like that, in her voice's own order. "The benches decided before the healer got across the stones. They've had three years of practice deciding about me."

"And your friends?"

"Sedrane knows it wasn't wet light. She said so, and then she said it was wet light anyway, and we left it there." He turned his cup a quarter turn, which was his father's habit and not his. "Corvane didn't ask. He was busy paying for six exchanges."

His mother was quiet.

She got up and went to the window, and did not look out of it, and came back. Kael filed the whole exchange the way he filed everything now, and it went in with an edge on it that he could not get his hand around: the questions had come in an order. Not in the order a mother asks. In an order, the way the mushroom litany was in an order and the tap-sign drill was in an order, built before he was born and carried a long way to get here. Where was the sun. Who was nearest. Their words, not yours. He had a name for what it was in the yard — it was a debrief, they did it after the exhibition spars, badly — and he had no name for what it was in his own house at his own table.

His father had let all of it happen without moving. Now he put both hands flat.

"The thread," his father said.

"It held," Kael said, and could not keep it entirely out of his voice, and knew that he had not, and did not care as much as he should have. "It rode the wood through a full parry from the best blade on this island. A year of burns. It *worked*."

"It did." His father let that sit exactly as long as it deserved and not one breath longer. "Where was your wrist when it went in."

"Low. Under the bind, inside line."

"And a breath later."

Kael went back into the exchange and found the answer standing there waiting for him, and did not like it. "I don't know. I stopped attending to it when the wood stopped."

"Yes." His father took his hands off the table. "You put a live thread into a blade and then went to look at what it had done. On a day when it works, that costs you nothing. It will not always be the day it works."

He nodded once, at the fire, at the whole account.

"The rest was correct."

Kael sat with that.

He had come up the mountain wanting exactly this. He had wanted it the whole way, under the loss and the arithmetic and the dead arm — the audit, the mistakes named in order, and somewhere at the end of it the one sentence that said the school had held. He had it now. *The rest was correct.* His father had listened to a boy describe opening a stranger's arm in a game, in front of four hundred people, with an edge nobody knew was there, and had found a single fault in the account, and the fault was wrist discipline.

He looked at the two of them across the lamp — his mother, who had wanted the angle of the light and the words of the nearest bench, and his father, who had wanted the wrist — and could not find in either of them the thing he had been carrying since he got up off the stones.

He had no name for it. He had been going through his pockets for one the whole way home. It was not guilt, which he had been given all the tools for and none of the names. It was nearer to reaching in the dark for the side of the bed where the wall had always been.

"Leave the wasters in the shed tomorrow," his father said.

It was said in the voice of moving a lesson from one shelf to another, and it went past Kael entirely, because he was still standing in the wreck of the other thing.

There was no punishment. There never was. He lay in the loft afterward with the arm coming back in pins and needles and the day going around and around, and what he found, when he had finished going through the pockets of the evening, was not the thing he had expected to find.

It was not the loss. The loss had a shape and he had already begun taking it apart.

It was that he had been beaten whole, and that he had one thing left in the world that might have changed it, and that nobody in that house was going to take it off him. There was no rule to hold to. There was no law lying on the table this time and no gate to be angry at. There was the drawer, and whatever was in it, and him.

And under all of that, in the cold clean place, something had already begun making lists.

He was awake a long time. He did not once think about being unhappy. He thought about her back foot, which did not reset, which was the whole problem, and about how a thing that never resets must be arriving from somewhere, and about the eleven exchanges he had, and where the twelfth would have to come from.

---

There was steel in the yard before dawn.

Two blades on the block by the woodpile, plain as spoons, blunted along both edges and rounded at the points. They were not new. Kael did not ask where they had come from.

His father picked one up, turned it over once, and gave it to Kael hilt first.

"Wood forgives you," his father said. "It bounces, it bends, and it lies to you about what you did. This does not lie. Everything you have been doing slightly wrong for four years, you are about to find out about." He took the other. "Guard."

The weight was wrong. Not heavier — differently distributed, alive at the far end in a way the wasters never were, wanting to keep going after his wrist had finished with it. Every correction he had built into his body had been built around a stick that forgave him. Twice he came off a bind late and felt the blunted edge go along his own forearm, and it did not cut, and his body understood with total clarity that this was a fact about the smith and not a fact about the exchange.

"Again," his father said. And, one stroke: "Elbow." And, later: "You are aiming at where it will be. It is longer than you think. Aim at where it is."

Then he put the thread in.

He had braced for the worst morning of his life. He had spent a year learning what wood cost, and steel was going to cost more, and he had come out to the block in the dark that morning with the whole of it priced.

The lightning went into the steel like water finding a channel.

He felt it happen and his hand opened by itself, and the blade rang off the block, and he stood in the frost saying nothing at all.

Wood had to be persuaded. That was the thing nobody had ever told him, because he had never had anything to compare it to: every thread he had laid into a waster for a year had been laid into something that did not particularly want it, held in place by his own exactness and nothing else, sitting where he put it because he was standing over it making it sit. Steel did not need standing over. Steel *took* it. It went the length of the blade in less time than it took to decide it had gone, end to end, hilt to point, whole — and it was not thinner for having spread. It was the same thread everywhere at once.

"It likes it," Kael said.

His father picked the blade up off the frost and handed it back.

"Yes," he said.

"You knew."

"Yes."

"Why did nobody —" He stopped, because he could hear the shape of the question, and the answer was in every hour of the last four years. Nobody tells you the next thing. You get given the thing in front of you, whole, and then you get given the next one. "How much easier."

"That is the wrong question," his father said. "Ask the other one."

Kael stood with the blade in his hand and the cold coming up through his boots and ran it, and got there, and wished he had not.

A thing that takes a thread that easily gives it back the same way.

"The burst," he said.

"The burst," his father agreed.

It came on the fourth exchange after that, off a hard parry, and it was not the wrist-bite of the wooden year. It arrived everywhere at once. He was down without any memory of going down, with his arm locked out straight and shaking and his teeth ringing in his head, and it took a long moment before he could work out which parts of him were his.

His father crouched and turned the hand over and looked at the speckling coming up from the wrist to the second knuckle.

"Bigger," he said.

"It's the same size thread."

"It is the same size thread in a better road." His father let go of the hand and stood. "You will not be exact enough for steel this year by being what you were last year. Get up."

Kael got up.

And that was the shape of the summer, and the shape of it was this: Kael asked for more, and his father gave him more, and neither of them ever once said out loud why they wanted it.

The hours went backward into the dark. It had been dawn; then it was before dawn; then it was a stretch of the night with a lamp on the woodpile and the sea invisible below them making its noise. Kael asked for that. He asked for it in the third week, standing in the yard with the light going and his hand shut around nothing, and his father looked at him for a while and then said, "The lamp is in the shed," and that was the whole of the negotiation.

His father, for his part, stopped teaching and started proofing.

There was a difference and Kael found it in about four days. Teaching was corrections at normal size and exchanges built so that a boy could win one. Proofing was his father coming at him at the speed the thing actually happened at, over and over, in the dark, to find out what broke. Nothing was explained afterward. What broke got worked until it stopped breaking, and then he went looking for the next thing.

And through all of it — this was the part Kael did not think about until much later, and then thought about for a very long time — his father was never once hurried. Whatever the speed was that the thing actually happened at, it was a speed his father had somewhere about him, unhurried, all summer, and gave out in exactly the amount the morning required and not one grain over.

He was hit a great deal that summer. The blunted steel did not cut and it did not need to; it went into the meat of the arm and the ribs and the outside of the thigh and left marks that came up black and went yellow and were replaced before they finished going. He learned to dress in the loft with his back to the ladder. And he noticed — he noticed everything, it was the one thing nobody could take off him — that in a whole summer of it his father never once put a mark anywhere above the collar or below the wrist.

Not once. Not by accident, either, because his father did not do things by accident.

The care in that was so exact, and so cold, and so entirely without comment, that Kael turned it over for a week before he understood he was looking at the coat. Everything the Temple would see was left alone. Everything the shirt covered was fair.

He was proud of it. That was the part he could not have explained to anyone at that Temple, and never tried: he was proud of it. He got up faster every week. He came off the ground with the count already running where a year ago he would have come off the ground and had to go and find it. The machinery was holding. He could feel it holding, under the bruises, the way you feel a floor.

Twice that summer he came in from the dark and his mother was in the doorway.

She did not say anything either time. She stood with one hand on the frame and the light behind her, and let him past, and had water and the salve out on the table before he asked for it, and her knuckles were pale on the wood, and she said nothing at all. Kael, going by her with his shirt stuck to him in two places, thought that she was probably tired. He had a great deal on his mind that summer.

Once, in the last week of it, he caught his father not moving.

He had gone down badly off a bind — his own fault, late, the thing he had been doing wrong for four years and had nearly finished unlearning — and he came up onto his hands and knees in the wet and stayed there a beat longer than he needed to, getting his breath, and when he looked up his father was standing over him with the blade at his side, absolutely still, looking at him.

Not at the mistake. At him.

Kael had been read by that face his whole life and had never once seen the thing that was in it now, and did not have anywhere to put it, so he filed it and got up.

"Again," his father said.

He burned four hundred and eleven times between the spring and midsummer. He counted, because counting was his.

---

The Temple came to steel at midsummer.

It came the way everything came there, with a procedure attached: blades issued out of the armoury by cohort, racked and numbered and counted back in at the bell, blunted and rounded to the pattern every school in the empire would fight the tournament to. The yard spent a week being extremely pleased with itself.

Kael took his out of the rack and it sat in his hand where it had been sitting every night for two months.

He did not hide it. He had never once hidden that he was good, only where the good came from, and there was nothing in the coat that covered a boy simply being at home in a weapon. So he was at home in it, from the first hour, in front of all of them — and the yard did what the yard had done for three years and decided it was nothing much, and Lucan Drusane, who had been issued the same blade on the same morning with a paid master's four years of steel behind him, watched the charity case handle it like a man taking back something borrowed.

He added a line to a column.

---

The cistern court kept longer hours from the next day, as promised, and by midsummer it did not keep hours at all. It kept whatever was left over.

They came to it filthy and stayed until the light went. Elarine brought theory in a satchel and never opened it. Aurelian brought bruises he had gone and got somewhere else, on purpose, from older boys, and reported them like a man reading a manifest. And what happened in that court over that summer was the strangest transaction Kael had ever been part of, which was saying something, because he had been raised inside one.

"Do it again," Kael said. "All of it. Don't make it small."

"It is not small," Aurelian said. "That is the entire complaint everyone has ever had about me."

"That's what I want."

Aurelian's fire came off his palm the way a door comes off its hinges, and Kael watched it the way he had watched the men on the six-oared boat: absolutely still, no expression, everything going in.

Because here was the thing he had worked out in the loft with his arm coming back, and it was so simple he was ashamed of the years it had taken. *Everything I do is small.* He had said that at a healer's bench, honestly, meaning it as a limit. It was also the reason he could not see his own art. His threads were the smallest things anybody on the island could make. There was nothing to look at. He had been trying to study a working through a keyhole the size of a working.

Aurelian's fire was the same grammar written a hundred times larger, in a hand you could read across a court.

"There," Kael said. "Stop. Do the first half of that."

"Which first half?"

"Before it's fire. There's a part where it isn't anything yet and you're still deciding."

Aurelian looked at him with a small, dangerous patience. "There is no such part."

"There is. It's about as long as a blink. You do it every time and you don't do it the same every time, which is why your second one is always better than your first." Kael crouched by the bench. "When you go big, everything you do goes big with it. Including the mistake. I can't see my own mistakes because they're the size of a hair. Yours are the size of a house."

There was a silence in the cistern court of a particular kind.

"He has just called you a house," Elarine said, from the wall.

"I am aware of what he has just called me."

"I am only checking. You have a face for when you have been insulted and it is not on."

"It is not an insult," Aurelian said slowly, still looking at Kael. "It is the first useful thing anyone has said to me about the art in four years." He turned his hand over and looked at it as if it had been lent to him. "Go on, then. What is this *blink*."

That was the trade, all summer. Kael got a magnifying glass. Aurelian got the why.

And the why turned out to cost Aurelian Corvane more than he had budgeted, which he paid, at full rate, in public, in front of a witness, which was the part that made it real. He had never once in his life had to find a working's structure, because he had always been able to afford to miss. Kael took that away from him an inch at a time, and Aurelian let him, and got worse for three weeks before he got better, and did not stop.

"You are throwing money at it," Kael said, in the fourth week.

"I *have* money."

"You'll be in the tournament with people who have money."

Aurelian stood in the middle of the cistern court with soot on his forearms, and something went across his face that his tier would not have recognized, and he laughed. It was a real laugh, short, surprised out of him, the first one Kael had ever heard from him that was not aimed at anybody.

Kael's breath went to the long shape before he could stop it. He caught it, put it back, and hoped the count had not showed.

Some things were not going to be fixed by a good summer.

Elarine, from the wall, was pricing all of it and saying nothing, and when she did speak it was to move the ledger sideways.

"We should ask her," she said.

Nobody had to ask who.

"She fights adults every morning and children never," Elarine said, to the general air. "She has been on this island for a season and she has spoken to precisely no one under sixteen except him, once, to take his name down. She is the best thing on this island by a distance that is frankly rude, and she is standing in a field by herself. I would like to know what she does with her training. I would like it very much."

"She'd say no," Aurelian said.

"Probably. I have been told no before. It has never once killed me." She looked at Kael. "You've fought her."

"Yes."

"So you ask her."

"No," Kael said.

It came out flat and fast and about a beat too soon, and he heard all three of those things arrive in the court and stand there.

Elarine's eyes did what her eyes did.

"Why not?"

And Kael, who could take apart a fencing form in two exchanges and had once cracked eleven weeks of his mother's night watch by writing down which boats went where, stood in a disused cistern court on a summer afternoon and could not find one single word to put in the gap.

"It wouldn't work," he said.

"That is not a reason. That is the shape a reason goes in."

"I know," Kael said.

Elarine looked at him a moment longer. Then she picked up the satchel she had not opened all afternoon and said, "Rates. Both of you. You have been buying all day," and the court went back to work, and she did not ask again, and Kael understood that she had decided not to, and did not understand anything else about it at all.

---

It came up the road with him in the evenings and was there in the morning: *What does she do all day, and how?* Twice he built the sentence that would ask her, and twice something in him put it down, and there was no drawer that fit it.

So he did the other thing. If you needed to know a thing, you looked at it from where it could not look back, for as long as it took; he had been taught that by everyone he loved. If he had been asked he would have said, honestly, that he was studying an opponent a year out from a tournament he was forbidden to enter.

What he learned in the first week was her art, and it was worth every hour.

She did not throw anything. Not once, in six days. Everything went inward. He watched them stand her in the middle of the court and hit her — grown men in the capital's grey, with weighted wasters, hard, on the arms and the ribs and once across the back of the thigh — and watched her take it and stay standing, and understood on the third morning what he was actually being shown. They were not teaching her to endure it. They were *calibrating* her. She spent the resonance inward, and the blow came, and the man who had struck her said a number, and she adjusted, and they did it again. Too much and she was slow. Too little and it cost her a rib's worth of breath. They were walking her down to the exact grain, over and over, at a price he could not imagine paying once, let alone forty times before breakfast.

He filed everything. The weight of her going into a step and the sound the flagstone made when it did not break, which was the sound of her having spent correctly. The way the reinforcement ran up and stopped, so that her hands stayed fast while her forearms went heavy. The half-count of lag on the very first exchange of each morning, before she was warm — which was, he thought, with a lift under his ribs that he did not examine, a lazy place, the first he had found in her, and worth more than a month of anything else.

What he learned in the second week was not about her art.

Nobody spoke to her.

Instructions were spoken *at* her, in the register men use with equipment they respect. When it was finished, the grey coats went up to the town, together, talking, and she wound her wrists and put her blades away in their case and went the other way, alone, and there was nobody at the gate, and nobody at the top of the path, and nobody at all.

She was first onto the court every morning by a quarter of an hour, and Kael, who arrived earlier than that to be in position, watched her come down alone into the empty court in the grey light and begin, with nobody there to see it, at the same rate she would keep for the next four hours.

He watched a woman with a chained book read a letter, out loud, before handing it to her, and watched Valeria Vel take it and read it standing, and put it inside her coat, and pick up her blades.

And in the treeline above the sea court, in the wet grass, with his heart going in a way he could not price, Kael assembled the last piece of the puzzle he had come up the headland to solve, and he assembled it exactly wrong.

*Nobody stands beside her.*

He turned it over and found it good, and the more he turned it the better it got, because he knew what it was worth. All his life there had been a man beside him. In every stillness he had ever been asked to hold, his father had been holding one next to him, longer. Every drill, every dark scree run, every hour of the standing game — beside him, always, at his shoulder, doing it better and staying. That was not comfort. Kael had never once thought of it as comfort. It was *how you built a person*: you put someone next to them who is further along and you leave him there.

Her school had no beside. Her school had above, and her school had at, and it worked, plainly it worked, it had produced the most complete twelve-year-old he had ever seen or heard of.

The looking. It never stopped. He'd spend two weeks looking, from the wet grass, through the gorse, in the grey light, at a child being made by people who did not love her — and never once notice that the only reason he had a word for the difference at all was that he had been made by people who did.
