# Chapter Twenty-One — The Edge

The blades had edges on them.

Nothing had been said about it. One morning in the turn toward spring Kael came down to the block in the dark and the two training swords were lying where they had lain every morning for a year, and the light off them was a different light, and that was the whole announcement.

He stood and looked at them for a moment. Then he picked his up and went to his place, because the alternative was asking a question he could answer by looking, and his father did not spend words on those.

They worked an hour before it came anywhere near him. That was the shape of it now: the first hour was still the first hour, footing and measure and the same corrections his father had been making since he was seven. Then the light got grey enough to see by, and the hour changed.

He caught the second one across the ribs, through the shirt, and it opened him about the width of a thumbnail and stung like salt.

"Where was it," his father said.

"Low. Inside my elbow, on the turn."

"Where was it before that."

Kael went back through it and found it: the blade had been low and inside for a full exchange before it went anywhere, sitting there being nothing, while he spent his attention on the point.

"It was already there," he said.

"It was already there." Vask stepped back to his mark. "Again."

Kael put his hand flat on the cut and looked at what came off it, which was not much, and went back to his mark.

---

He brought the two knives up on the ninth morning.

He had been eleven days getting to it, four of them lying awake with the thing turning over, and he had made himself write it down first, because a thing you can write down is a thing you have actually got.

"I want to try something," he said.

His father looked at him and then went into the shed and came back with two short blades and did not say where they had come from. They were not new. One was heavier than the other by enough to feel, and the grips had been wrapped by somebody who had been in no hurry.

Kael took them and said nothing about that either. It was the family trade.

"Say it," Vask said.

"Steel takes a thread whole. So a sword is one road, and everybody I fight knows where it is, because it's the thing in my hand." He had written it down and it came out in the order he had written it. "Two blades is two roads."

"Two roads."

"And both of them live at once. Either one can be the real one."

His father turned one of the short blades over and looked along the edge of it and did not say anything.

Kael waited. Then he stopped waiting, because the waiting was doing what it always did.

"And a parry costs me everything I have," he said. "A short blade is a shorter line. There's less of it to fold."

"They will not stop anything."

"No."

"Show me."

It went well for about forty seconds, which Kael understood afterward was the worst possible outcome, because it taught him nothing except that he was right about the part he was already right about. The two points did work. They asked two questions at once, from two places, and his father had to answer both, and for four exchanges Kael was quicker than he had ever been in his life.

Then Vask stopped answering the questions and came in past them.

Kael's left hand was full. His right hand was full. His father took him by the wrist, turned him, and put him face down on the wet stone with an arm folded up behind him, and it was done so fast and so entirely that Kael's body had not yet finished the exchange it had been in the middle of.

"Get loose," Vask said.

Kael could not get loose. There was nothing to get loose with. Every escape he had was built out of a hand, and he had spent both of them on being fast.

The pressure came off.

Kael got up. His shoulder was going to be a colour by supper.

Ten years of that school, and every lesson in it had begun with him already caught. Not one had ever begun with him winning. He had spent four nights building a way to walk into a fight with both hands already gone.

"How long would it take," he said, "to build the escapes again, for two hands full."

His father did not answer that immediately, and when Kael looked at him he found that he was being looked at, and that whatever was in his father's face was not on the list of things Kael had names for.

"Years," Vask said.

"I've got about eight weeks."

"Then you have eight weeks of finding out how it fails, which is not nothing, and it is not the same as having it." He nodded at the blades. "Keep them. Do not take them anywhere near a man who is trying to hurt you until I say. Again."

They ran it eleven more times that morning and Kael lost eleven times, in six different ways, and he wrote down all six on the road down with his hand cramping around the pencil.

---

The threading through two blades was worse than he had budgeted for.

He had known it would cost double and had accepted that. What he had not priced was that a threading wants the whole of him, and there were two of them, and he did not go round twice. The first four attempts the left-hand blade simply went out, quietly, somewhere in the middle, and he did not notice it had gone until he was already using it.

The fifth one did not die.

It went out of true on the contact, the way they did, and came home into his own hand, and because there were two of them and they were both live it came home into both of them, and Kael sat down on the wet stone with his arms out in front of him and his fingers doing something he was not asking them to do.

He counted eleven before he trusted his voice.

"That," he said, "was the worst one yet."

"Yes."

"Both at once."

"Both at once," his father agreed. He crouched and turned Kael's hands over and looked at the speckles coming up along the second knuckle of each, and put them back. "You have made it possible to be wrong twice in the same instant."

"I can't hold two."

His father took one of the short blades off the stone and stood up with it.

What came up the steel was not what Kael made. Kael's was a stitch, the finest thing he could manage, laid in and kept quiet and gone within the exchange. This surged. It went up the blade hilt to point and it kept going, the whole length of it running and running, and it did not falter anywhere along the way, and the arm holding it was not doing one thing that Kael could see.

"The problem isn't capacity," Kael said.

"No." His father let it run a while longer, and then it was not there. "My purse means I can be careless with that and pay nothing you could measure. It has never meant one thing more than that." He turned the blade over in his hand. "I am not being careless with it."

Kael did not say anything.

"Where does it start."

"Your shoulder."

"My shoulder." Two fingers, once, against it. "Then here. Then the hand. Then the steel. Fast the whole way and tight to me the whole way and nothing coming off it anywhere between, and I have to know where all of it is at the same time." He set the blade down on the stone. "You do not know where your own elbow is when your hands are full."

Kael opened his mouth and shut it again, because it was true.

"Put them down."

"...The blades?"

"Both. Nothing in your hands." His father waited while he did it. "Now thread. Both hands."

"That's the off-hand drill. I did that at eleven."

"You did it with one hand, holding the smallest thing you could hold, standing still." Vask stepped back toward his mark. "You have never once done it with two, because nothing has ever asked you to. Do it now. When you can hold both of them and still tell me where your feet are, pick the blades up — and it will go out into them, because by then there will be nowhere else for it to go."

That was the whole of it, and Kael took it as it was meant, which was that his father had priced the idea and had not thrown it away.

Then he stood on a wet block with his hands empty and got it wrong for the rest of the morning, and for a great many mornings after that.

---

Later — because it was the turn toward spring, and because the mornings ran long now — his father came at him with an edge on the blade and Kael's guard was a quarter-count late.

The edge stopped across his throat.

It did not touch him. It arrived at the place where it would have gone through and it was not going through, and it stayed there long enough for Kael to feel the cold coming off the steel onto his skin.

"Dead," his father said.

Then the blade turned over in the hand that held it and the hilt came into his chest under the collarbone, hard, and put him back a step and took the air out with it.

He worked out two things while he was getting the air back. The first was that his father had not hit him in the throat and was never going to, because a boy who walks through a Temple gate with a bruise on his neck is a boy somebody asks about. The second was that the arithmetic had been done inside a killing stroke, at speed, and that nothing whatever about it had shown.

"Again."

The guard was better and it was not better enough. The point came in under his arm and stopped against his ribs, over the heart, and stayed there.

"Dead."

The hilt. Lower this time, and harder.

"Again."

Stomach. "Dead." The hilt. "Again." Across the kidney, from behind, arriving from a direction Kael had not known was a direction. "Dead." The hilt. "Again." The inside of the thigh, which he had not known was anywhere at all, which his father did not explain and never would, and which he would go and find in his mother's anatomy that night and then wish he had left alone.

"Dead."

"Again."

By the sixth his heart was doing what it had done in a yard in the dark when he was nine. His hands had that lightness in them. Everything had gone very clear and slightly too bright at the edges, and sound was arriving a fraction early, and he had gone past being frightened somewhere around the third one and come out the far side of it into the place where the count lived.

And he was getting better. That was the part. Not across weeks — inside the same morning, between the fourth and the seventh, his feet stopped arriving late and something in his shoulders finally learned the thing it had been failing to learn for days, because the price of not learning it had now been stated seven times, in a flat voice, by a man who could take his life off him without hurrying.

He trusted his father the way he trusted the ground. It had never in his whole life occurred to him to be afraid of him. He understood, going back to his mark with his chest banging and his ribs already stiffening under the shirt, that this was not the same thing as being safe. His father was not going to make a mistake — nothing in eleven years suggested his father made mistakes — but the entire arrangement stood on that and on nothing else, and there was no second mechanism anywhere in it.

He went back to his mark. He was glad to.

His father could kill him on any morning he chose, and had chosen not to, on every morning of his life, several times a morning, for years.

He found that he was proud of it. Not of surviving it — of being worth it. Of being a thing his father had decided to bring an edge to.

He went in to breakfast and ate two bowls and did not think about it again until he was old.

---

The Temple had stopped pretending.

For years it had been a school with a tournament somewhere out ahead of it, the way a village has a feast out ahead of it, a thing that shapes the year from a distance. Now the thing was the building. The colonnade had lists on it that were not the boards. Rooms that had been store-rooms since before Kael was born had beds in them. There were carpenters on the seaward side from the second week of the turn, and by the fourth week you could hear them from the letters room, and no master ever once told them to stop.

The entered boys were a class of person now. It had not been announced. It had simply arrived, the way the weather arrives: the entered ate first, and got the dry end of the yard, and were excused from the chronicle in the last week, and Ruthen stood their bouts himself instead of handing them to an older boy.

Kael was entered. It took him until the middle of the turn to notice that this applied to him, and he noticed it because a kitchen woman put a second piece of fish on his plate without looking up.

"You get used to it," Aurelian said, sitting down opposite with his sleeve rolled back off a forearm that had gone through green into yellow. "I have been getting extra fish for thirteen years. It is not the great compensation people imagine."

"What happened to your arm."

"Meloran happened to my arm." Aurelian said it with satisfaction. "He is seventeen and he is entered and he has a spear, and I have asked him for the hour before supper four days a week, and he has taken it, because his house and my house are polite to each other and he cannot say no."

"He is much better than you."

"He is *enormously* better than me. That is the entire point of him." Aurelian pulled the sleeve down. "You do not get better by fighting people you can beat. You told me that, in a cistern, when I hated you."

"I said it about form."

"You said it about everything and then pretended you had said it about form, which is a thing you do." He took Kael's bread without asking, which was a thing he had started doing in the autumn and which Kael had decided to allow. "I am going to be pulled apart in the first week. I have accepted this. I intend to be pulled apart at the highest possible level, and my brother is going to hear about it from three people before I write to him."

Elarine came past with a slate under her arm and did not sit down.

"Ledron is gone," she said.

Kael had read that name on a wall in the autumn, two lines above Sedrane and six above his own, and had never known one other thing about the boy except how far he could reach.

Aurelian's face changed. "Gone where."

"Home. Last night, on a boat that came for him." She said it in the flat voice she kept for facts. "His house sent. There was no letter to the First Master. I asked the clerk."

"Ledron is entered."

"Ledron *was* entered. He is off the sheet and there is a line through it." She shifted the slate. "That is the second one this month. Tavrian in the cold term, and everyone said Tavrian's mother was ill, and I believed it."

"Northwest houses," Aurelian said slowly.

"Both of them northwest houses. Both of them called home in the season their sons would be looked at by every house in the empire." Elarine let that sit for one beat, the way she did. "I have no conclusion. I want it noticed, that is all, and neither of you is to say it in the hall."

She went. Aurelian sat and looked at the table for a while, which was not like him.

"My brother's last letter," he said, "had a sentence in it about the eastern roads. That the eastern roads were slow this year." He turned his cup round. "He has never once written to me about a road."

Kael thought about that for the whole of the afternoon lesson, and got nowhere with it, and put it in the drawer where the things went that he could not price, which had a great deal in it now.

Valeria was on the sea court in the hour after the yard, as she had been every day for eleven months. They did not talk about the tournament. They had never talked about the tournament; the tournament was the thing at the end of the corridor they were both walking down, and neither of them had ever seen the point of describing a corridor.

She put him on the stones twice and he put her on the stones once, which was the best week he had ever had.

"Your left hand is doing something," she said, at the end of it, not looking at him, wrapping her forearm.

"Yes."

"It is doing it badly."

"Yes," Kael said.

She finished the wrap and bit the end of it, and did not ask what the something was, and went up the path.

---

The night still belonged to his mother.

She had been at the table when he came in, with the lamp already trimmed, and in front of her there were nine things laid out in a row: a copper rod, an iron nail, a length of dry oak, a length of the same oak that had been in a bucket since morning, a dry rope, a wet rope, a bone from the winter's goat, a cup of water from the butt, and a cup of water from the sea.

"Sit down," she said. "You are going to spend, and I am going to hold the tow."

She had done the tow herself, before he got there — a wisp of it teased out fine and hung off a splinter at the end of the run, so that whatever arrived at the far end would lift it, and the amount it lifted was the amount that had arrived. It was a better instrument than the one he would have built. He looked at it for a moment longer than he needed to.

"You built me a measure."

"I built you a measure," his mother agreed. "You have spent two years telling me that the answer is in the how much, and getting the how much off your own thumb. Your thumb is a liar. Begin with the copper."

He began with the copper.

They ran it for three hours, and it was the best three hours he had had since the garden.

The same thread. He had to make the same thread nine times, which turned out to be the hardest single thing in the evening, and she made him prove it by running the copper first and last and showing her the same lift. The same length of every material, cut to the copper's length. One thing changed at a time.

The metals took it and were not close to anything else. Copper lifted the tow further than iron, which surprised him and did not surprise her. The dry oak did almost nothing and cost him more than the copper had. The wet oak did a great deal more than the dry oak, and Kael stopped with his hand still out and said, "Wait."

"No," said his mother. "Finish the row."

He finished the row. Bone, nothing much. Dry rope, nothing. Wet rope, a great deal. Fresh water, some.

Sea water lifted the tow nearly as far as the iron.

Kael sat back and put both hands flat on the table.

"It isn't the wood," he said.

"Say it properly."

"The dry oak and the wet oak are the same oak. I cut them off the same branch. If the wet one carries and the dry one doesn't, then the thing that carries isn't the oak, it's the water that's in it." He was going too fast and knew it and could not slow down. "And the water isn't the water either, because these two cups are both water and one of them is nearly iron. So it's the *salt*—"

He stopped.

He had learned about salt when he was nine, in a different room in the same house, out of two pails left on a step overnight. She had taught it to him as a fact about cold. He had taken it away and done his own far-counting with it in the loft, alone, and it had killed the last thing he had ever believed without checking.

His mother was looking at the sea water and not at him.

"Go on," she said.

"It's what's in the water," Kael said. "Not the water."

"That is better, and it is still more than you have." She reached over and turned the two cups so they stood square to each other, which was a thing she did when she wanted him to look at something as a pair. "You have nine results and one of them is interesting. What you do not have is nine results and a law. Say what you have."

"That some things carry a thread and some things don't, and it isn't about how hard they are or how heavy they are, because copper and bone are both hard. And that a thing which doesn't carry can be made to carry by putting something into it."

"Yes."

"And I don't know what the something is."

"No," his mother said. "You do not."

She got up to fetch the second lamp, and it took her a while, and Kael watched her do it and then made himself stop watching, because she had told him in the winter, once, flatly, that she would ask when she wanted a hand.

She was very big now. She had been carrying it in front and high since the storm-weeks, and in the last month it had begun to be a thing that happened to her rather than a thing she was doing. She could not get to the table properly any more; she sat side-on to it, with her left arm along the top and her weight back in the chair, and she reached with the one arm for everything, and she had rearranged the whole surface so that everything she needed was inside that arc. Kael had watched her do the rearranging over about a week without ever once saying anything about it, and had understood while he watched that she had done it early, deliberately, at a stage when she did not yet need it, so that nobody would see her do it when she did.

She got the lamp lit and came back and did not sit down straight away.

"The word is *conductivity*," she said.

Kael looked up.

"The property you have spent this evening measuring. It has a name. The things that carry it well are *conductors*, and the copper is a better one than the iron, which you now know because you measured it and *not* because anybody told you." She lowered herself into the chair in the two stages it took her now. "The property of a thing that decides how readily it carries. Not how much of it there is. What it is."

Kael sat with his hand on the table and did not say anything for a moment, because a thing had just gone through him sideways and he wanted to have hold of it before he opened his mouth.

He knew every word the Temple had. He had spent four years collecting them the way other boys collected slights — the minima, the resonances, proficiency, declaration, the whole flat learned vocabulary that Priscian laid out like cutlery. He could have written the list. *Conductivity* was not on it. Priscian had never said it. Priscian did not have it to say; the man had a fine word for what a thing was made of and no word at all for what a thing would *do*, and Kael had noticed the hole two years ago and had assumed the hole was in the world.

And it was not a village word. There was no version of his mother's life in which a woman came up on this island and learned that a length of copper had a property with a name on it.

His mother was watching him have all of this. He knew that she was, and he knew that she had known he would, and that she had put the word on the table in the same way she had put the nine materials on it.

"You have never used that word with me," Kael said.

"No. This is *science*. The empire hates that word."

He waited. She let him wait, and the lamp did what lamps do, and it went on for long enough that he understood he was not going to be given anything else, and that this was not the same as being refused.

He had a shut door of his own. He had had one since he was nine. He knew exactly what one looked like from the outside, and he knew what it cost to stand near one and not knock, because he had made his mother stand near his for four years.

"All right," he said.

"There will be more of them," his mother said. "A great many more, and I am going to give them to you quickly, and I am not going to slow down." She squared the two cups again, though they were already square. "From tonight you will not be told to make a thing smaller. You will be told what it is called."

"Why now."

"Because you have got as far as anybody gets on words a child made up." She said it without one grain of unkindness, which was somehow the worst available way to say it. "Far-counting. What fire eats. The standing game. You built those because nobody had handed you anything better and you needed somewhere to put what you knew, and they have carried you further than they had any business carrying you." Her hand went flat on the table. "They will not carry you into a room with the empire in it."

"That isn't the reason."

His mother looked at him.

"No," she said. "It is true, and it is not the reason. Ask me in a year."

She went faster after that, and she went faster every night for four weeks. She gave him the words for things he had been carrying around in his own bad language since he was ten, three and four a night, and she did not explain where any of them came from and he did not ask where any of them came from, and twice she gave him one that he could tell, from the shape of it, belonged to a whole country of other words that he was not being given.

She worked past her hour. She had never in her life worked past her hour.

One night late in the turn Kael looked up in the middle of writing and his father was standing in the door of the room with his shoulder against the frame, doing nothing at all, watching his wife lean forward off the back of the chair to reach a cup she could have asked for.

Kael had seen his mother stand in a doorway like that. He had seen it his whole life, at the top of the yard, with her hand on the frame and her knuckles going pale while something was done to her son that she had agreed to.
