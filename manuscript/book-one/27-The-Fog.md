# Chapter Twenty-Seven — The Fog

He told them all about it, and it was the shortest telling of his life.

He gave them the road first, because that was the order it had come in: the boy who pointed with his whole arm, the man with the rope, the two words in whitewash on somebody's bed linen. His mother's chin came up at the sheet and stayed up.

He gave them the opening — both hands, three directions, the sword coming forward behind all of it. He gave them the wrap coming off his back in the middle of the empire's floor with eleven thousand people watching, and he did not put anything in front of it. His father took that without moving anything at all.

He gave them the fire coming apart on the blade. Then the fire coming apart on his hand. Then the two at once, and the arm, and sitting down on the sand because his legs had found out what he had spent.

Then he stopped.

He had meant to go on. He was fairly sure he had opened his mouth to go on. There was the old man crossing that floor at a walk and going to Elarine first, and there was a hand still on his arm and a question in it, and the whole of it was sitting behind his teeth in the shape of a conversation he had not finished having with himself.

"Tomorrow," he said instead.

Neither of them moved on it. His mother put the cup down in front of him. It was the mint off the step, and he had drunk it after every hurt of his entire life.

"It will be there in the morning," she said.

He looked up at her. In eleven years he had never once been the one who stopped.

"Eat," said Vask.

He ate about half of it and could not afterward have said what it was.

His mother got up to take the plate and did it in two parts, with a hand flat on the table in the middle, and nobody said anything about that either. When she came back she stood behind her chair instead of sitting down in it.

"It is soon," she said.

"How soon?"

"Tonight. Tomorrow. This week." She said it the way she said the result of a trial, which was the only way she had ever said anything. "He has come down. There is nothing left to work out about it."

Kael looked at the door without deciding to. Both bundles were where they had been that morning, and the morning before that, roped and squared and out of the way of the step.

"Go up," said Neris. "You fight the Vel girl in the morning."

---

He did not sleep. He lost to her.

He lost to her eleven or twelve times before the middle of the night, in the dark, with his hands out on the blanket, and every one of the losses was real, because he built them out of real material. He had a year of it. Four hundred afternoons of what she did with her feet when she meant it, what she did with them when she was only spending his time, the way she put her weight into stone before the stone had any reason to expect it.

He had everything except the thing.

Everything he had ever taken off her, he had taken once. The floor had worked once. The gap in the fourth exchange had worked once. There was a way to make her stand still that had worked exactly once, in the autumn, in the rain, and she had come back the following afternoon having solved it, and had not mentioned it, and had never given him the chance again.

Somewhere near the end of the night he stopped hunting and lay still and let himself want it, which he did not usually allow, because wanting it was not information.

Then the fog came up the mountain.

It was in the house before he was out of it. He came down the ladder into a room with grey standing in the doorway. When he put his boots on the step the split rock was not there. The sea was not there. The village was not there. There were about four strides of wet path and then there was nothing, and the whole of the world had been reduced to the sound of his father's block, which came from off to the right and could have been anywhere.

Vask was a shape and then a man.

"Off the water," he said, without stopping. "It will burn off by the third bell, or it will sit on us all day."

"Which?"

"Both are common."

His mother did not come to the door. Kael registered that, and set it down, and went.

The road was full and he could not see any of it. He heard carts. He heard a man swearing at an animal a long way off in a direction the road did not go in. Twice, people came out of the grey at him and were past before he had finished pricing them. Both times his hand had gone to the knife inside the coat before his head had any say in the matter.

They still knew him. That was the strange part. He could not see them. They could not see much of him. The word went down the road ahead of him in the fog anyway, one voice and then another, the way water finds the low ground.

He heard the arena before he saw it.

He came up the last of the rise and the building arrived out of the grey all at once, close enough to touch, full to the top rail, the sound of it standing straight up into the fog like smoke off a fire in still weather.

Half the field was gone in two days. Every seat in that place had somebody in it.

He went in under the seating and stood in the cold with his back against the new timber, listening to eleven thousand people spend the last of the waiting on the two names they had left.

"VEL."

It came off the western side, where the houses sat, in blocks, the way men shout who have been taught to shout together.

And then, from the standing ground and the cheap seats and the seaward end where the island was:

"GHOST."

Neither of them was winning. They went back and forth over the top of him for a quarter of an hour. Under it, quieter and everywhere, ran the thing half that building had worked out for itself and had stopped bothering to pretend about: that whatever happened tomorrow was going to be a ceremony, and the thing worth having was happening this morning.

---

The fog had come into the bowl and had not left it.

It lay across the sand in a thin sheet at about the height of a man's knee. It sat over the top rail like a lid. The far side of the building was a suggestion, and the seats above him went up into it and stopped being seats and became noise.

The floor had been raked flat. There was nothing on it anywhere.

She came out of the western gate with her hair bound and both blades in her hands, and she did not look at the crowd once.

The herald had a chain and a voice built for weather.

"Valeria, of the Line of Vel. Of the Collegium of the Capital."

The building came down on the name like a wave hitting a wall.

Kael was standing four strides away with his feet already set, and he saw it happen. Something went across her face and was gone inside half a breath. Not fear. Nothing whatever to do with pleasure. It was what a man's face does when a door shuts behind him in a corridor he has walked down a thousand times.

Then it was not there, and neither was anything else, and she came to her mark.

"Kael," the herald read out, "of the Island."

The noise that came back for that one arrived from further away and lower down and went on longer. He did not listen to it. He had been given four strides and about forty seconds, and there was still a hole where his answer went.

"They are shouting for you," Valeria said.

"They're shouting for you as well."

"Yes." She did not look up. "It is not the same."

He had nothing to put beside that. Being quiet was one of the two things he had ever been good at with her.

She set her feet. Then she said, "So. Figured me out?"

He spoke the truth. There had never been any point to lie to her.

"No."

"No," she agreed. Her chin came round and the green came up at reading distance, bored with nothing, the boredom that had died against him in a yard two springs ago and had never once come back. "You have had a year."

"I know how long I've had."

"You will find it in the first minute or you will not find it." She turned the left blade over in her hand, once, the way she did. "I have thought about that a great deal."

"So have I."

"I know." And then, because she was Valeria Vel and she paid what she owed at the moment it fell due and never afterward: "It is the only thing on this island I have been unwilling to guess at."

The judge came out to the line between them with his arm down at his side.

Kael put his weight onto the front foot and everything in him went quiet, the way it had gone quiet on scree in the dark since before he could remember, and he thought: *first minute.*

The third bell went.

---

The judge's hand came up.

Eleven thousand people made the sound they had been holding since the fog.

The hand did not come down.

Something went over the top of the building. It came in high out of the grey from the seaward side, going fast and going away from him, and it was the size of a cart, and it was burning.

Every face in that arena turned with it.

It came down into the standing ground at the seaward end, into the packed part where the island sat, and the sound it made going in was not loud.

That was the first wrong thing. He had time to notice it and no time to do anything else with it. Thousands of people shouting and one soft crumping thud a hundred rows away, like a sack coming off a cart onto wet ground.

Somewhere behind him a man said, quite conversationally, "What was that?"

Then the building started screaming.

It did not start where the stone had landed. It started everywhere at once, which was the second wrong thing, and it went round that bowl faster than anything Kael had ever seen a crowd do, and by the time he had turned his head to follow it the whole seaward end was standing and moving and no longer had a shape.

The second one came over while they were still getting up.

He saw this one properly. It came out of the fog over the rim with fire going backward off it in a tail, and it went into the western curve about halfway up, into seats, and this time he heard it, and this time it did not stop when it landed. It went down through three rows of new timber and took the timber with it and stopped somewhere underneath and burned there.

"FIRE — fire in the boards, there's fire in the—"

"Sit *down*, you'll have us all—"

"Where's it coming from? Where is it coming *from*?"

A judge went past Kael at a dead run with his chain still on. Behind him, up at the rail of the host's box, somebody was shouting for the First Master over and over, screaming.

*Trebuchets,* said something in him. *Off ships. They are ranging on the building.*

He knew the range of a trebuchet. He had read it in a cold room four years ago, in a book about the reduction of fortified towns, because Priscian had left it on a shelf and he read all that was on that shelf. He knew what threw a stone that size that far and he knew what it had to be standing on to do it and he knew, standing on the raked sand of the empire's arena with his weight still forward on a front foot, exactly and precisely what was out there in the fog.

The third and the fourth came over together.

And through the seaward arch, where the world showed between the columns, the grey moved and did not close again, and there were shapes in it.

Not one. Not four.

They came out of that fog in a line that went as far along the water as the arch would let him see, and behind that line there was another one, and the near ones already had their bows in the shallows and men going over the side into the surf in numbers, and it was not a landing, it was a pouring, and the whole of it was happening in complete silence because the building was making too much noise for anything else to exist.

Then a voice from the seaward end got over the top of everything, one word, torn.

"*BOATS!*"

And after it, a great many voices at once, and none of them saying anything usable.

"—the sea, look at the *sea*—"

"How many? How many is that—"

"They're on the beach. They're already on the—"

Somebody up in the western curve began to make a single high sound and did not stop.

The crowd went.

Eleven thousand people who had come to watch two children fight got up out of their seats and tried to leave a stone bowl through four gates, and Kael stood on the sand in the middle of it.

They went over the rails first. The ones at the front, who had nowhere back to go, went over the rail onto the sand, and that was survivable and most of them survived it. Behind them the mass was moving toward the vomitories and the mass at the vomitories was not moving at all, and the pressure went through the middle of that crowd like weight going through a stack, and people began to be pushed down.

He watched a woman in a good coat go down in the second row of the northern curve and not come back up, and the row above her walked over the place where she was, because it was not walking, it was being carried.

"Get *back* — get back, she's under, she's—"

"Marun. MARUN."

A woman said that one name about nine times while the crowd took her along the rail, and each time she said it exactly the same way, as though the correct number of repetitions had simply not been reached yet.

He found Oss.

It took him about a second and a half. Three rows up, hands already going, exactly where he had been on the opening day, and he was not moving with the crowd yet because he had turned round to get a smaller boy off the bench behind him. Kael saw the hands. Kael saw the round face turned the wrong way against the flow of the whole world.

Then the north curve stood up as one thing and Oss was inside it, and Kael did not see him again.

---

The First Master came down off the host's box.

He did not go to the rail. He did not go to the stairs. He came over the front of it, dropped the height of a man onto the walkway, and went along it into the north curve, into the four hundred, against the flow. The fifth stone came over the rim while he was doing it.

It came down at the north curve. At the children.

And it stopped.

It stopped in the air over the seats with about the length of a man to spare, and it hung there. The fire on it went on burning, going nowhere. People who did not know what they were looking at looked at it anyway, and for one entire second the noise in that building went out like a lamp.

Then the stone went sideways.

It moved off the seats the way a barrow moves when a man leans on it, ordinary, unhurried, absolutely without effort. It went out over the top rail and dropped somewhere behind the building, and the ground jumped under Kael's boots when it arrived.

Nothing had shown. No light. No sound. An old man in a grey robe on a walkway with one hand out.

The next one came over eleven seconds later.

It came down into the western curve. Severin was on the wrong side of the building. It went in among people, and he did not stop it, and that was the whole of it: he could hold one. He could not hold two. There were going to be a great many more than two.

Kael watched him understand it. From the floor of the arena, at the distance of a thrown stone, through fog, he watched the First Master of the Temple of the Southern Isles stand still for about a breath and a half and price the arithmetic and take the only answer it had.

Then Severin turned his back on the sky and started getting the children out.

He went into the north curve with both arms and began moving four hundred students down the walkway toward the northern arch. The ones at the front went because he told them to. The ones behind went because the ones in front were going. It worked. It was the smallest possible thing to be doing while the sky came apart, and he did it, and he did not look up again.

Kael did not move.

He was aware, in the way you are aware of weather, that he had not moved. He was standing on the sand where the judge had left him, on his front foot, with both hands empty, in the middle of the flattest and most open ground on that headland. Every school he had was running perfectly. None of them was producing an instruction.

---

The bowl came apart at the seaward end.

The stone that did it went in low at the base of the standing ground where the timber met the stonework, and the standing ground folded downward into the space under it, four hundred people at once, and the sound of that arrived across the sand as a single soft enormous crump and then nothing, and then the fire from the fourth stone found the new timber and began to go along it under the seats, and the smoke came up through the boards in fifty places at the same time in neat little rows.

The seaward arch went with it. The northern arch had a wall of people in it that was no longer moving and no longer, in any useful sense, people. The western arch was open and burning.

"The east! The east gate's clear — the *east*—"

That left the eastern gate, and there were men standing in it.

They had come up from the harbour and they had come fast, and they were not in a formation and they were not in a hurry, and they were killing everyone who arrived in the mouth of that gate. There were maybe thirty of them. There did not need to be more. They stood in the gateway and the crowd delivered itself to them in ones and twos out of the smoke, running, with its hands empty and its back to a burning building, and the men worked.

Kael watched them work.

They were good. That was the part his hands would not stop reporting. They were not frenzied and they were not cruel about it. They stood square and they let the ground do the arriving and they took what came with the short economical strokes of men who had done a great deal of it and expected to do more before noon, and they stepped over what they had made and reset and waited for the next of it, and he could have named the school. It had a school. Somebody had taught them, one stroke at a time, beside them, longer.

They called to each other while they worked. Short, twice, and then a third time from further back in the arch.

He understood every word of it.

*Two more on the left.* And then, from a man who had stopped to wipe his hands down his own coat: *We are behind.*

In his whole life Kael had heard that language come out of exactly two mouths. One of them had taught it to him on a flat rock above the house, going slower in it than she ever went in anything else, correcting him in it long after she had stopped correcting him in the others. The other had used it once, at his own table, to say hello.

He stood on the sand and heard thirty men use it to organize a morning's work.

He saw a man take a boy off his feet with a spear butt and finish him on the ground without changing the rhythm of anything.

He saw two of them kill a household — a man, a woman, three others — in the time it took the smoke to go across the gate and come back.

He saw a man in very good clothes stop in front of them with both hands out and begin to say *I can pay*, and get most of the way through it.

He saw the fifth one, who was young, hesitate over a girl of about six and then not hesitate.

Under all of it, from somewhere in the packed dark of the northern arch where nothing was moving any more, a boy was calling for his mother, steadily, the way you call across a field.

He knew what he was looking at. Every part of it. He knew the reach of the spears and the weight in the shoulders and which of the thirty was tired and which one of them was going to be a problem and where the ground under that gate went soft. He knew that the smoke was moving left to right and would be at the eastern wall in under a minute and would blind them. He knew that eleven thousand people had come into this building and that a number he could estimate to within about two hundred were going to come out of it, and he knew the two hundred was the part he was least sure of and he could feel his mind going back to work on the two hundred, patiently, because that was what it did.

He knew his mother was six miles away with a child coming down.

He knew his father would be on the ridge above the house by now with the whole of it under him and the fog burning off it, watching men land on his island.

It all arrived at once, all of it correct, all of it useless, and the machine that had produced one clean answer at the end of every problem of his entire life produced ten thousand of them and no way whatsoever to put them in an order.

And Kael stood on the sand and stopped.

He was not frightened. He looked for it later and it was not there. It was simply that there was nothing to do first — that every single thing was first, that the girl at the gate and the smoke and the two hundred and the ridge and his mother's hand flat on the table all had exactly the same size, and that a thing with no order in it cannot be started.

Somewhere above him the western curve went over.

He heard it as furniture.

Then something took a fistful of his coat at the shoulder and turned him bodily out of where he was standing, and his back met the timber of the judges' enclosure hard enough to empty him, and Valeria Vel put her face in front of his face.

She still had both blades. There was blood on her forearm that was not hers and a burn on the side of her neck he had not seen her take.

"Kael."

He looked at her.

"*Kael!*"

She hit him. Flat hand, open, hard, across the side of the head, and it was not temper, it was the correction of a fault; she did it the way the grey coats on the sea court did it, called and delivered and finished, and the whole arena came back into him at once through the ringing.

"There," she said. "I need you."

He heard his own voice say: "Thirty at the eastern gate. The smoke will take their eyes in forty seconds. It will not last."

"How long."

"Twenty. Thirty."

"I need you to be sure, Kael. Please, fast."

Kael steadied his breath.

"Thirty."

"Then we are going in thirty." She had already turned; she was already reading the gate. "Not the harbour road. Not the coast. Inland."

"Inland is—"

"The nobles' district." She said it flat, the way she said the result of a thing she had already checked twice. "It is a mile up from the water and it is walled and it is the only wall on this island that was built to be a wall. My orders name it. They will hold it and they will hold it for days."

Behind them the fire found something under the western curve and the seats went down another course, and the heat came across the open floor and moved the fog in one long sheet.

"My mother's on the mountain," Kael said.

Valeria Vel looked at him for about a second.

"Yes," she said. "And the mountain is behind them. We must regroup with the army at the nobles' district. Please, come with me. They'll help you to the mountain afterwards, I'm sure of it."

Then the smoke came off the western fire and went across the eastern gate exactly as he had said it would, and the men in the gateway put their heads down and their arms up, and she was already moving.

---

He went with her.

He got eleven strides out onto that sand. His body took the eleven strides; his mind stayed where it was for about four of them. Then it came, and it came all the way.

The smoke shut the gate. They went through it low with two blades each and did not use them. Behind him the arena where he had been named twice fell down into itself with the noise of a hill giving way. Ahead of him a road went up off the headland into fog that had not burned off and now never would.

Every third step, he looked left, at the place where the mountain was.

There was nothing there to see. There had been nothing there to see since the middle of the night.

He went up the inland road with a girl of the Line of Vel and eleven thousand people behind him. It was the wrong direction. He had worked that out before he took the first step, and he took it anyway.
