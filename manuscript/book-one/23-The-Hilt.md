# Chapter Twenty-Three — The Hilt

Lucan saluted.

It was a very good salute. It had a name and a count in it and four years of a paid master behind it, and it was aimed at the host's box and not at Kael, which was correct.

Kael gave it back. His was not as good. It was right.

Then the judge dropped his arm.

---

Lucan came on the straight back, at the measure, blade out ahead of him and the off-hand low and open behind the hip — the opening Kael had watched him make on Temple sand for four years, and it had got better since. The wrist was quiet now. The feet said nothing at all.

Kael did not take the measure.

He went out on the diagonal, past the place where the exchange was supposed to happen, and stood well outside it with his point low.

The arena made a small sound. The sound of eleven thousand people deciding a boy was frightened.

Lucan reset and came again, and Kael went outside it again, and the blade came round after him fast enough to move his hair and he was under it and out and standing at nowhere in particular.

Lucan's off-hand turned over. The palm came round to face him.

And the air in front of it stopped being simply itself.

It went *hard*. A column of it the thickness of a man's chest, out from the open palm and across the whole width of the floor between them, between one part of a count and the next, the light bending along the whole length of it the way light bends over hot stone and gets everything behind it wrong, and the sand coming up off the floor beneath it in one straight line.

It took him across the chest and the shoulder and lifted him off his feet and put him down eleven feet from where he had been standing.

The arena came up off their benches at once.

Kael lay on his back with the sky over him and no air anywhere in his body.

He got up faster than he had any business getting up, because his father had spent his life putting him on wet stone five and six times a morning and had never once given him the time.

His ear was singing. His shoulder had gone the temperature of a stove.

And on the way up he found it, and it had not been the air at all.

It was the elbow. Lucan moved his elbow, just slightly, off the line of the sword, a moment before he threw — the same small movement every time, the weight going where the weight had to go to get in behind the open hand.

A quarter of a count. It was not much. It was enough.

*It was already there.*

The elbow. Then the hand. Then the air.

He started counting.

---

Lucan threw the air at him four more times in two minutes.

The first two went hard through a space with nobody in it. Kael was moving before the palm finished coming over — out, across, gone — and the second lifted a fan of sand thirty feet into the bright air and brought it down over the front seats, and the front seats loved it.

The floor was keeping the account. Every throw left its line in the raked sand, a long clean scar running out from where Lucan stood to where Kael had not been, and they were beginning to fan out across that arena like the spokes of something, all of them radiating out of one boy.

The third was smaller and much faster, and it went past his ear close enough to take the sound out of it for a moment. He was already going the other way.

The fourth came from much closer in.

Lucan had shortened the setting and put a harder body under it, and it arrived almost before the hand had finished, and it went through the place where Kael's chest had been and pulled his shirt along after it.

That one he had very nearly not made.

He had read the elbow and gone, and the thing had still come close enough to touch cloth. Had Lucan been nearer Kael would have caught himself inside it.

So there was a ring of ground around Lucan Drusane — the middle distance, the comfortable one, the one every fighter in that arena had been taught to hold — and everything in this fight that could hurt Kael lived in it.

---

He never reached for the art. Not once, in nine minutes.

He did not need to.

His father had been coming at him with a live edge every morning since the turn.

Not drilling. Not correcting. Coming — at speed, out of the dark, five and six times before the light was properly up, and stopping the stroke by deciding to.

There was a boy in front of him now who had spent years being taught by a master his house paid by the season, and who had never in his life been in a room with somebody who intended it.

Kael had.

---

He never once stood at the measure.

Well out of it, or right in against him, and nothing in between, and the entire beautiful expensive grammar in front of him had been built by men who assumed both fighters wanted the same conversation.

Out there Lucan had to throw it, and throwing it that far cost him what it cost.

In against him he could not throw it at all — air that leaves a body from that near arrives back into it — and Kael was under the sword arm and inside the elbow, where the boy had been taught nobody would ever be, doing small ugly things that had no names in them and had never been shown to anybody. Two fingers into the inside of an elbow. A heel behind an ankle. The flat of a hand on the back of a wrist to turn a good stroke into a bad one and hand it back a half-beat later.

Nothing that would have scored in a yard. Nothing that had a form.

In. Out. In again.

And Lucan Drusane worked it out.

That was the part Kael had not budgeted for and never forgot. Somewhere around the fifth minute the boy stopped throwing the air at a place where a boy had recently been, and stopped resetting to the measure like a man waiting for a bout to become the bout he had trained for, and simply came.

He came with the blade and he came *forward*, and he brought the air in tight where it cost him almost nothing — short, hard, out of a half-turned palm from arm's length, not enough to throw a body, plenty to move a foot.

It was the right answer. Kael thought so at the time and thought so for years afterward: the correct solution to the problem in front of him, found in the middle of a fight he was losing, by a boy that nobody in that arena credited with anything except a straight back and a master his house paid for.

It bought him ground, and a scratch.

Kael gave him the ground, because ground was the cheapest thing he owned. He went back, and kept going back, and the sand went out from under both of them, and the noise came down off the north curve in a way he could not spare anything to hear — and every time the palm half-turned he was already a half-step to the wrong side of where it was going, because the elbow still had to move first, and the elbow was still moving first.

Once, it got him. An edge came off a stroke he had made bad and had not quite made bad enough, and went across his ribs and opened him — shallow, a hand's width, the sort of cut a butcher's boy takes twice a week — and it bled hard at once and ran hot down into his belt.

"*There*," said Lucan Drusane.

Through the helm, out of breath, to nobody at all, in the voice of a boy who has been right about something for four years in a room where nobody would agree with him.

He had been right. He was the only one in that building who had ever been right about it — and it had taken him four minutes and most of what he had to put a hand's width of blood on a boy who did not look down at it.

Kael did not go down and did not slow and did not put a hand on it. He came back inside the elbow where he had been living for five minutes and did the two fingers again, in the same place, because it had worked the first time.

That was where it turned. Not the cut. The four seconds Lucan spent looking at what he had done.

---

After that Kael made him carry it.

He gave him nothing worth throwing at and made him want to throw anyway. Stood out at the edge of it and let the arena watch a Drusane throw the air at a boy who was not there. Came in tight against him and made him wear it and was gone before the answer could be shaped. Made him move, and move, and move — on sand, in a helm, in front of his own house's seats, at the pace a man sets when he is winning and cannot make it stop being untrue.

The tells came up one at a time, the way they had come up in a hundred yards.

The breath went first, loud inside the helm, arriving before the strokes instead of after them. Then the strokes got bigger — the wide ones, the ones a paid master beats out of a boy by nine, arriving now because a big stroke feels like more. Then the air started coming without the weight under it, at nothing, at the space where Kael might be, at the sand.

Then the helm came off, wrenched off with the free hand between one exchange and the next, and the face under it was wet and red and eleven thousand people got to see it.

In the eighth minute the off-hand came up off the hip and turned over, and nothing came out of it.

Kael saw the whole shape of it happen. He saw the hand do all of the work and he saw the face arrive at the place where the thing should have been and find the place empty, and it was the most naked thing he had ever seen a person do in public.

*Strength is a purse.* He had been taught that at ten years old, standing at the back of a yard with a capacity he could have carried in a cup, and he had understood it the way you understand a law about ships.

Nobody had ever told him what it looked like from inside a big one, on the day the big one ran out.

He went in.

He came up the outside of the sword arm with his own hand turned over and took the wrist — not the blade, not the guard, the wrist — and put his thumb hard into the small tendons at the base of the palm, and the fingers on that hand opened because there was nothing left in them that could decide otherwise.

The sword came out of Lucan Drusane's hand.

Kael turned and dropped it in the sand behind him, out of reach, and came back.

Lucan came at him with both hands and no plan, for the first time in his life, and Kael put a heel behind his ankle and his palm flat under his jaw and laid him out on his back on the raked sand, and stood off him, and let him get up if he could.

The judge called it.

---

The arena did something Kael had no drill for at all.

It stood up. Not the boxes — the rest of it, the high seats and the ends and the whole banked seaward curve where the island had been let in, all of it coming up at once and making a sound that was not the sound they had made for the princess. That one had gone on a long time and had been about something they would never be nearer to. This one was short and hard and it came down onto the sand like weather, and it had his own noise in it somewhere, and it went on and on.

Kael stood in the middle of it, bleeding into his belt, with his ear still singing, and understood something that he would spend the rest of his life failing to put down properly.

He had not been better than Lucan Drusane. That was not what had happened out here. He had been *made* — by a man who taught him the body, and a woman who taught him the mind, and eleven years in which every single hour had been spent on it and no hour ever on anything else — and the boy on the sand behind him had been given the finest instruction his house could buy from the finest master money could hire, and had come out here to fight a weapon.

He was thirteen years old and he was better at this than anyone had any business being, and it had been done to him on purpose, by people who loved him, for a reason nobody had ever once said out loud.

Somebody high up in the cheap seats shouted a word.

Kael did not catch it, and did not know it was about him, and went to find his sword.

---

He was nearly at the gate when the noise up in the north curve changed shape.

He had his sword still in his hand because nobody had told him what to do with it, and the sound behind him went from one thing to another thing in about half a second — a wrongness in it, high, off to the left, the sound a crowd makes when it can see something the man in front of it cannot.

He had been reading that face for four years.

He had watched it do arithmetic badly in the well-shed and in the hall and over a fouled slate and on a wall in the autumn, and he knew exactly what it looked like a beat before it finished, because it always showed, because it had always shown, because it was the one thing about Lucan Drusane that had never once been taught out of him.

Kael turned around into it.

Lucan had his sword out of the sand and he was already close and coming, and there was nothing in the face now, nothing arithmetic about it at all, and the blade was up and it was not going to stop and it was not aimed anywhere that stops.

The cold came up in Kael the way it came up on a wet block in the dark at the fourth *Dead*.

Everything went clear and slightly too bright at the edges, and sound arrived a fraction early, and he was not frightened.

There was no moment when he chose.

He let the sword arm come past him. He took the wrist on the way through — the outside of it, where a man carries, high, with the heel of his hand — and turned with it and put his hip in and folded the whole long body of Lucan Drusane over onto itself, and his own knife came up out of the sheath at his hip where his father had put it, and it came up edge first, into the face.

And then it turned over in his hand.

The pommel took Lucan under the point of the chin, hard, going up, with Kael's shoulder and hip and the ground under it and eleven years of somebody else's economy in the whole line of it, and the long body went out entirely and folded down onto the sand and did not move.

It was very quiet.

Kael stood over him with the knife still turned around in his hand and did not know what he had just done. He knew what he had done. He did not know why the hand had done that instead of the other thing, and the arena was making a noise like the sea coming into a cave.

Then he looked up, because something had moved above him and to the right.

The First Master was standing.

He was on his feet at the rail of the host's box with one hand out flat on the wood, and there was nothing at all in the old face, and he was not looking at Lucan Drusane. He held there a moment. Then he sat back down in the seat that had been built to be looked at, and put his hands together, and was an old man in a chain again.

Kael put the knife away.

---

Nothing happened.

That was the part he took home. Two judges came out onto the sand at a walk. A physician of the games knelt over Lucan and did the things you do, and after a while four men came with a board and took him off through the competitors' gate, and somewhere in the middle of it a man in the games' colours said a form of words at the Drusane seats, and the Drusane seats said a form of words back, and the whole transaction took less time than the salute had.

Nobody said anything to Kael at all.

He stood at the mouth of the gate with his ribs stiffening and waited to be spoken to about it, because a thing had happened out there that was outside every rule the empire had read out loud on its own opening morning, and he had been raised by people who audited him for four mistakes on a mountain in the dark.

The judge who had called the bout walked past him and said, "Second day, ninth bell," and looked at a board, and went.

---

The round ran all afternoon, and Kael sat in the north curve with a cloth held on his ribs and watched what the art looked like when the people who had it were allowed to take it all the way.

He saw a girl of fifteen from the capital take a boy's feet out from under him with a sheet of ice that came off her own forearm and was across the sand before it was anywhere. He saw a northwest boy who had not said a word to anybody all week put his hand flat on the ground and bring a foot of the arena floor up in front of him like a shutter, and take a blow on it that would have gone through a door.

He had watched a hundred bouts in that yard and fought in a good number of them, and he understood, sitting up there with the cloth going stiff against his side, that he had never in his life seen anybody finish one. What was on that floor all afternoon was the same art with the stopping taken off, and it was not a bigger version of the thing he knew. It was a different thing wearing its clothes.

He watched all of it the way he had watched a cooper at a hull.

Valeria fought last. Her northwest boy was seventeen and enormous and had a spear, and had, Kael thought, been told exactly what he was walking into, because he came out fast and hard and correct and tried very seriously to finish it inside four exchanges.

It took her nineteen seconds.

The arena saw a small black-haired girl in the capital's colours stand still, and then not be where the spear was, twice, and then be inside it. Eleven thousand people made a polite, impressed, faintly disappointed noise — the noise you make at a thing that is over before you have understood that you are watching it.

What Kael saw was that she had not spent one grain either.

---

The hall that evening was the loudest he had ever heard it, and it had a word in it.

He had picked the word up twice already without it meaning anything: once in the lane behind the arena, from a man calling it over a brazier to another man who had not been there, and once an hour later from a boy of about eight sitting on a wall, who said it directly to his face with his whole chest and then went red and could not do anything else at all.

In the hall it had stopped being shouted and had started being said.

"You are aware," Aurelian said, sitting down opposite with his mouth swollen out of true on one side, "that you have a name."

"I heard it in the lane."

"You have heard a *rumour* of it in a lane. I have now heard it at four tables, from a carter's son, from two kitchen boys and from a man who sweeps the colonnade, and I am reliably informed the village has had it since about three o'clock." He was enjoying himself in a way that was costing his mouth something. "The Ghost. Of the Island. They are extremely pleased with themselves."

"It's a word for weather."

"It is a word for a thing you cannot hit." Aurelian took Kael's bread without asking. "Nine minutes, Kael. He put every grain he had into you for nine minutes in front of the entire empire and he did not lay one finger on you, and then he ran out, and everybody in that building watched him run out. My tier has not stopped talking about it and not one of them is enjoying it."

"You're enjoying it."

"I am in a great deal of pain and I am enjoying it enormously. It is not the same thing." He put the bread down. "Sedrane has been unbearable since four."

Elarine arrived at that, put her slate on the table, and did not sit.

"Nothing is going to happen," she said.

Kael looked up.

"About Drusane. I have been finding out, since you plainly have not." The flat voice, the one she kept for facts. "The games' office has entered it as an *incident following the call*. That is the whole of it. That is a phrase for a man's hat coming off. It has been entered, and the entry has been read by nobody, and there is not going to be a hearing, and Lucan is in the physicians' hall with a broken jaw and his name is still on the sheet for the second round, which he will not fight, so the line will simply be scratched and the next boy goes forward."

"He came at my back with a sword."

"Yes." She said it without any heat at all, which was worse. "In front of eleven thousand witnesses, four judges, the First Master and the daughter of the Emperor. And the empire has written *incident following the call* and gone to its supper, and it has done that because of what is in the second column of the sheet and not for one other reason on this earth, and I want that noticed. That is all. I am not going to say it again and neither of you is to say it in the hall."

"You are saying it in the hall," said Aurelian.

"I am saying it at a table with my back to the room, which is different, and if you cannot hear the difference you deserve everything your house is going to do to you." She picked her slate back up. Then she stood there a moment longer, and Kael, who had been being looked at for four years by everyone in that building and had learned to tell the kinds apart, understood that he was being looked at in a way she had not decided about yet.

"He threw at you from the far end of that floor," she said. "Twice. Then he came in close, once, and it very nearly finished you, and after that he never got near you again all afternoon, because you did not let him. And in nine minutes you did not spend one grain."

The hall went on being loud around the three of them.

There was a question standing at the end of that, and she let it stand exactly one beat past the place where it would have gone, so that he would know it had been available.

Then she picked up her satchel.

"Second day, ninth bell," Elarine said. "Get the ribs looked at properly and not by a games physician, they are paid by the hour. Corvane — your mouth is going to close in the night and you will not be able to eat, so eat now."

"I *am* eating."

"Eat more."

She went. Aurelian watched her go with an expression Kael had come to recognise over eighteen months and had never once been able to put a word to.

"She sat up with the law all last night," he said. "Before the bouts. She told me it was to check something about the draw." He turned his cup round. "It was not about the draw."

Kael did not say anything.

Three tables away, under the windows, in the good light, a boy with a soft build and a bought name was sitting with people who were not laughing at him, and had been listening to the word go round the room since the bell, and had not once looked over.

Kael knew he had not looked over, which meant that Kael had been looking.

---

The board went up on the colonnade of the first court at full dark, in the clerk's flat hand, with a lamp hung over it, and a crowd four deep in front of it that had not been four deep in front of anything since the spring.

He waited it out, and when it thinned she was already there.

She had waited it out too. Of course she had; there were four thousand people in that court by suppertime and she had spent a year not being looked at in rooms that were looking at her. She stood in front of the sheet with her arms folded and her hair still wet from wherever she had washed the boy's blood off, and did not turn round when he came up beside her, and did not say anything at all.

They read it together.

Sixteen names had become eight, in two columns of four, and the columns did not touch until the second sheet, and the second sheet was a shape any child could read: four and four, then two and two, then the two who were left.

Kael put his thumb under his own line, which was his mother's trick for holding a column of figures still, and walked it down.

His name. Then, three lines below it in the same column, the seventeen-year-old with the spear who had been taking the hour before supper off Aurelian four days a week since the turn.

And at the bottom of the same column, in the same flat hand as everything else on the sheet: *Valeria, of the Line of Vel.*

Same column. Same half. Two bouts each, and then the fourth bell of the last day.

He did the arithmetic four times, because a thing you get once is a thing you might have got wrong, and it came out the same every time.

"Meloran," Valeria said, "has four years and a foot of reach on you, and he is better than Corvane, and Corvane is better than everyone here except us."

"I know."

"Good." She was still looking at the sheet. "I have watched him for eleven days. He is slow at the recovery on the left and he does not know it, because nobody on this island is fast enough to have told him."

Kael turned his head.

She did not turn hers. She said it the way she said everything, flat, at the wall, as though she were reading it off the sheet along with the rest, and then she unfolded her arms and went, past him, without her name in his mouth or his in hers.

He stood in front of the board a long time after she had gone.

She was three wins away. So was he, and one of the three was her, which meant the thing he had wanted for a year was not at the end of this — it was one round short of the end, and after it there would be a final, and the final would be somebody's, and he found he could not make himself care about the second sheet at all.

Behind him the court was still saying the word.

He stood in the cold with his ribs pulling and a name on him that two days ago had been an empty space, and it had come at him from the wrong direction entirely, out of eleven thousand people who did not know one single thing about him, and it was not a place and it was not a house.

*You are not absent,* said a girl with her back to a door, in a room in the autumn. *You are a hole.*

She had meant it as a warning, and she had been right about it, in a room, in front of nobody.

Now it was on a wall, and they were pleased about it.

And he thought, with no warning, from nowhere he could name: *and then what.*

He had wanted the lights. He had got the lights. He had wanted to be measured by people who could tell, and eleven thousand of them had stood up on a bright cold morning and made a noise about him, and he had walked off that sand knowing precisely what he was and how it had been done — and there was still, underneath the whole of it, in the place where every other child on that sheet kept a house or a name or a Line, nothing at all holding the other end.

He put his hand flat on the cold stone beside the board for a moment, the way you touch a rock you have gone past your whole life.

Then he went to get his kit, and go up the mountain, and be home before his mother started counting.
