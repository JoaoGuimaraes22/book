# Chapter Eighteen — The Entry

The list went up before the first bell, because the Temple liked a thing to be standing there already when the children came out to it.

Kael came in off the road with his boots wet to the ankle and the rain still in his hair, and the first court was solid at the colonnade, three deep the way the yard had gone three deep in the spring, and making the same sound. He went along the outside of the crowd to the west end, where the pillar stood off the wall by the width of a hand and a small boy could get his shoulder into the gap and read sideways, and he read the list sideways, in the grey light, over the top of somebody's collar.

It was two sheets. The Temple's clerks had ruled the columns with the same hand they ruled everything with, and the names came down in earned order, off the standings that had been hanging on that wall since the spring, and it read like the boards had learned to walk.

*Valeria, of the Line of Vel.*

He had known that since the day she arrived and she had said so herself, out loud, on the steps, in front of everybody, and it still went through him.

Then Corvane. Then two of the senior cohort he only knew by their reach. Then Sedrane.

Then, four lines down, in the clerk's flat hand, no taller and no shorter than anything else on the sheet:

*Kael.*

He read it. Then he read it again, from the top, all four names, to make sure he had not come in at the wrong place on the page. Then he read it a third time with his thumb under the line, the way his mother had taught him to hold a column of figures still while he checked it, and it stayed where it was.

And for about the space of one breath, before anything else got there, he was happy.

It came up through him the way the want had come up on the steps a year and a half ago, with his whole body, leaning. The thing he had asked for and been refused and stopped asking for was standing on a wall in a public hand where four hundred people would read it, and it had his name on it. He felt his own face start to do something. He caught it about halfway and put it back.

Because a list is a document, and documents are made by clerks, and clerks make mistakes.

That was the whole of it. That was the only drawer in the house that fit. Somebody had copied a line wrong off the standings, and by the second bell a man with a pen would come out and score through it and write it again correctly, and there would be an afternoon of it in the hall and then it would be over.

He got his shoulder back out of the gap and stood in the wet with the crowd noise going over him and made himself finish reading properly, which meant the columns he had skipped.

Every name on that sheet had a house after it.

*Corvane. Sedrane. Drusane. Vel.* The clerks had put them in the second column with the same flat hand, because a boy does not enter a tournament that gives away a city; a boy's house enters him, and the city goes to the house, and the boy holds it in his name until he is old enough to be told what he owns.

Kael had no house. He had never had one. On the day he was entered in the roll, at ten years old, the space had been left empty and a clerk had drawn a line through it to close it, and the master had said *leave the space as it is, empty is true,* and Kael had carried the line for four years like a thing he owned.

The space was not empty.

There was a name in it, and after the name a word he did not know, and the word had the shape words have when they mean an office rather than a person.

The name was Halden.

---

He stood there for a while with the collar in front of him moving and the rain coming off the colonnade roof in a line, and the drawer he had put the judge in two years ago came open by itself, the way a bar comes off a door somebody has been leaning on for a long time. It was the shut one, the one with the level warmth in it that he had filed and not examined and not opened since.

He did not know what was in it. That was the thing. He had a wrongness with a face on it now and not one single word to put after the wrongness.

"There you are." Aurelian Corvane came out of the crowd with his hair wet and his whole carriage a hand higher than it had been yesterday, and he took Kael by the shoulder with both hands and shook him once, hard, the way you shake a post to see if it is set. "You *lied* to me."

"No."

"Four lines. Four lines, and *below* Sedrane, which she will be unbearable about for a year, and you sat in that court in the dark and told me it was not for you." He was grinning. It was an entirely undefended grin and Kael had never seen it on him and did not have anywhere to put it. "Was it a house? Did somebody take you? Tell me it was not Vessaline, I will not be able to bear it if it was Vessaline—"

"I don't know who it was."

Aurelian's grin came down about a quarter of the way, the way a sail comes down, unhurried, still full.

"You don't know."

"No."

"Somebody has stood for you at the empire's games and you do not know who."

"No," Kael said.

And Aurelian Corvane, who had been raised inside the arithmetic of houses standing for people the way Kael had been raised inside a count, went quiet, and looked at the wall, and then looked back at Kael, and something crossed his face that had nothing at all in it of the morning.

"Right," he said.

Elarine had not come out of the crowd. She was on the far side of it, at the second pillar, and she had been there the whole time, and she was not reading the list. She was reading him. When he found her she did not look away and she did not come over, and she moved her eyes once, deliberately, from him to the second column and back, and let him see her do it.

Then she turned round and went in to letters, because the bell had gone.

Valeria came past him at the arch with her case in her hand and her blades in the case and the grey coats waiting up the path, and she stopped, which she did not do, and stood in front of him for exactly as long as it took.

"You're on it," she said.

"Yes."

"Good," said Valeria Vel, and went.

It was the only clean thing that happened to him all day, and it was going to be a long time before he could take it out and look at it without the rest of the day coming out of the drawer attached to it.

---

He had never gone up to the study without being sent for.

Four years, and every one of the summonses had come the same way: a boy from the clerks' room at the end of the third lesson, the same words every time, *the First Master will see you before you go down*, and Kael had gone up the stair and sat in the chair and had his book read and his year audited and gone home. He had never once climbed that stair on his own account. It had not occurred to him that a person could.

The door was open. That was the first thing.

The second thing was that the master was standing, and the fern on the sill had not been touched, and there were two letters on the desk lying open, and the honey jar was where it always was, and the room had the look of a room somebody had been walking around in since before it was light.

"Come in and close it," Severin said.

Kael closed it.

"You have seen the list."

"Yes."

"Sit down." The master went round the desk and did not sit himself, which Kael had also never seen. He put one finger on the nearer of the two letters and moved it very slightly, as though it had been lying at an angle he did not like. "I am going to tell you three things and then I am going to answer whatever you have. The first is the one you came for, so I will not make you carry it up the hill. I have spent this morning finding out what I can do about that sheet."

"Yes."

"Nothing," said Severin.

The rain went along the window.

"I want you to hear the shape of it, because you will build something wrong out of it otherwise and I would rather you built the true thing." He came round the desk's end and leaned back against it, and folded his arms, and looked at the floor between them the way a man looks at ground he is about to walk over. "The Temple is mine. The tournament is not. The empire holds the games; three schools convene; the island keeps them because the island is convenient and because I asked for them, four years ago, when I thought I understood what I was asking for. Entries are made to the games, under the games' seal, by men the games appoint. A judge may stand for a competitor who has no house. It is a small provision. It exists so that the empire never loses a boy off a farm because his father cannot write."

"And it was used."

"It was used correctly," Severin said. "That is what I have spent the morning learning. There is no error in it anywhere. It is entered, it is sealed, it is lodged in the capital, and it was lodged nine days ago, which means it was lodged before the standings closed, which means whoever wrote it knew where you were going to come on that sheet before you did." He looked up. "I can refuse you a room. I can refuse you the yard. I cannot refuse you the games, and I cannot take your name off a document that is not mine, and if I write to the capital about it I will be writing to men who will want to know why the First Master of the archipelago is troubled about one entry among four hundred, and I will have made the thing I am trying to make small, large."

Kael sat with his hands on his knees and ran it twice.

"Then somebody is above you," he said.

"Yes."

He had expected the man to soften it. Four years of doors with nothing written on them, and this one had a wall behind it and the master had let him walk straight into the wall without putting a hand out.

"That is the second thing," Severin said. "And the third is this, and I have been carrying it since the spring and I have chosen every single day not to say it to you, and I was wrong, so I will say it now."

He waited.

"In the spring you put an edge on a piece of wood in front of four hundred people," the master said.

Kael did not move.

"I have not asked you what it was. I am not going to ask you now." The old man's voice did not change and did not rise and stayed exactly where it had been, which was somehow worse than if it had done either. "You will notice that everybody on this island agreed within a quarter of an hour that the sun had been on the sea. That agreement was not luck. Four hundred people in that yard wanted an easy answer and were handed one and took it, and I let them take it, and I have let them keep it since." He turned his head and looked at the window. "I am telling you that somebody in that colonnade did not need an easy answer. He had a hard one already. And nine days ago a man wrote your name onto a sheet in the capital."

The rain went along the window again.

"You are twelve years old," Severin said, "and I would give a great deal not to be saying any of this to you, and I find that I am going to say it anyway, because the alternative is that you go down that hill this evening and work it out on your own on the road, and you would, and you would work out three quarters of it, and the missing quarter is the one that gets people hurt."

"Who is Halden," Kael said.

"A judge of the games."

"That's the sheet's answer."

"It is also mine," the master said, "because I have been asking since the first bell and it is the only answer anybody in my Temple has for me, and I do not like a man of whom that is true. He is not one of the great houses. He is not anyone's cousin. He arrived two springs early with a warrant that was correct, and he has walked my grounds for two years being pleasant to everybody, and I have looked at that warrant twice this morning and both times it was still correct." He unfolded his arms. "He is the empire, Kael. Not a house. Not a family with a grudge in it that can be traded with. The empire is not one thing that wants one thing — it is ten thousand men in ten thousand rooms, and any one of them who has a seal and a reason can reach into another man's school and take a boy off a wall, and there is no door to knock on, because it was all done correctly."

Kael looked at the two letters lying open on the desk and did not read them, which took something.

"You told me at the rock," he said, "that you were done carrying me."

"I said it at the gate, and I said it because you needed to walk into that court on your own feet." Severin came off the desk. "I am telling you now that whatever this is, you are not standing in it by yourself. Not you, and not your house. Whatever it costs, and whatever it takes, and however far it has to go, you have that from me, and you will have it in the spring, and you would have had it if you had never once climbed this stair."

Kael sat and looked at his own hands.

He had been given a great many things in that room over four years: a blank book, an empty column, a jar of honey that was never mentioned, a sentence about not-knowing that he had carried since he was ten. This was the first one he could not price at all.

"Go to letters," the master said. "You are late, and being late is a thing people remember about a boy on a list."

---

The judge was leaning on the rail above the yard at the end of the afternoon, in the place where the wall gave the best view of the stones, with his coat done up against the weather and his hands comfortable on the timber, and he was watching the second cohort spar, and he turned as Kael came past as though he had felt the air change.

"There he is," said Halden.

Kael stopped.

It was, he thought afterward, the one moment in the whole day when something might have gone another way, because he could have gone past. The road was there. The bell had gone. And he stopped, because the drawer had come open in the morning and it had been open all day and he could not walk past the only hand in the world that might have something to put in it.

"Congratulations," the judge said, and put his own hand out.

Kael looked at it.

"Ah," Halden said, and took it back without the smallest change anywhere in his face, and folded both hands on the rail again. "You have read the second column."

"Yes."

"Then say it. Go on. You have been carrying it up and down this hill since the bell and it will do you no good in your pocket."

"Why."

"Because it was a pity," said the judge.

He said it easily, in the voice that had made two years of corridors and doorways and accidental crossings feel like a warm room, and he said it looking out at the yard, where two boys of the second cohort were being stood up by the yard-master and made to salute each other again.

"Look at them," Halden said. "That is Amerien's second son and that is a Kaeth, and both of them will be entered, and neither of them will last the second day, and both of them have been entered since the week the games were announced, because their houses wrote a letter and paid a fee and the thing was done. Nobody asked whether they were any good. Nobody in the empire has ever asked that question about a boy with a name." He turned his head. "And four lines above them, on the same sheet, at four hundred to one against, there is a herder's son who has been three lines off the top of this Temple's boards for two years and cannot be entered by anybody, because there is nobody to write the letter."

"There's a provision," Kael said.

"There is a provision." The judge looked genuinely pleased, the way he looked pleased at everything. "You went and found it out. Of course you did. Yes. There is a small dusty provision in the games' law, put there by men who are all dead now, and it is used perhaps twice in a generation, and it exists exactly and precisely for the situation of a boy off a farm whose father cannot write his name." He spread one hand on the rail. "What a pity it would be, not to showcase the empire's finest."

Kael stood very still.

"You want to know what I get," Halden said. "Ask it."

"What do you get."

"Nothing at all, and I am aware that you will not believe that, and I would think less of you if you did." He shrugged, comfortably, the whole broad shape of him going up and coming down. "Here is the honest article. A judge who stands for a boy who wins is a judge who was right about something, in front of every house in the empire, in one week, once, and carried for the rest of his life. That is what I get. It is not a small thing and I am not going to pretend to you that it is nothing. But it does not cost you a grain, and it does not cost your master a grain, and it does not put one hand on you anywhere. It buys me a good week in the spring if you are what I think you are." He smiled. "And if you are not, I will have been wrong in public, which men in my position generally take care never to be. I priced it. I am content with the price."

"Take it off."

"I cannot."

"You put it on."

"I lodged it," the judge said, "and it went to the capital, and the capital sealed it, and the capital does not unseal things because a twelve-year-old on an island has changed his mind. Neither would you, if you had ever seen the room it is filed in." He said it without one grain of unkindness. That was the thing Kael kept getting stuck on. There was no cruelty in it anywhere. "I will tell you the truth, since you have asked me for it twice and got it twice. If I could take it off, I would not. You do not want me to. You have wanted this since before I ever came to this island, and everybody within a mile of you knows it, and the only reason you are standing here with that face on is that somebody has forbidden you, and you have not told me who, and I have not asked, and I am not going to."

The yard-master's voice came up off the stones, flat and even, counting a cadence.

"Two years I have been on this rock," Halden said, "and in two years I have watched them price you at nothing four separate ways, and every single time they were wrong, and every single time you let them stay wrong, which is a discipline I have never in my life seen in a child and which I would like, someday, to understand." He straightened off the rail and worked his shoulders, an ordinary man at the end of an ordinary day. "In the spring they will not be able to. That is all I have done. I have taken away their ability to go on being wrong about you, and I have not asked you for anything, and I am not going to ask you for anything now."

He pulled his coat straight.

"Go home, lad," he said kindly. "It is coming on to rain again and you have a long road."

---

He made the man at the second bend.

It was not a thing he did on purpose. His head was full of the four lines, the second column, the two letters lying open on a desk, *he is the empire, Kael*, and none of that had anything to do with his feet, and somewhere underneath the thinking the part of him that had been built first went on counting the way it always did.

At the bend above the village the road gave you two hundred strides of the way you had come. Kael had never in his life turned a corner without buying the corner first.

There was a man on it at about a hundred and fifty, walking.

That was the entire content of it. A man on a public road at the hour when men come off the water, in a wool coat the colour of every coat on this island, going the way half the island went.

Kael came round the bend without changing one thing about how he walked, and thought, in his mother's voice: *ask twice, with one thing changed.*

He stopped at the top of the rise and made a long slow business of a lace that did not need it, and counted forty, and went on.

At the next place the road looked back on itself, the man was at a hundred and fifty.

What happened in him then was not one thing, and he was ashamed of about half of it for years afterward.

The first half arrived at once and it was very nearly joy. Everything he had — every dark scree run, every count, every hour of being made into nothing while a boat sat off the coast — came up under him together and stood there ready, and it was enormous, and it was *fun*. He had been carrying a whole school around inside his body for years and not one single person had ever *needed* him to use it. Now somebody did. His chest was going like a boy running and he was not running, and he wanted, very badly, to be very good at this.

The other half came in underneath it about ten strides later and was the size of the mountain.

There was one road. It went up, and open, past the split rock, and it went to one door, and it had gone to that door every day of his life. Nobody had ever said out loud that a road works in both directions. Nobody had ever needed to.

*This house lives because it is not looked at.*

He had a school for this. He had never once been told what the school was for.

The losing took about four minutes and cost him nothing whatsoever, which was the part he could not afterward get over.

Where the road dipped through a fold, a walker behind you lost the top of your head for four seconds, and on the uphill side the bank gave out into scree, a long tilted field of loose grey stone that a man in town boots cannot cross quietly in the dark and that Kael had been running loaded, in the dark, with a count going, since he was eight years old. He used three of the four seconds. He went up eleven feet of bank on the outsides of his feet and lay down in the stones.

The man came along at the same unhurried pace he had kept for two miles and went by twenty feet under him: wool cap, wool coat, a man about his father's build and nothing whatever like his father's carriage, no badge, no colour, nothing on him anywhere that meant a single thing. Kael never saw the face and did not need it. He took the walk instead, the way he had taken Aurelian's to get to fourteen strides, and put it away in the place where he kept a six-oared boat and a dark stick going up to a face.

The man did not slow, and did not look up, and did not come back.

He had not been sent to follow a boy who could do this. Nobody had told him there was one.

The stillness game ended on his father's word and always had, and there was no one on that hillside to say *done*, so Kael set the rule himself: the lamps at the water, all of them, and a hundred after that. He held it long past any use, because being finished was not the same as being sure. Then he went home the wrong way: up over the shoulder, along the goat line, down onto the path above the split rock from the side that had no road on it, adding forty minutes to a walk he could have done blind.

He came at his own house from above and stopped and looked at it the way he had been taught to look at anything before giving it his weight. The lamp was lit. His mother's shape crossed the window and did not stop at it. The bar would be down, because the bar had been down every night of his life.

Some of it was still going in him. That was what he carried down the last of the hill: that a grown man had walked behind him to within two miles of his mother's door, and that he had beaten him without hurrying and enjoyed it somewhere a long way down — and that he was now going to have to go in and say all of it out loud to the two people who had built the part of him that enjoyed it.

He went down.
