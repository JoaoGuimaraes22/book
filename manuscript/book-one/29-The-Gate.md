# Chapter Twenty-Nine — The Gate

They came down off the rise into the back of it.

From up there it had looked like ground — a thing you could cross, given time and a line to take. Eleven strides in, the road stopped being a road. It became a surface that had people standing on it, shoulder to shoulder and back to chest, and the forward you got was the forward the bodies in front of you gave up, which was none.

He had never been inside people before.

His father had taught him crowds the way he had taught him scree and gorse and wet rock, which was as terrain, from above, at a distance, with a stick in the dirt. *Where does the weight go. Where is it thin. What is it standing on.* Nothing in any of that had mentioned the smell, or the heat coming off the backs of strangers in the cold, or what it is like to have four people touching you at once who do not know they are doing it.

His hand went inside the coat and found the knife and stayed there. He made it come out. It went back twice more before he stopped noticing.

"Kael."

"I'm here."

"Stay there."

Then the crowd changed, all down its length, the way a field of barley changes when the wind arrives at the far end of it.

He turned round.

He could not see the road below them; there were too many heads. He could see what the heads were doing. From somewhere back down the hill a piece of information was coming up through thirty thousand people, and it was travelling faster than anybody could walk, and it was arriving as a physical event — a lean, and then a press, and then a sound that had no words at the front of it and picked some up on the way.

"—coming up. They're coming up the road—"

"How far? *How far?*"

"Move. MOVE. Get *up* there—"

"There's nowhere to go, you stupid—"

Kael did the arithmetic while people came apart around him. The lane in the village was four hundred strides below the flat. He had come up it himself inside the hour. Fifteen men had needed a lane and a well to hold two children, and there were not fifteen coming.

"Twenty minutes," he said. "Perhaps thirty. They'll take the road, not the terraces. The road is faster and there's nothing on it to worry about."

Valeria was already looking at the wall.

"Then we go now."

"You can't go through this."

"I can."

"You can go through it," Kael said. "It won't be there afterwards."

She stopped, which cost her something to do, and looked at him properly for the first time since the top of the hill.

There was a version of this where she went up that road the way she had gone up the lane, and everything in front of her moved or was moved, and Kael had watched her do it to grown men with weapons an hour ago. Nobody standing between her and that wall had a weapon. Most of them had a child or a bundle or another person's arm.

"Show me," she said.

So he took her through it.

He had spent eleven years learning ground, and the ground was people, and it worked. The seams were where the seams always are: behind anything solid, because a cart or a wall or a fallen animal makes a pocket downstream of itself; along the ditch lip, where the footing is bad and the crowd thins out of ordinary caution; wherever somebody big had stopped moving, because a stopped man is a stone in a river and there is always slack behind a stone. He went low and he went sideways and he went at angles that were not toward the gate at all, and every one of them bought ten strides that going straight would not have bought.

He talked the whole way, which he had never done in his life.

"Excuse me. Excuse me. Coming past you — thank you — coming past—"

They let him. That was the part he had not expected and had no time to look at. He was a boy of thirteen with blood to the elbow and two short blades across his back, and people looked down at him and made a space that was not there a moment before, and most of them said nothing, and one man put a hand under his arm and lifted him bodily over a low wall and set him down and turned away without waiting to be thanked.

Twice he lost her and found her by the sound of the crowd rearranging itself.

Twice a woman asked him something and he had gone eight strides before he understood it had been a question.

Two hundred strides short of the wall the crowd stopped being a crowd and became a face of people, packed hard, pressed against the last of the open ground and not going anywhere, because the last of the open ground was where the men on the wall had put a line of spears and told everybody to stand back.

The wall went up out of it, cut stone, higher than the arena rail, with men on the top of it standing in the ordinary bored way men stand on walls.

The gate was shut.

It had been shut for some time. You could see that from how the people at the front had settled — nobody at a gate that has just closed sits down.

"They said they'd hold it," Kael said. "You said they'd hold it. Not shut it."

"Yes."

"Val."

"I heard you."

They got to the spear line the way he had got them everywhere else, and then the spear line was the end of it.

Valeria did not argue with the man in front of her. She stepped out of the press into the strip of open ground where nobody was allowed to be, which took a certain amount of doing, and she stood in it, and she let herself be seen.

She was thirteen years old and covered to the elbows and her hair was out of its binding on one side, and she stood in front of a wall of the empire holding two blades she had not put away since the arena, and she did not say a word.

It took about four seconds.

"On the left. *On the left* — that's Valeria Vel."

Somebody up on the wall said something to somebody else. A head went away and came back. The crowd behind them heard the word go along the top of the wall and did what a crowd does with a rumour of a door opening, which is lean.

The spear line dropped its points and leaned back into it and held, and the noise came up out of the ground and off the stone and there was a while where Kael could not hear anything at all.

Then the gate opened.

It opened the width of a person. Not a gate opening — a bar coming off and a leaf coming back about eighteen inches and stopping, with men behind it and their shoulders in it, and the whole weight of that hillside arriving against the front of the spear line in the same second.

The judge came out into the gap.

He was exactly as he had been in a corridor at the Temple two springs ago and in a judges' enclosure two days ago: broad, comfortable, grey at the temples, wearing the morning like a man who had come out to see about a delivery. Nothing about him hurried. Behind him, inside, there was light on stone and the ordinary sound of an ordinary street.

"Valeria Vel," he said. "Come through, please."

"There is a boy with me. It's Kael. He studies at Temple too."

"I know. Come through."

"He comes through with me."

"He does not." Halden said it the way you say a fact that has already been checked. He had raised his voice enough to carry and no further. "Look at what is behind you, and then look at this gap. It is the width of one person. It is going to be the width of one person for exactly as long as it takes you to walk through it, and then it is going to be shut, because if it is ever the width of two, thirty thousand people come through it and this wall falls at noon instead of tonight."

"Then let them through."

"No."

"There are *children* out here—"

"Yes," said Halden. "There are children in here as well. Come through, please, Miss Vel. I am going to ask you twice."

She did not move.

Kael saw the exact moment it happened, and he was the only person on that hillside who had the frame to read it, because he had spent a year of afternoons learning the contents of that girl's body and he knew what all of it meant.

Her chin came up. Her weight went down onto the front foot, the way it went down before she took a lane apart. And then something came off her face that Kael had never once seen there — not in a duel, not on the sand, not with a blade of his going into her; not when the herald read her name and the door shut behind her in the corridor.

Valeria Vel was angry.

"I was *sent* here," she said, and her voice did something. "My orders name this district. My orders name this *wall*. I have walked six miles with him and he took me through that" — the blade came up and swept the whole hill, thirty thousand people, and came back down — "and you will open it eighteen inches for a boy who weighs eight stone. You know what he is. You *entered* him. Your name is on his line."

"I did."

"Then open it."

"No," said Halden, and there was no more edge in it than there had been in any of the rest.

"I am not coming in."

"You are."

"I am *not*—"

He came out of the gap and took her by the arm above the elbow.

She set herself. Kael saw her do it — the weight going down into the stone, the thing she did, the thing that had held against grown men in a lane inside the hour and against four hundred years of packed road under her feet.

It did not matter.

She came through the gap the way a chair comes across a floor. She got one blade round and Halden had the wrist of it in his other hand before it was anywhere, and he was talking to her the whole time in a voice like a warm room, and none of it was cruel, and he did not appear to be hurrying.

"KAEL—"

He was moving before he decided to. He got to the gap. He got a hand on the edge of the leaf.

"Sir." He had never once called anybody sir in his life. It came out anyway. "Sir. Please. My mother is on the mountain."

Halden looked at him.

There were men putting their shoulders into the leaf and a girl of the Line of Vel being carried backwards up a lit street with her boots off the ground, and thirty thousand people arriving against a line of spears twenty feet behind him, and the judge stood in the gap and gave Kael the whole of his attention, which is what he had always done.

"I know," he said.

"She's having a child." Kael heard his own voice climb and could not get underneath it. "Four miles. The road on the other side is four miles to the mountain road and I can run it. I'm not asking to stay. I'll go straight through and out the other side and you can put a man on me the whole way, I don't care, I'll—"

"Kael."

"—I'll go out the north side, you don't even have to open this again, I can go over from the inside, there's nothing on the north side, I *looked*—"

"Kael."

He stopped.

"The road on the other side of this district," Halden said, "has been closed since the second hour. There is nothing on it going anywhere. If I put you inside this wall you will be inside this wall until it falls or until it doesn't, and it will not be four miles, it will be as long as it lasts. That is the truth and I am not going to dress it up for you. There is no version of this where you get to the mountain through here."

The leaf came back another inch under the men's shoulders.

"The First Master." Kael got it out fast, both hands on the wood now. "The First Master will stand for me. Send for him. He's — he was at the arena, he's coming here, everyone's coming here, he *promised*—" and it was out of his mouth before he heard it, and hearing it did not stop it, "—he said I wasn't standing in it by myself. Not me, and not my house."

Something went across the judge's face and did not stay.

"Yes," said Halden. "He would."

"Then send for him."

"He is not here."

"Then *wait*—"

"Kael." Halden took a breath. "I have nothing to give you. I am not going to pretend I am sorry in a way that costs me nothing, and I am not going to ask you for anything. Get off the road before they reach it. Go high and go east and do not be on any road at all."

"*Please.*"

The gate shut. The bar went across on the other side of it, and it was it.

---

He stood in front of it for a while.

Then he looked up, because there were people on the wall and one of them might be somebody, and that is what a boy does.

There were about forty men on that stretch. Behind them, he spotted some of his fellow students. And among them, Elarine Sedrane.

He saw her for about as long as it takes to see somebody. She was three heads along from a boy taller than the men beside him, with dark-gold hair. Both of them were looking down into thirty thousand people the way you look into thirty thousand people, which is at all of it and none of it.

Then one of them stopped.

Kael could not see her face. He was a hundred and forty strides out and below, with smoke coming across from the west, and he could not have told you at that distance whether her eyes were open. He knew it was her. She knew it was him. That was the whole of the information and there was not going to be any more of it, because the crowd came forward against the spears again, the man beside Kael went sideways into him and took him four strides down the hill, and when he got his feet back the piece of wall he had been looking at had different people on it.

He tried to look for them, and failed.

They were in and he was out.

That was all he had, and he took it, and what he built out of it was finished before he had gone twenty strides.

Whatever there had been between the three of them, it was weaker than that gate.

It was weaker than those walls.

He was glad they were alive. He found that out about himself in the middle of it and it did not change anything else.

---

Nobody at the wall had said a number, so he made one.

He took a section of the near ground where he could see the edges of it — from the ditch to the second olive terrace, about forty strides by thirty — and he counted a strip of it properly, multiplied, and checked the multiplication against a second strip because his mother had made him check every number he had ever produced in his life. Then he ran it back down the road and out over the shoulder of the hill by eye, allowing for the ground he could not see and being honest about it.

It came out between thirty-one and thirty-six thousand.

Then he continued.

Twenty minutes to the flat, which was now perhaps twelve. The road came up onto the shoulder in one place. The crowd began two hundred strides short of the wall and ran back down the hill for a quarter of a mile. There was no third gate. The terraces went up to the north and ended against the district's own wall; the ditches to the south went down into the olive ground and the olive ground went down to the cliff path and the cliff path went to the water, where there were ships.

He came to the answer he wanted to find the least.

He had found it, and it arrived complete, the way findings did to him. Not *it will be difficult* and not *many will not get out.* There was no arrangement of that ground, those numbers and that much time that got thirty-one thousand people off this hill, and there never had been.

He was the only person sitting on that hillside who knew it.

He looked at what was around him while the knowledge sat there being useless. A man with a bandaged hand had gone to sleep against his own knees. Two women were arguing quietly about whether a third woman should be made to drink something. Somebody's grandmother was being fanned with a hat. Further up, four men had got a plank out of a fence and were carrying somebody on it. They had been at it for an hour, and were going to go on, because putting him down was not a thing they were going to do.

None of them were doing anything wrong. That was the part. They were doing the correct things. They were queuing at a gate, and looking after each other, and waiting to be let in, which is what you do at a wall your own side built.

"You're bleeding, love."

He looked up.

She was somewhere past fifty. She had a shawl, one bundle, nothing else at all, and she was pointing at his shoulder with two fingers, at a cut he had taken at the wells and not looked at since.

"It's not mine," Kael said, which was true of most of him and not of that.

"It's yours." She got down beside him with the noise people that age make getting down, and she took the corner of the shawl off her own shoulders, and she wet it out of a stoppered bottle that was the only thing in the bundle worth anything, and she cleaned it. She did it briskly and not very well. She talked the whole time about somebody called Ilva who was not there and who she was quite sure had gone to her sister's.

She did not know who he was.

That was the thing he could not put anywhere. There was no house in it, no name in it, nothing to be got out of it. She had a bottle with about a third of a pint in it, she was going to be on this hill in twelve minutes, and she spent some of it on a boy she had never seen.

"There," she said. "Now don't pick at it."

"Thank you."

"Where's your mother?"

"On the mountain," Kael said.

"Well." She patted his knee and got up. "You want to get on, then."

He got up too. It came out of him with no shape on it at all, because he had spent the last quarter of an hour establishing that there was nothing to be done and had not once thought of saying any of it out loud.

"They're coming up the road. They'll be on this hill in ten minutes and that gate is not going to open again. Go down the ditch on the south side, get into the olive ground, keep going, and don't stop at the cliff path."

She looked at him for a moment with the bottle still in her hand.

"That's kind of you, love," she said.

Then she put the bottle away in the bundle, worked her way four steps back into the press, to the place where she had been standing before, and stood in it.

He watched her do it. He had given her the only thing he owned that was worth anything, correct to the minute and checked twice, and she had thanked him for it the way you thank a child, and gone back to her place, because the gate was at the front of it.

---

He went east.

He went the way Halden had told him to go.

Getting out of that crowd was easier than getting into it. Everything was pointed at the gate; nothing was pointed away from it, and a body moving against a stationary mass is not fighting anything.

He got up onto the high ground and the whole of it was underneath him.

The wall. The road up from the village with its two dry-stone sides. The crowd, which from up here had gone back to looking like ground. The village behind and below, burning in four places now, not three. The headland beyond that with the smoke standing off it. The sea past the headland with the ships on it.

And on the road, coming up out of the village onto the flat, small and unhurried and in no formation at all, the front of it.

Then he turned the other way, to look at what he was walking into.

The ground east of the district went up in terraces to the olive line, broke into scrub and goat ground, and beyond that, standing in the haze with its top in cloud, was the mountain.

It was not empty.

He counted eleven fires, and then stopped counting them and began reading them instead, because a fire out there was a house, and nobody puts a house down at random. The low ones stood where the water stood. The ones going up the shoulder stood along a track. Eleven houses, spread across four miles of ground that had perhaps thirty on it, which meant they had been at it for about an hour and were working from the water inland.

And between the fires, in ones and twos and fours, going along the field walls the way water goes along a gutter, there were men.

They were not marching. They were finishing scraps.

On the eastern water there were more boats coming in.

He stood on the terrace wall with his hands hanging and read the ground between himself and his mother, because reading ground was what he did, and it read the same the whole way to the haze.

Everything from here on was through them. There was no line to take that was not through them. He looked for one for a long time, because looking for one was also what he did, and the ground did not offer him anything, and he was going to go anyway.

Behind him, the faculty nobody in his family had ever built or trained or mentioned once in eleven years put out a hand.

He knew every one of the arguments. He had made them all sitting against that ditch wall and every one of them was correct. He could not fight three hundred men. He could not move thirty-one thousand people. He could not open a gate that masons had built specifically so that men could not open it. Nothing he did on this hill changed one single thing that was going to happen on it, his mother was six miles off with a child coming down.

All of it was true. He turned it over twice looking for the flaw, and there was no flaw in it, and he went anyway with the thing still holding on to his sleeve.

He put the mountain in front of him and started.

The sound behind him changed about four minutes later.

He did not turn round. He had known since the ditch wall what it was going to sound like, because he had done that arithmetic too, unasked, the way he did all of them.

What he had not done, having never in his life held the information required for it, was work out how long it would go on.

It went on.

It did not arrive in a piece. It came the way rain comes onto a roof, with no part of it being the loud part, and every so often something came up over the top of it that had one person inside it.

And the cold voice, which had counted two in a settlement yard when he was nine and five in a lane before noon and did not care in the slightest what it was counting, started on the hillside behind him.

Thirty-one thousand. Thirty-six at the outside. Gone. Forever.
