# Chapter Eight — Enrollment

The gate stood open, and it was meant to.

That was the first thing the Temple taught him, before any master spoke: a door could stand open on purpose, all day, and be right. He walked through it behind the master into a court the size of the upper meadow, walled in stone that had been asked to be flat and had agreed. The court was full of the sound he had heard from the road — voices, many, loose, weaving — and his eyes went out and came back with the count before he had asked them to. Two dozen children crossing on nine errands. Three clusters holding ground. Colonnade north and east; the walls gave nowhere. His breath found the long shape. None of it showed.

The air was crowded here too, but crowded differently than the one other crowded air he knew: ink somewhere, and wet stone, and bread in quantities no one family could want, and under everything the smell of many many people. His body checked it the way you check a pail lid, and let it pass.

"The roll first," the master said. "Then I am done carrying you."

---

The roll lived in a long room off the first court, in a book the size of a door, in the keeping of a clerk with ink on the side of his hand like a scar he renewed daily. Ahead of Kael a boy was being entered, and the boy's father stood behind him in a coat worth more than the goat, saying the name for the clerk to take down, and the name went on and on. It had the boy's own name in it, and then a name for honor, and then a name for the house, and the pen walked the whole length of it across the page like a fence being built around something valuable.

Kael listened the way he listened to a new language. So that was what the chains were for. You could hear, inside the name, everything the boy came with — the house, the coat, the fence around the whole of it. When the master had asked him, at the split rock, what he was *of*, he had had nothing to put there. He had thought, then, that nothing was a lack, like a missing tooth. Standing in the roll-room he understood it better: nothing was also a thing the pen would have to write.

His turn came. The clerk did not look up.

"Name."

"Kael." He heard his own voice put the name down plainly, a stone on a table, and then, because the room asked and the coat was on, he wore the rest of it: "From the mountain. The First Master brings me books." Which was true, and carried the rest.

The pen took *Kael* and then stood, patient as a heron, over the long empty stretch where the other names had kept going.

"He is entered under my sponsorship," the master said, from the doorway, already half gone. "My charity case. Leave the space as it is. Empty is true."

The pen came down and made the emptiness official with a small line, the way you close an account. And Kael saw, because his eyes did what they always did, that the line took the clerk less time than any single name of the boy before him, and that two children waiting behind him had heard the whole of it, *charity case* and the line and all, and were already telling it with their faces to a third.

The master left without a farewell. That was its own kind of lesson. On the mountain, the master's leaving was the end of the best day of the year. Here he went the way weather went. The Temple closed around the space where he had been, and Kael stood in it alone — entered, blank where the fences were, the day not even at its noon.

---

They put him with the ten-year-olds, in rooms.

The rooms were the strangest part of the morning. The table became a market when his mother said so; the scree was a school because his feet were on it. Here the school was nailed in place — benches, a lectern, a wall of slates — and the lesson came to you, at its hour, like the tide. Boys and girls his own age filed in and draped themselves on the benches with the particular boredom of people who own a thing, and Kael sat where a space was, and learned quickly to be furniture, which was one of his oldest schools.

History came before noon, and history nearly undid him.

At home history was rationed. It came in scraps, sideways, and never — never once in his life — on asking. Here a master with a chronicle open on the lectern *poured* it, reign after reign, in quantities that would have lasted his mother a decade. Around him the heirs of the story dozed on their fists. Kael sat with his hands flat on the bench and took all of it, every reign, and had to remind himself, twice, to blink like furniture blinks, because the hunger in him had come all the way up to his face.

No one had ever answered him on asking. Here they answered before he asked. He filed the strangeness of that where he filed everything, and under the strangeness, far under, something else went by — small, quick, like a shape running a treeline — and was gone before he could turn to it.

Between the rooms, crossing the second court in a line of ten-year-olds, he saw a master going the other way with a streak of white through his dark hair, one clean seam of it, like quartz through rock. Nobody looked at it. So Kael didn't either.

The last room before the meal was natural philosophy, and the natural philosopher was called Priscian. Priscian was old in a different way than the master was old: not worn to what mattered but stuffed to the seams, a man with too many books inside him and the bindings straining. He took the lectern the way Kael's mother took the table, as territory, and what he lectured on, that first day, was the smallest thing in the world.

"The minima," Priscian said, and wrote it on the great slate in letters a hand high. "The smallest grains of the world. Division has a floor, gentlemen — split the log to splinters, the splinter to dust, and beneath the eye's failure the world is still made of pieces, and the pieces have a smallest, and the smallest is where the art lives. All matter carries them. All matter *is* carried by them. The mage is one who feels the grains as the hand feels warmth through cloth — not seeing, mark me, feeling — and having felt them, works."

Kael sat very still, and this time the stillness was not furniture.

He knew this. He had known it for a year, the way he knew the stairs in the dark. And he had never once had a word for it, because she gave that school no words at all. She had given him the pail, and the wind, and the fire's — the *feel* of things, where they carried, and here was an old man in a room nailing a name to it, hand-high, in chalk. The word arrived over the feeling like a roof arriving over a room he had lived in all his life, and the word was not hers, and he thought, without meaning to, of the master on the road: *their names for things will be new to you, and that is convenient, because it is even true.*

Priscian lectured, and Kael attended him the way he attended the sea.

The trouble came at the end, and he brought it on himself. Priscian, closing, threw a question at the benches: "What, then, is flame? Anyone. What is fire?"

The benches offered him what benches offer.

"It's hot," a boy said, and got the quiet agreement such answers earn.

"It's bright."

"It is the minima in haste," said a third, who had clearly been fed the doctrine at home and wore it like his coat. "Quickened past their nature by the working of the art, whereupon the fiery nature—"

"Whereupon," Priscian said, "you will tell me what nature they had before they hastened." The boy did not. Priscian took the silence with the face of a man tasting thin soup. And the itch came up in Kael, the old one, the deep one, because a question was standing in the room with no one under it, and a question from a master was a lifted stone —

"It's the wood being eaten," Kael said.

The room turned. He heard it turn, felt it on his skin, and kept going, because the stone was up and he was under it now. "Fire's an eater, but it's picky. It takes the fat of the wood and leaves the bone — that's the ash, the bone. And it doesn't start everywhere the same. It starts where the wood is —" and he heard, coming up the path of his own sentence like someone he recognized at a distance, a word of his mother's, *lazy*, her word, from her school, and he stepped around it the way you step around a board that creaks — "where the wood is weakest. Where the load of it sits. You can watch it choose."

Silence, one breath of it, with a new temperature.

"You can watch it choose," Priscian repeated, slowly, the way you repeat a thing to keep it still while you look at it. "Who taught you to watch fire, boy?"

"No one," Kael said. "We have a fire."

It was true, and it carried the rest, and it made the benches laugh — a short crop of laughter, many throats at once, and his breath went long on its own and his hand found the strap of his bag, near where nothing hung, and none of it showed. But Priscian did not laugh. Priscian looked at him for a moment the way the master had looked at him in the yard, the first day, unhurried, and said, "Everyone has a fire," in a voice that meant the opposite, and let the benches go.

Kael filed the whole of it walking out: the too-far answer, the turned room, the one crop of laughter, and the way the girl at the end of the second bench — dark-haired, straight-backed, with a face that gave out nothing it hadn't priced first — had not laughed. Her stillness had changed when he spoke; that was all. Everyone else in the room he had learned nothing about. About her he had learned one thing, and it was the only thing all day he knew how to read.

---

The meal was in a hall that held more people than he had ever seen under one roof, and he did his eating the way he did his blinking, at the local speed, and it was there that the golden ones came to look at him.

He knew the leader before the leader spoke, the way you know the lead goat: by where the others put their feet. A boy of eleven or twelve, tall for it, built a year ahead of his age, with dark-gold hair and a chain of a name Kael had heard twice already that morning traveling ahead of its owner — Aurelian Corvane. Kael's body priced him on the way over: unspent strength, carried high, the stand of a boy who had never once been put on the ground by anything. And the table priced him too, in its own language: room arriving around him where no one had moved, so that he crossed the hall the way weather crosses water, announced by the surface. He came with four behind him and stopped across the table with the pleasantness of someone doing a thing he has done before and enjoys.

"The charity case," Aurelian said, to his four, presenting him. Then, to Kael, kindly, the way you speak to something in a pen: "So what does a herder study up a mountain? Goats?"

"Say yes," one of the four said, low, to the others. "Please say yes."

And Kael — because a question was a question, and this one had an answer — considered it honestly and answered it honestly. "Yes. Goats teach a lot. Where they'll put their weight, what they'll spook at, what they can eat that you can't." He thought a moment more, because the answer was still coming, and gave the rest of it: "And weather, and snares, and mending. And letters, from my mother."

The laughter came down the table like a wave down a beach — many throats, one animal — and his body did its arithmetic under it, breath long, eyes out and back, the hand quiet, and the arithmetic came home with a strange sum, the same sum it had been finding all day and now finally showed him whole: *nothing here is heavy.* The boy across the table carried no weight anywhere it could fall from. His four were the same. And Kael was still there when the laughter died, unmoved the way the split rock was unmoved, not because he was brave — he could not have said what he was — but because his body, which made these judgments without him, had judged, and declined to spend anything.

Aurelian's face changed by a degree. Whatever he had come to collect, it had not been paid, and he was not old enough to name what had been paid instead. "It talks," he said, to his four, a little too late and a little too loud, and took them off down the hall, and Kael went back to the local speed of eating and filed the change in the boy's face next to the change in the room's temperature that morning, two entries, same drawer, a drawer with no name on it yet.

"That," said a voice beside him, arriving along the bench together with its owner — a soft-built, round-faced boy, quick-eyed, whose hands were already talking before the rest of him sat down — "was Aurelian Corvane, and you shouldn't answer him, or you should always answer him, I honestly don't know which is worse for you, but it was wonderful. Ostrian Vessaline." The boy said his own house's name quickly, the way you step over a known bad board, and put out his hand. Kael took it, because you took what was offered with both hands or not at all, and filed, in the taking, that everything the boy wore was slightly too new: none of it had learned him yet, the way a tool new from the maker has no hand worn into it. "Everyone says Oss. You should too, it's faster."

"Kael."

"I know. Everyone knows. You're the day's whole news; last week it was a boat with a two-headed fish, that's the standard you've beaten." And Oss talked, the hands drawing the Temple in the air while the mouth drew it in words. He talked the way the downhill language ran, all its length without a stone in it: who the four behind Corvane were, and which houses mattered and which thought they did, and what the masters were called behind the masters' backs, and that the girl who didn't laugh in natural philosophy was Elarine Sedrane, "and don't bother, she's priced everyone in this Temple and no one's met it yet" — and Kael listened the way he listened to rain on the roof, learning the shape of the country by where the water ran. It cost the boy nothing to talk, that was plain; the words came off him like heat off a wall. It was also plain — filed, not judged — that no one else had sat down.

---

He left when the light said leave, because the light was the law now.

The road home ran the day backward: the gate, the shoulder of the coast, the village drawing itself up the slope roof by roof — downshore a cluster of roofs stood among the others with its smoke standing up like all the rest, and he did not look at it longer than he looked at anything — and then up, and up, the world going quiet by degrees, shedding its smells one by one until the air was one clean thing again.

His father was at the lower pens.

Kael saw him from the last bend and filed it without weighing it: the lower pens, at dusk, where the evening work was not. The rail his father was mending was a rail Kael had watched him mend in the spring; it did not need a man. But there the man was, at the bottom of everything, with the light going long and red behind him, working on a mended thing, and when Kael came up the path his father straightened and looked at him — at the whole of him, once — and Kael said, "Evening," and his father nodded, one nod, the size of every other nod, and fell in beside him, and they walked up through the pens together with the tools swinging in his father's hand, and neither of them said anything, and his father's pace, the whole way, was exactly his.

His mother was in the doorway, and the good smell came out past her, and on the table there was the seed-bread — the feast bread, the one that rose for the master and no one else — and Kael stopped in the door and looked at it, and looked at her.

"Sit," she said. "Eat. And tell me."

So he told her. And here was the strange thing: she asked. All his life the questions had run one way, downhill from him, and she had answered until his head was full, and tonight she sat across from him with her hands still and her supper cooling and asked.

"The rooms first. Where does the light come in? Where did they put you?"

"Wherever there was a space. The lessons stay in their rooms — you go to them. The room *is* the lesson," he said, trying the thought aloud for the first time, and something small and pleased crossed her mouth, and she asked on: how they taught the letters, whose hand they taught; what the philosopher said the world was made of; and what the other children — and stopped, and started differently — "And what are the masters called?"

And he answered and answered, he talked the way Oss talked, he heard himself doing it, and she listened with her whole body. Once, when he was telling the part about the book the size of a door, and the pen, and how his name had gone in short with the empty place after it, her eyes went to his father's, one glance, quick as the shuttle of a loom, and came back, and Kael read the glance the way he read everything and filed it wrong: *they are checking I did it correctly.* Their faces through the whole of his talking were the faces of people at work, attentive, weighing. And then he came to the fire question, and what he had answered, and that the room had laughed and the old man hadn't, and his father made a sound.

Kael stopped. It had been short, low, one note — and his father's hand came up and covered the lower half of his own face, thumb and knuckle, a man rubbing his jaw at the end of a day, and above the hand his eyes had gone bright and creased at the corners, and his mother was not looking at either of them, she was looking at the bread, cutting it, and her mouth was pressed the way she pressed it when the bird got the song wrong on purpose, and the lamplight was moving on both their faces.

"You told a room of the empire's sons," his mother said to the bread, evenly, "that everyone has a fire."

"We do have a fire," Kael said, and his father stood up and went to see about the goat, who did not need seeing about, and was some time in coming back.

It was the best supper Kael could remember, and he could not have said what had been in it besides bread.

Only at the end, gathering the bowls, his mother stood a moment behind him and put her hand on his head — not the resting it did after lessons, but cupped, the whole hand, the way you hold something you are carrying across ice — one breath, two — and took it away, and said, in the voice of her ordinary evenings: "The lamp's lit. Bring the slate. Show me what this philosopher of yours thinks the world is made of."

And that was how the third school of the day began, and how the days had their shape from then on: the dark before light his father's: the wasters, the spear, the bow at distance, the body's school moved earlier and grown no smaller; the light itself the Temple's; and the lamplight his mother's, her schools gone to evening the way the birds went to the eaves, the slate between them, her questions running under the Temple's answers like water under a boat. He was ten. He crossed the whole of his known world twice a day, at its two speeds, wearing its two coats, and in the loft, past the edge of sleep, on the good nights, he could hear them below him, the two voices, low, trading the day back and forth — his day; he could tell by the shape of it — and the sound went up at the ends, and put things down, and he fell asleep inside it the way you fall asleep near a fire, in the winter, when the door is holding.
