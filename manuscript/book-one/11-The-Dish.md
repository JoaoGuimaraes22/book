# Chapter Eleven — The Dish

The second autumn began with the dish.

He was eleven, and the Temple had decided it was time to learn what its children were. The cohort was walked into the first court on a grey morning and formed into the roll's order, and at the head of the court stood a table Kael had never seen: an iron dish the size of a cartwheel hub, and around it, in a half-ring, the plain things of the world — a candle, a bowl of water, a box of dry sand, a green sprig in a pot, a copper rod on a cloth. The yard-master stood behind the table. Priscian stood to the side with the registry clerk, and the clerk had the book, and the book was open.

"Your art has been leaning since before you knew you had one," the yard-master said, to the line, in the voice worn smooth by being true. "Today it says its name. You will spend one grain — one — at the dish, and we will watch what answers. The dish does not flatter and it does not lie." He looked down the line, unhurried. "What settles, settles. The dish only asks early."

They went up one at a time, in the roll's order, and the Art said its name in each of them, loudly or in a whisper.

For most of the bench it was a whisper — a gutter in the candle, a shiver on the water, readings the masters bent close to catch and the clerk wrote small. But the leanings that had shown early showed now like weather. The river-province girl's bowl climbed its own sides and stood in a ring, and she looked at it with her sleeve pressed to her mouth. Oss's sand rose in a slow mound and settled, like a held breath let go, and Oss beamed down at it as at a relative. "Earth," said the clerk. Lucan Drusane's grain went into the air itself: the dust on the dish lifted and wiped itself outward in one neat ring, a lane swept clean, and the clerk wrote air, and Lucan stepped back with his posture already wearing it.

Then Elarine Sedrane went to the table, and the Temple learned what her discipline had been holding.

She spent her grain the way she did everything, exactly, and the candle did not gutter. It stood. The flame rose off the wick in one straight governed column as tall as her forearm, bright enough to put color on the iron, and held there, not wavering, the size she had set it — and then she shut it off clean, all at once, the way a door closes. The court was quiet. Everyone had priced Sedrane small, because she was small, and neat, and never spent. The flame said what the purse said: there was a great deal in there, and it was all obedient.

"Fire," said the clerk.

Aurelian Corvane's fire surprised nobody and was cheered anyway — the wax of the whole candle going up in the soft orange sheet the yard remembered, the yard-master's cloth coming down over it with the patience of long habit. Two fires in one cohort, the benches whispered, and both of them Corvane's tier; the order of the world held.

Then the clerk read the next name, and it was his.

Kael went to the dish through a court that had already decided what it would see. The mountain's candle was a settled matter; the whisper had explained him years ago. He stood where the others had stood, and found the dish and its half-ring of the world already familiar — water, fire, sand, the sprig; he had spent threads into all of them in a loft and a garden nobody here could imagine — and he did what he had done at the workings bench for two years. He felt first. Then he spent his grain.

The crack was small and white and absolutely clear.

It came off his fingers and went to the copper rod like a thing going home — one thin stitch of light, a hand's width, there and gone, with a sound like a knuckle against glass. Kael stood looking at his own hand, and in his chest something opened like a door onto a room he had owned all his life without the key: the dry-wool spark. The little winter crackle that had answered before the resonance was fully made, that had waited for its name to be said. It had been saying its own, all along.

At the book, the clerk's pen paused over the word, having so little habit of it.

"Lightning," said the yard-master, for him.

Kael walked back to the line with his face at the court's temperature and his heart doing something entirely different, and the whisper was already remaking itself behind him — lightning, said the benches, lightning — because lightning was rare, rare enough that no one in the cohort had ever seen it declared, rare enough that the old whisper had to be rebuilt to hold it. Somebody's blood in that line somewhere had been a sneer for two years. He heard it said again now, behind him, in a new voice. It had stopped being an insult and started being a theory.

He carried it up the mountain that evening the way you carry a coal in a pot.

He poured the day at the lamp — the dish, the half-ring, the column of Sedrane's fire, the white stitch off his own fingers — and his mother listened with her whole body, and asked her close exact questions, what the masters said and what the clerk wrote and who stood where. His father sat with the harness strap he was mending and did not stop working it, except once. Kael did not see what his hands did, because he was talking. He saw the strap lie still for a breath, and then go on being mended.

"Lightning," his mother said, the way you check the last knot of a load.

"Lightning." He heard his own voice do something around the word, and did not try to stop it. "It's rare. The clerk's pen didn't know how to write it. It — of everything at that dish, it's the only thing that ever answered me like it wanted to."

His parents looked at each other — one look, level, like a weight being set down after a long carry. His father put the strap aside.

"Mine as well," his father said. "Lightning."

The room did what a room does when a wall shows a door in it. Kael sat very still. His father held up one hand, palm cupped, and for a breath nothing — and then a thin white crack stitched itself across the hollow of it, twin to the one at the dish, there and gone, with the same small glass-knuckle sound. His father looked at it the way he looked at weather, and picked the strap back up.

"Air," his mother said. No demonstration came with it. She poured his milk. "You will not ask the next hundred questions tonight."

He did not. He sat between his two parents — two Resonants, who had never once in eleven years shown him the Art, whose one school of it had come to him in drops, small, smaller than you can — and the whole of his childhood turned over once in his head and lay back down in a different position. The telling had been given the way her tools were given: one tool, whole, on the day his element got its name, and nothing said about the years it had waited in the drawer. He slept the sleep of a boy who had been told by the world itself that he was something, and that he was theirs — and below him, past the edge of that sleep, the two voices traded the day long into the dark.

---

The yard changed within a fortnight, because the empire did not teach its children the Art in order to watch them wrestle.

The measure was drawn fresh and wider. Sand barrels came to stand at its corners. The declared were taught the stance the fencing-masters sold — weapon hand and art hand, the waster forward, the off-hand open and empty at the shoulder, for the Art — and the yard-master walked the line adjusting elbows exactly as he had always adjusted them, as if fire were a kind of footwork. The rules were three, and he said them once: nothing above the shoulders. Nothing the healer cannot mend by supper. And his own presence, which was not called a rule but was the largest of them: he stood the duels the way the split rock stood the path.

The first resonance duels of the second cohort were, mostly, bad. Fire that missed. Air that shoved its own man. A girl who spent her whole purse in the first exchange and had to sit down on the ground, grey-faced, and be walked through the purse lecture again from the beginning, kneeling. Kael watched every one of them from the line the way he had once watched the sea, and the itch in him was awake the whole time, because this was new, all of it — the Art walking around in bodies, making mistakes, having costs — and new things could be read.

Elarine Sedrane went to the well before the pairings on a rain-grey morning and spoke to the yard-master, three words, maybe four. The yard-master looked at her the way he looked at the sky. Then the flat voice called: "Sedrane. The charity case."

The yard slowed down the way it had slowed for Drusane, and differently. Aurelian came off the rail to watch, his four arranging themselves. Lucan stood at the edge, very still, face already at its sums. Behind Kael, Oss said, low, to nobody, "Oh no."

She came to the middle with her waster in one hand and the off-hand open at her shoulder, and made the taught salute — and before its echo finished, her open palm was burning.

She initiated: a flat fan of flame sweeping from her palm at knee height, crossing the lane his next step wanted, so that the step went elsewhere, and her wrist was already turning, and the elsewhere was crossed too. She walked him. Sweep after sweep, unhurried, each the size it needed to be, cutting his lanes like a woman closing shutters, and the heat of them reached his face from three strides off. The rain hissed into her fans and took nothing from them. Between sweeps the fire did not go out. It stood at her palm, a handspan of flame burning on nothing at all — no wick, no wood, rain falling past it — the way a lamp burns when somebody is paying for it and intends to keep paying all night. Her breathing had not changed since the salute.

Kael gave ground and read. She had watched every duel of the fortnight from the same line he had, and it was all here. When he slid off-lane the way he had against Drusane, her line had already turned to meet it — the answer bought before he had asked the question. When he feinted at the gap by the north barrel she declined it and spent nothing, her eyes doing over the flames what his own were doing: pricing. That gap stayed open exactly long enough to be almost trusted, and when his weight leaned toward it, it closed with a sweep of flame that scorched the air where his chest would have been.

He went through the next gap because it was that or the barrels, and paid for it: the fan that closed it caught him across the shoulder — a wet-cloth slap of heat, cloth scorching, pain arriving real and entire. The yard made its sound. Kael's count did not break. He filed the pain the way he filed weather, at a distance that had taken two years to build, and he took the burn's price for the burn's information: her fans rose no higher than his ribs. Nothing above the shoulders. She fought inside the rules the way she answered inside the doctrine. Correct, all the way down.

Fire resonance is bad close. Everyone knows it the way everyone knows the sea is wide; she knew it best of all, and had spent the whole fight making sure close never happened. So he bought close with the two smallest threads he owned.

The first went into the ground — not across it. He could no more set the Art down at a distance than anyone in that yard could. He put it down through his own boot. The ground was touching him; the ground was touching her; it was one connected thing the whole way between them, and a thread spent down through his heel ran along the packed dirt the way sound runs along a beam, to the one place her lead foot kept returning to. Her reset came down — the same reset, the third time, exact as everything about her — onto a thumb's depth of dirt gone loose as garden soil, and rain-slick. Her ankle wrote the surprise before her face did; the reset became a stumble the size of half a step.

The second thread went into the back of his own hand, into the rain that stood on it, as he closed and struck at her face — a strike that was never going to land, thrown slow enough to be read, because she read everything. She read it. Her guard came up correct; her eyes tracked his hand all the way in — and the rain on it flashed to steam. It burst off his knuckles in a white breath, into her face, scalding — a kettle's worth, no more — and for half a breath Elarine Sedrane was blind and stung and her weight was on a foot that had stopped being ground.

The waster took her below the ribs, flat and hard, and swept through her planted leg, and she went down onto the rain-dark yard with the fight knocked sideways out of her — and came up onto an elbow with her teeth showing and her off-hand already rising, and the air over her palm had begun to shiver with heat when the yard-master's voice arrived like the bar of a door.

"Enough."

Everything stopped. The rain fell into the measure. Kael stood with his sleeve smoking faintly and his shoulder screaming quietly, and Elarine sat propped in the dirt with a red bloom coming up across her cheekbone and her governed column of a temper banked back down by visible effort, one notch at a time.

"The match is the charity case's," the yard-master said, flat, to the clerk-of-the-yard, and then, to the line, in the voice of a man adjusting an elbow: "Sedrane fought the best duel this yard has seen this fortnight. She lost it to the ground and a kettle. Go and think about that." And he called the next pair.

The laughter came late, and wrong-footed, and less sure of itself than it wanted to be — a girl put down by the charity case was a verdict, but the yard had seen the fans, and the column at the ceremony, and the yard was no longer entirely sure what it had seen since. Aurelian Corvane, at the rail, did not laugh at all. Kael filed that without a place to put it. And Lucan Drusane watched Kael's smoking sleeve with his face doing an arithmetic that had stopped liking any of its numbers a long time ago, and added the line anyway.

They ended side by side at the healer's bench, which nobody had planned, on the plank worn smooth by generations of the mended, his shoulder and her cheek taking the same salve out of the same pot.

"I had you priced," she said, to the wall in front of them. "Range. Pace. The rules. The wet." Each word set down like a weight proved false.

"You did," Kael said. "You read the strike right, too. That's why the steam worked." He considered whether to say the rest of it, and said it, because it was true and she had paid for it: "I aimed it at your reading."

Her head turned. The red bloom on her cheek was going to be a bruise, and her eyes over it priced him for a long moment the way she priced everything — looking for the gloat in it, finding none, recalculating.

"The ground," she said. "That was earth. Your dish said lightning."

"The dish said what's loudest. The others still answer, if you understand what you're asking them to do. Small." He looked at his hands. "Everything I do is small."

"Hm," she said, in the voice of someone declining, on principle, to be fascinated. Then she looked back at the wall, and Kael looked at her looking, and something went wrong in his chest on the way to nothing at all.

He had read faces all his life — for weather, for danger, for the size of the trouble he was in. He had never yet stood this close to one and lost his footing on it: pale, severe, one cheek ruined by his own steam, a stray lock of ink-dark hair come loose from its binding and ignored as if it had personally failed her — and his chest filed a report he had not asked it for, and when she said, "Tomorrow you will show me the ground trick," his answer came out in the wrong order.

"Yes. The — after the yard. If you show me the fans. Slowly. I want to know what having that much fire to spend is like, deciding — how you decide what not to spend." He rebuilt the sentence the way he rebuilt a knot. "You have reasons for everything you do. I want to learn the reasons."

It was the longest thing he had ever said to anyone at the Temple, and she priced it, visibly, and it came up honest.

"Tomorrow," Elarine Sedrane said. "Not here." And she took her salved cheek off the bench and away down the colonnade, no thanks given, nothing agreed, and an arrangement existed between them the way weather exists, without anyone having signed it.

That evening he poured the day at the lamp, most of it. The duel he gave whole — his mother's questions ran close and exact over the ground-thread and the steam, and twice her stillness changed at the same place, the way still water changes when something passes under it far down. One part he did not pour, because he could not find the handle on it: the moment at the bench when his words had come out in the wrong order. He filed it, in the end, on the shelf with the mist — the shelf of things that answered before he had finished understanding the question. The spark had lived there two years, and the spark had turned out to be his own element calling him by name. He fell asleep without wondering what that meant for the other things on the shelf.

---

By the cold term the hall had rearranged itself, the way halls do, without anyone moving a bench. The story of the duel wanted telling, and Oss was the best teller alive, and the middle of the hall was where telling paid — so that was where Oss mostly was now, his hands drawing fans of fire in the air over the tables, giving the yard back to itself with better timing. Kael ate at the bench's end and watched the performance the way he watched weather, and did not judge it. The seat beside him stayed Oss's. It just stood empty more than it had.

But the sea-wall stayed theirs. That was the thing about Oss that Kael filed and kept filed: the middle of the hall got the timing, and the wall got the truth.

"Two houses have asked my father about you," Oss said, out at the beacon stump, with the gulls wheeling on the grey and a stolen pastry divided two ways. He said it low and fast, the way you hand something over while it is hot. "Don't ask me which — no, ask me, I'll tell you this much: Corvane wasn't one of them. Houses with lightning somewhere back up the line, both of them. They asked what the charity case's sponsorship costs. Whether the First Master holds you or the Temple does. What your mother's name is."

The last one went through Kael like the cold. "Why my mother's name?"

"Because that's where blood-theories go, mountain boy." Oss pulled his cloak tighter, and looked, for a moment, entirely unlike a boy at his trade. "Lightning is rare. Rare gets explained. A herder with a rare art is a story with a hole in it, and holes get filled — with a name if they can find one, with a better story if they can't. My grandfather bought a name; I know how this works from the inside. They will not ask you anything. They'll ask around you. They already are."

"Why tell me?"

Oss opened his mouth for the joke, and set it down. "Because my father would sell them the answers," he said. "If he had them. It isn't even wickedness. It's just the trade." He shrugged, the way he stepped over his own house's name. "I wanted you to hear the water moving before it reached you. Nobody did that for us."

The gulls went over. Kael thought about thanking him and could not find the shape of it, so he gave what he had instead.

"Ask me the mountain question again."

"What?"

"From last winter. What it's like. Up there."

Oss stared at him. Then he sat forward the way he had sat forward at this same stump a year ago, the words all the way off him, his face open and turned — and Kael told him. Not the whole of it; the coat stayed on. But true things, arranged true: the loft and the ladder, and the goat who had hated him from birth on principle, and the winter the snow reached the eaves, and the waterer — the trough and the stone that watered the garden all day in fair portions with nobody there. Oss laughed at the goat, the real laugh, not the trade one, and then asked four questions in a row about the waterer, every one of them good, and for the length of the cold walk back neither of them was performing anything. Both of them were served — and this once, a little, met.

At the door of the hall Oss stopped him, a hand on his arm, which Oss never did. Kael's body filed the hand and let it stay.

"One more thing, and then I'm done being wise, it's exhausting." He let go. "You know where you stand with laughing people. You learned that fast; I watched you learn it. Now comes the other lesson. Smiling people. Laughing people cost you standing. Smiling people cost you things you don't get back."

Then his face put its trade back on the way you put on a coat, and he went toward the middle of the hall, and Kael went toward the end of the bench, and that was the shape of that winter: the wall and the hall, the truth and the timing, both things real.
