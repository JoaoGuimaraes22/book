# Chapter Nineteen — The Winter

The door opened when he was still four strides off it, which meant his mother had been at the window and not at the fire, and had been there long enough to know his walk before she could have seen his face.

Behind her, in the middle of the room, his father was standing.

Kael came in and put his back against the wood and shut it with his weight.

"I was followed," he said.

His mother went past him and put the bar down and it went into the stone the way it had gone into the stone every night of his life, and the sound was the same sound, and everything in the room changed anyway.

"From where," his father said.

"I don't know where he picked me up. I had him at the second bend above the village, a hundred and fifty strides back, walking."

"Sit."

Kael sat. Neither of them did.

"Go."

It came out of him fast and complete, because it was the one part of the day he had a shape for.

"A man on a public road at the hour men come off the water. Wool cap, wool coat, the colour of every coat on this island. My height and a good deal heavier through the chest. Boots not made here — the sole was too flat and he set the whole foot down at once, so he came off a ship or he came off a horse. He never closed and he never fell back. A hundred and fifty at the bend. A hundred and fifty at the top of the rise."

"You tested it."

"Twice. I stopped at the top of the rise over a lace that did not need tying and counted forty, and then went on. A man walking home covers forty strides in that time. He was still a hundred and fifty back. I did it again lower down and got the same number."

"Where did you lose him."

"The fold under the goat line, where a walker behind you loses the top of your head for four seconds. I used three. Eleven feet up the bank on the outsides of my feet and down flat in the scree."

"How long did you hold the stillness."

"Until the lamps at the water went out. All of them. Then a hundred more."

His father came and put one hand on the back of the chair opposite and left it there.

"Did he go past you or did he stop."

"Went past. Same pace he'd kept for two miles. Didn't slow at the fold."

"Did he look up the bank."

Kael opened his mouth and found that he had to go and get the answer, and that it took him longer than it should have, and that his father waited for it without moving.

"No," he said. "He never looked at the bank at all. He didn't check the high ground. Not once, anywhere on that road, and there are four places on it where I would have."

Something went across the table between his mother and his father that Kael could not read, and he had been reading those two faces his entire life.

"Then one of three things," Vask said. "He was bad at the work. Or he was not alone, and did not need to be good at it. Or he was never meant to be hidden from you at all."

The fire moved in its hole.

"What's the third one mean," Kael said.

"It means a man walked up the only road there is, behind a boy, at a distance the boy could see him from, and did not do one single thing to keep himself out of sight. That is not always poor work." His father took his hand off the chair. "Sometimes a man is put on a road to be noticed. You learn a great deal about a house by letting it find out it is being looked at, and watching what it does next."

"And what did this house do."

"Nothing yet," said Vask. "Because you are good, and because you came the long way, and because I have not gone outside."

Kael understood, about a breath later, that his father had been standing in the middle of that room for forty minutes not because he was waiting, but because going out to walk the ground was the one thing in the world he wanted to do and the one thing he could not afford to be seen doing.

His mother sat down across from him then and put her hands flat on the table and began.

Where did you first have him. What was the light doing. What did you change. What did he do when you changed it. How many on the road behind him, how many in front. Was there a boat off the point when you came over the shoulder, and how many oars. Where was the sun. They came in the order they always came in, no matter what the thing was that had happened, and he answered all of them, and somewhere in the middle he noticed for the second time in his life that the order did not change when the subject did, and that he had a name for that in the yard and none for it at this table.

"His face," she said at the end.

"I never saw it. I have the cap, the shoulders and the walk."

"You have the walk."

"I have the walk."

"Then you will walk it for your father tomorrow," Neris said, "at speed, four times, and he will tell you what that man does for a living."

"And nothing on him," Vask said. "You would have said."

"Nothing. No badge, no colour, no ring, no dog, nothing on him anywhere that meant one single thing."

His father nodded once, slowly, at the middle of the table, and did not look pleased and did not look afraid, and said, "Good," and Kael discovered that he had been waiting eleven years to be frightened by that word.

---

"Now the rest of it," Vask said.

Kael put his hands on the table because he did not know where else to put them. His body tensed.

"The tournament lists went up this morning. In earned order, off the standings." He made himself say it at one speed. "Vel. Corvane. Two of the senior cohort. Sedrane. And me, four lines down."

Neither of them moved.

"I did not enter," Kael said. "I want that known first. I have not gone near it, I have not asked anyone, I have not written one word to anybody. Somebody entered me."

"Who."

"There is a provision in the games' law. A judge of the games may stand for a competitor who has no house." He kept his eyes on his father, because four years ago he had learned the reading was better there. "A judge used it. It is sealed and it is lodged in the capital and it went nine days ago — before the standings closed. Which means whoever wrote it knew where I was going to fall on that sheet before I did."

"The master," said his mother.

"Spent this morning finding out what he could do about it." Kael heard his own voice do something and put it back. "Nothing. The Temple is his and the tournament is not. He can refuse me a room and refuse me the yard, but he cannot refuse me the games."

His father sat down.

It was the first thing either of them had done all night that Kael could put a price on, and what it came to frightened him more than the road had.

"There is a second column on that sheet," Kael said. "Every name on it has a house written beside it, because a boy does not enter the games, his house enters him. Mine has never had anything in it. Today it has a man's name and a word after it that means an office."

"Then give me the name," said his mother.

"Halden."

He was watching both of them when he said it. He had decided on the road that he would, and he had spent eleven years being taught by these two people how to read a face while it takes something in, and the last of that schooling had been beaten into him over one autumn by a girl who could read a room of six hundred in a single pass.

He got nothing.

Not stillness — stillness would have been an answer. What he got was two people hearing a name for the first time in their lives. His mother's eyes went very slightly up and to the side, the way they did when she was going back through everything she had, looking for something. Then they came back, and there was nothing in them. His father waited while she did it, which told Kael which of the two of them carried the names.

"No," Neris said.

"No," said Vask.

It had taken four seconds, and neither of them had any idea what they had just told their son, which was that the man who had reached into his life was somebody they had never heard of. Nothing else that happened to him that day came near it.

"Who is he," his mother said.

"A judge of the games."

It came out level and unhurried, and every word of it was true, and it was the smallest true thing he could have said. Behind it stood two years of corridors and doorways and meetings shaped like accidents; a warmth that ran at exactly the same heat for everybody, which was the one wrong note he had ever caught in the man; a judge who talked about the capital the way Oss had once talked about the Temple. He had shut all of it in a drawer and had not opened the drawer since, and it had come open by itself that morning, in the rain, in front of a wall.

"He came two springs early with a warrant that is correct," Kael said. "The First Master looked at it twice today and both times it was still correct. He is not one of the great houses and he is not anybody's cousin." And then, because the rest of it was the part that mattered and he was not going to keep that back too: "The master said — *he is the empire, Kael. Not a house.*"

Vask looked at Neris.

"There is one more," Kael said. "I think it is the worst one and I have been carrying."

"Give it."

"He talked to me about the spring. The exhibition. The thing I did to the Vel girl's arm." He had told them that himself, in this room, at this table, in the summer. "He said the agreement was not luck. Four hundred people wanted an easy answer and were handed one and took it, and he let them keep it. And then he said he was not going to ask me what it was, and that he had chosen every day since the spring not to say any of this, and that he had been wrong to." Kael looked at his hands. "And then: *somebody in that colonnade did not need an easy answer. He had a hard one already.* And nine days ago a man wrote my name onto a sheet in the capital."

His mother stood up.

She did not go to the window. He noticed it because he had cracked the window pattern when he was nine years old and had gone on quietly testing it ever since, the way a man puts his hand on a rail he does not trust. She went to the door instead and put her palm flat on a bar that was already down.

"Say the judge's own sentence," she said. "The one at the rail. Not the master's."

"*What a pity it would be, not to showcase the empire's finest.*"

"And he asked you for nothing."

"Nothing at all. He said so twice." Kael turned in the chair. "He was kind about it. That is the part I cannot get past. There was no cruelty in it anywhere."

"No," his mother said. "There would not be."

---

"I've known for a while know. That something, or someone, is looking for us. How long have you known it would come for us? I deserve to know. Please." Kael said.

Neither of them asked him what he meant.

"For as long as you lived," his father said.

"And you have watched a sea every night of my life for... whoever that *someone* is?"

"Yes."

"Then say the rest of it." None of this had been planned and it was arriving now in the order it had stacked in since he was born. "You told me at this table that whatever we hide from is a weight, and that it kills. You told me this house was alive because nobody had ever looked at it. You told me I would have the whole of it one day and to pray the day never came. A man walked behind me to within two miles of that door tonight, so I would say the day has opinions of its own." His voice went, and he let it go. "You told me once that I would not ask the next hundred questions that night. It has been a year and a half. I have kept every one of them."

"Ask," Neris said.

So he asked them, and they let him ask, and it was the cruelest arrangement anyone had ever made with him, because they let him get all the way to the end.

Who is hunting us. Who are you. What did you do. Who is the bad employer. Whose was the blade that made the seam in your shoulder and where were you standing when it came. Why does a woman in a village have four languages and an order to ask questions in. What is my name — not what am I called, what is my *name*.

His father let the whole of it run out and did not once look away from him, which was worse than any of the answers would have been.

"We are hunted," Vask said.

"By who."

"That is not tonight."

"Then what is."

"That you are hunted because you are ours," his mother said, from the door. "That is the whole of it and it is not the piece I am handing you to keep you quiet. It is the part everything else stands on. Nobody in the world is looking for a herder's son on a hill. They would be looking for you because you came out of us."

"Then tell me what you *are*—"

"No."

"Why."

"Because the day you know it you are carrying it," Neris said, "and a boy carrying it stands differently and answers differently and comes through a door differently, and every one of those is a sentence a stranger can read. You have spent this evening telling me how a man on a road gave himself away by the way he put his feet down. You are asking me to put a thing in you that you cannot set down."

Kael sat with that.

"That is the same answer you gave me last year in a different coat."

"Yes."

"It was a bad answer then."

"Yes," his mother said. "It is still the only true one."

His father had not moved through any of it. Now he put both forearms on the table, the way he did in the yard when he was going to explain a thing once, and the tally-marks came up out of the sleeve and lay there where they had lain in front of Kael for twelve years.

"Three sentences," Vask said. "They are not the history. You will not have the history from me tonight or on any night I can see from here. You are going to hate me for how small they are. Listen anyway."

"I'm listening."

"We were owned. We stopped being owned. People who own things do not forget."

Kael counted to eleven before anybody in that room moved.

"That is three sentences," he said.

"It is."

"There are about forty years missing out of the middle of it."

"There are."

Then his mother said, from the door, in the flattest voice she had used all night, "It may not be them."

His father did not turn round.

"It is them."

"A man walking a boy home is them. A name written onto a sheet in the capital is not obviously them at all." She came off the door. "Your son has been three lines off the top of that Temple's boards for two years. He put an edge on a piece of wood in front of four hundred people and the whole island agreed to look away, and one man on a colonnade did not. The empire looks at children, Vask. It has built a school on this island for the express purpose of looking at children. An empire noticing a clever boy is not the same animal as the one we have been watching that sea for, and you cannot make it the same animal by wanting there to be only one."

"And if it is both," Vask said.

Neris did not answer that.

Kael sat between the two of them with his hands flat on the table and understood, with a coldness that arrived somewhere under his ribs and stayed there for years, that his mother and his father — who had never in his whole life disagreed about anything in front of him, not once, not about a rule or a lesson or a road — did not know. They had no more of it than he did. They had eleven years of watching an empty sea and one man in a wool coat and a word neither of them had ever heard, and the answer changed everything they were about to do, and they could not get it.

"Then I'll ask the other one," he said.

They both looked at him.

"The master. You are going to say his name in about four minutes, because you always do, because every road out of this house has him standing at the end of it, and I have never once asked you why." He kept his voice where his father kept his. "You have hidden from everybody alive for eleven years. Why not him?"

His mother's chin came up very slightly.

"Because he has never once been wrong about us," Vask said.

"He knows us. Truly," said Neris.

And that was the whole of it. Neither of them said another word about it, and neither of them looked at the other one, and Kael sat there and read the shape of the answer instead of the answer, which is what he had been trained to do.

---

"We go," his father said.

He said it to Neris, over the top of his son, in the voice of a man saying a thing that had been decided somewhere else a long time ago and was only now being taken down off its hook.

"With what," said his mother.

"There are four boats in that village that would go out tonight for enough coin."

"And by the third day all four of them have told somebody where they carried three people in the dark, because it is the most interesting thing that will ever happen to a man who fishes." She had not moved from the door. "The hulls that do not talk write instead. A merchant keeps a manifest, and a manifest is a sheet of paper with our names on it in a room in the capital, and we have spent eleven years staying out of rooms in the capital."

"Then we take one and do not ask."

"In the cold term." Neris said it with no weight on it at all. "On that water. One sail, no second hand aboard who knows the passage, past the last of the islands in the dark. You would be putting the three of us in a boat the sea does not have to try very hard to take."

Kael had been running it alongside them from the first word, because he could not help running it, and he heard himself say the next part out loud before he had decided to.

"And the harbours are watched."

They both looked at him.

"He came off a ship or off a horse, and he did not know this island — he never once looked at the ground he was walking on," Kael said. "If somebody can afford to put a man on the road above the village to find out where I sleep, then somebody has men standing on the quays, because that is cheaper and it is the first thing anyone would do. Whichever of us walks down to hire a boat walks past him."

His father was quiet for a moment.

"Yes," he said.

"Then not a harbour." He had it built before he opened his mouth, the way he built everything. "There are beaches on our own side of this mountain that nobody has any reason to walk to. It needs a hull that is not ours and is not anybody's, and somebody to have put it there beforehand, and we go down in the dark and nobody on this island ever knows one thing about it."

Neither of them said anything at all, and about a second later Kael understood that he had just described, out loud, from nothing, a plan they had already made.

"Yes," his mother said. "That is how it will be done."

"Then we can go now."

"No."

"You just said—"

"I said that is how it will be done." Neris came off the door. "I did not say when."

"Why not now."

A stop. He could see his mother's breathing got heavier.

"Because I am carrying a child," his mother said.

The fire went on doing what the fire did. His father did not move. Kael sat with his mouth open around the next word of an argument that had stopped existing somewhere behind him.

"You're—"

"Yes."

"Oh. Wow. Mother... since when."

"Since the summer." 

Silence sat in the room for a while. The as Kael's eyes widened, as he said:

"I'm going to have a brother. Or a sister."

"One or the other," his mother agreed.

It was the second time that day that a thing had got all the way through him before the rest of it could arrive, and this time there was no rest of it.

"Will I be allowed to hold it."

He heard it come out of his own mouth and could not think where it had come from. It was not a count and it priced nothing and nobody in that room needed it, and he was twelve years old and three lines off the top of the boards of a Temple full of the empire's children, and he had asked it in the voice he had asked things in when he was five.

His mother looked at him for some time.

"Yes."

"When."

"When it is dry and it is breathing and I say so." Something came up into her face that had not been anywhere in it since he came through the door, and stood there a moment, and went. "You will be careful with the head."

"I know about heads."

"You know about heads," his mother agreed.

Kael ran the rest of it because his head could not be stopped from running things. The summer, and three months gone, which put the birth into the spring — and the spring was the games, and the games filled this island with hulls and retinues and quiet men on every quay with a list in their hands.

"Then it's born in the spring," he said. "And the spring is the one season we can't move through."

"There will be perhaps three weeks after it comes, before the sea fills up." Neris came off the door. "We will use them. And the master will be here for it."

Kael looked over at his father, because his father had a face for everything and had been wearing one all night.

"The master works nature," Kael said.

He had seen it once, two years ago, in the study: a dead fern standing itself back up out of its pot while the old man sat across the room with his hands in his lap. He had never once put that beside anything else.

"Nature is the art of mending," his mother said. "Most of what a healer does badly with a hot iron and a needle, a skilled artist does properly." She sat down on the bench by the door, which was not where she sat. "And the art on its own is not worth very much. A man who can push a bone toward mending and does not know which way the bone goes will push it the wrong way, and there are a great many more of those about than anyone will admit to you. You want both halves in the one head. He has read medicine for as long as I have been alive and he works nature, and there is nobody else on this island who is both."

Kael sat with that.

"He will find the boat as well. Not a hull with our faces written in it — something of his own, kept dry, on our own side of this mountain. We go off a beach in the dark and we are past the last of the islands before the village has its shutters open."

"Where."

"That is not tonight either."

Kael had thought a great deal of the master for as long as he had known him.

He sat and listened to his mother lay out a road built end to end out of that one man, and thought a great deal more.

"Women have children on this island every week of the year," Kael said. "Nobody sends up the hill for the First Master of the Temple."

"Your mother will have the best hand there is on this island," his father said.

It was the first time all night that either of them had answered a question that had been put to the other one. Kael knew what it means when the *wrong* person answers, and he sat and looked at the two of them and let it go, because there was nothing else available to do with it.

Kael did the rest of the sum before either of them offered it to him.

The cold term. The storm-weeks. The turn toward spring. The child, and then three weeks, and then a beach in the dark.

Six months. Call it six months and be honest about it, because a count you hurry is a wish.

Nobody in that room said the word *friends*, because nobody in that room had ever used it about him, including him.

"The games are in the spring," his mother said, to his father, in the same voice she had used about the hull.

"Yes."

"He is written on a sheet in the capital under a seal. A boy who is written down and does not appear is a question somebody asks out loud, in a room we cannot see into."

His father was a long moment answering.

"Then he appears," he said.

"He appears." Neris did not look at Kael once. "He goes as far as he goes. And on the day the boat is ready we take him off that field and down to the water — whatever day it falls on, and whatever he is standing in the middle of."

Kael sat perfectly still, with his hands flat on his own table, and did not say one word.

He had spent four years learning exactly what it cost to be seen, and had built a very good coat out of it, and the best work he had ever done with it was done at his own table, on the two people who had cut it for him, over one clause his mother had said about a day in the spring.

---

After that they stopped talking to him and started talking about him, and did not send him out.

He would think about that for years and never entirely settle what it had been. They had put him out of rooms his whole life for smaller things; there had been a night when he was nine when the world turned on a conversation held behind a wall with a boy on the other side of it declining to listen. Tonight they left him at the table and built it in front of him in the voice they used for salt and weather and the price of a goat, and never once asked him what he thought. He could not decide, then or afterward, whether it was the first time they had ever treated him as a man or the last time they had ever treated him as furniture. Possibly both. It was the family trade.

The list of what went was short and it was mostly not things. The books went, because the books were the master's and could go back to the master. The salt went, because salt is money anywhere there is weather. The slate did not go. The loom did not go, and his mother said that out loud and said nothing else about it, and Kael found that he was watching his mother price a loom and looking away from it at the same time.

His father crossed the room in the middle of it, thinking, his thumb moving along his fingers the way it did when he was counting a thing that was not in the room, and stopped at the foot of the loft ladder and put his boot flat down on the third board and stood on it.

Kael had counted around that board since he was nine years old.

His father stood on it with the whole of his weight and it did not creak.

It moved. It went down about the thickness of a fingernail, all of it at once, end to end, and when the weight came off it came back up all at once as well, and in the black seam along its edge, for about as long as it takes to blink, there was a line of iron.

A nailed board does not move. This one had gone down along its whole length and come back up, which meant it was not fixed to anything, which meant it was lying on top of something.

His father took his boot off it and went on counting on his fingers and said something to his mother about the goat, and his mother answered him about the goat, and neither of them looked at the floor, and there was no reason on earth why either of them should have.

Kael sat with his hands flat on the table and did not ask.

He put it where it went. He did not take it out again for a long time.

---

"Then there is the hill," Vask said.

Kael's head came up.

"He does not go back down it," his father said. "Not tomorrow, not in the cold term. He is sick. Boys are sick all winter. It buys us until the turn and by then we know more than we know now."

"No," said Neris.

"He was followed home."

"He was followed home from a school he has walked to every day for four years, by a man who did not look at the bank." She had not raised her voice at any point in the entire evening and did not raise it now. "Think about what you are asking for. On the ninth day of it somebody in that Temple says *where is the mountain boy*, and a clerk writes it down, and a judge of the games who has just put a name on a sheet in the capital hears that the name has stopped coming to lessons. What have we told him? We have told him the boy has somewhere else to be and somebody at home who decided it."

Vask said nothing.

"A boy who stops going is a boy worth asking about," she said. "He goes."

"He goes down a road with a man on it."

"He goes down a road with a man on it," Neris agreed, "and he goes differently, and he does not go empty-handed, and we do not give anyone on this island one single new thing to look at. That is the whole of the argument and you know it is."

His father stood for a while with his hand on the back of the chair.

"Say it, then," he said at last, to Kael, without turning round. "Since it is your road."

"Not the same road twice," Kael said. "Not the same hour twice. Never the last stretch in the light if I can help it, and never the same way up off the road onto the shoulder." He had built it on the walk home over the goat line without knowing he was building it. "And nothing changes at the Temple. Nothing at all. I go in at the same gate at the same bell and I am exactly as boring as I was yesterday."

"And if you are taken on the road."

"Then it happens on ground I chose."

His father turned around and looked at him for a long moment, and something crossed his face that Kael did not have a category for and never afterward built one for either.

"Yes," said Vask.

Then he went to the wall and took down the knife.

It was Kael's own, the small one, in its place where it had always been, cleaned once years ago back to the metal's own weather by hands that had never said anything about it. His father turned it over twice, the way he turned over everything before he gave it to anybody, and held it out across the table hilt first.

"Not on your belt," he said. "Not where a boy carries a knife to be seen carrying one. You will show me in the morning where you have put it and I will tell you whether it is a good place."

Kael took it.

"It is a tool," his father said. "It has been a tool your whole life and it stays a tool, and if you ever have it in your hand on that road it is because everything I taught you before it has already failed. Do you understand me."

"Yes."

"Say the rest."

It was the oldest lesson in the house and he had been able to say it since he was eight.

"That the knife is the smallest part."

"That the knife is the smallest part," Vask said, and let go of it.

Later, in the loft, with the two voices going below him and the fire down to its coals and the bar across the door, Kael lay on his back in the dark with the thing wrapped in cloth beside his hand and ran the count again, because he could not stop running it and because it was the only part of the whole day he could hold in one piece.

The cold term. The storm-weeks. The turn toward spring. The child. Three weeks.

Six months, and it came out the same every time, and it was the only honest thing anybody had given him since the first bell.
