# Chapter Three — Chores

The day had two halves, like the mountain. The mountain had a sun side and a shade side, and the line between them moved but was always somewhere. The morning belonged to his father. The afternoon belonged to his mother. Kael was the line that moved between them.

He was eight, and he had theories about most things now. The theories came out of questions, and the questions never stopped; they hatched out of each other like flies. His mother said his questions had questions. A day with a new thing in it was a better day than a day without, and the empty days made him restless in a way he had no proper name for. It was an itch, but not on the skin. Skin itches he could out-wait; he had out-waited itches beside a fence post for half a day. This one you could not out-wait. It just moved deeper in and kept asking.

His father was already a shape by the cold hearth however early Kael came down the ladder, and they went out into the dark together, because the mountain in the dark was a different mountain and you had to know both of them. They ran the scree first. You could not run scree by looking — by the time the eye told the foot, the foot had already decided — so you ran it in the dark until the foot stopped asking the eye at all. Kael had fallen on the scree more times than he had numbers for. His father had watched him fall from two steps away, every time, and never once been falling himself.

After the scree there was wood, and water, and the snares. The snares were where the knife lived. Kael had his own now, a short one, and the first true thing about the knife was that the knife was the smallest part of the lesson. The lesson was everything the cutting was not: where the other hand lived while the blade worked, what the feet were doing under you, what your eyes kept doing — going up, going out, coming back — because a boy with his whole face in his work had stopped being on the mountain, and you were never to stop being on the mountain. His father taught the way he split wood. One correction, one stroke. He would move Kael's elbow in by the width of two fingers and say nothing. The silence was not empty. The silence meant: again, and this time it is yours.

That morning there was a hare, stiff in the dawn snare, and Kael skinned it on the flat stone while the light came up grey out of the sea. He was good at it now. He knew he was good at it, and the knowing was the mistake, because the hide gave all at once where he expected it to hold, and the blade skated off the breastbone toward the soft of his own wrist —

— and stopped. His wrist was inside his father's hand. He had not seen the hand leave the woodpile. He had not seen his father at the woodpile. The hand was simply there first, the way it had been there before the ground was, years ago, the way it had been under the slate. It held his wrist the exact time it took the blade's want to go out of it. Then it put his wrist back where it had been, and his father said, "Elbow," and moved it in by the width of two fingers, and that was all. The correction was the same size as every other correction. Kael finished the hare with his heart going loud and his hands going quiet, and was proud of the quiet hands.

His father was fast the way weather was fast. You did not see it start. You were just in it.

Later, at the trough, his father stripped to the waist to wash, and Kael read his back the way he read the goat paths. He knew the marks by heart: the long pale seam under the left shoulder, like a place where his father had been let out to grow; the round puckered one with a brother on the front, so that something had gone in and kept going; the short ones on the forearms, many, all the same length, all the same slant, like tallies. Once, this summer, he had asked what work made those, because work made all marks — the rope its stripe, the scythe its notch.

His father had wrung the water out of the cloth before answering. "A bad employer."

Kael had not known the word, and had it explained: someone you work for. "Did you leave?" he'd asked, and his father had said, "Yes," and Kael had said, "Good," and gone back to his rope, and so had missed the thing that crossed his father's face, having no reason to look for it. The marks went on the list of true things. Snow was cold. Fire lived in its hole. His father had worked for a bad employer, and left.

At noon they ate, and the day crossed its line, and he was his mother's.

His mother's school left no marks, and Kael could not have said why it was harder. It was the school where you were never told whether you were done. In the afternoons the table became wherever she said it was — a market, a ship's deck, a garden — and she spoke to him in one of the languages, and the rule was that the language was the only door: if you wanted anything, even water, you knocked on that door or went without. The bird language had been the first, when he was small, but there was the one that clicked and cracked like stones, for market days, and the soft one that ran downhill all its length, that she was strictest in, and sang in sometimes at the ends of afternoons, low, when she thought the lesson was over. He knew the languages the way he knew paths: not as things learned but as places he could go. He had asked once who else spoke the stone-click one, and she had said, "Traders," and the story had a shut door in it.

History was the smallest of her schools, and the only one he was hungry in. The others she poured; history she rationed. It came in scraps, never on asking — a war mentioned sideways while her hands worked, a city with towers she had seen once and would not say when, how a king was a kind of weather that happened to countries. The scraps were bright and exact and did not touch each other, and between them the world down there stayed dark. There were rooms in her stories she walked him around the outside of, and by eight he knew the shut doors the way he knew the split rock: you could stand near them. You did not knock. He had a theory, which was that she was saving the rest, the way you saved the honey.

There was far-counting, his own name for it: counting things you could not walk to. The day she showed him that a stick's shadow and a crag's shadow were the same story told in two sizes was one of the great days of his life; he had spent the rest of that summer taking the height of everything that would hold still — the crag, the tall pine, the house, the goat (the goat would not hold still), his mother, who stood for it in the yard with her arms out, laughing, while he paced off her shadow like a surveyor of small countries. "Well?" she would say, arms out. "Taller than the door. Not by much." "Then measure the not-by-much," and she held still until he had. He could find himself by the stars now, which she said mattered more than finding north, though he had never yet been anywhere else to be.

And there was the standing game, about why things stood and why they fell, and it was the school his hands liked best. In the spring a stone had come off the slope into the gate of the upper pen, a stone with the weight of the house in it. His father had gone for the iron bar, and his mother had come out and said, "No. It's his." And it had been his: his all morning, while they went about their work and did not help. He had cried at it once, quietly, and stopped, because crying was asking and there was no one to ask. Then a pole, and a log under the pole — and where the log sat was the whole of the secret, he found out by doing it wrong; the pole did not care how strong you were, only where you stood on it. By noon the stone was out of the gate, walked over the ground in stiff little tips and shoves by a boy who came up to its shoulder. His mother watched the last of it from the doorway and said, "Everything heavy is lazy. Remember that," and went back in. He had the feeling from that morning filed where he could reach it: the mountain was stronger than everyone, stronger than his father even, and the mountain could be tricked.

That afternoon was an herb afternoon. The basket had been out in the wet wood with her since first light, and she turned it out on the scrubbed boards: the good greens, the roots, and, on a cloth of their own, the mushrooms.

The herb game had grown teeth over the years, the way the stillness game had. When he was five it had been smells and rhymes. Now she set the caps out in pairs, each beside its shadow, the eater beside the killer, and went through them with him, her finger moving, her voice even. This one feeds you. This one, its twin, stops a heart. Not the cap — the cap lies. Look under. Look at the collar on the stem. Look at what the flesh does when you break it, what color it turns, slow or quick. Ask it questions with the vinegar, with the salt, with a hot iron — things that cannot lie make the best witnesses. You learn them both, the eater and the killer, one beside the other, so you never pick wrong.

Then she cleared them all but two. The two were the same mushroom twice — brown caps, dry, a little shine; the same smell when she broke the edge of each, or almost: one had a sweetness far down under it, like something too polite. She set the pot on its hook, and put the onions in, and said, without turning around: "One of them goes in. Choose."

And she went on with the onions.

That was the whole of it. She did not watch him choose. That was the part that made his chest go tight, tighter this year than last, because he understood more this year than last: the pot was the pot they would all eat from, and she trusted the lesson all the way, further than he wanted to be trusted. He took the caps to the window light. He turned them under. He broke the flesh and watched what the color did, and smelled again for the politeness, and stood there long past the point where he was sure, because sure was the feeling that had skated the knife that morning. He put one cap on the cloth by the pot and the other in the slop pail, and his mother added his choice to the onions without looking at it, and sang, later, in the downhill language, while the pot worked.

Supper was the answer. They ate, and the fire sat small and orange in its hole, and nobody died, and nobody remarked on it, because you did not get remarks for doing what was expected. He was eight. Other children — down there, wherever children were — must be learning the same things from their own mothers, he supposed. How else would anyone live to be big?

In the loft that night he did his numbers. Not counting-numbers; the list. The scree in the dark, both legs sure. A hare out of its skin in the time the light took to come up. Nothing beside a fence post for as long as asked — longer than the asking, now. Four languages like paths. The killer told from the eater by what the broken flesh confessed. The height of anything from its shadow, himself from the stars, a stone the weight of the house tricked out of a gate. And still the split rock, and still the sea not frozen.

The wind came to the door and pushed, and the door held.

The best thing about the list was that it was not finished. He could feel the unfinished part, the way you feel the dark past a lamp — all the things not yet known, waiting. And when he tried to feel where they were, the new things, they were not up the mountain, where he had been everywhere. They were down. Down at the languages' far ends, down in the shut rooms of his mother's stories, down where the fallen stars burned in their corner, low and warm, the way they had burned all his life.

He could wait. He was very good at waiting. Everyone he loved had made sure of it.
