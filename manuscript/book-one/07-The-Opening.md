# Chapter Seven — The Opening

The winter measured him in mendings.

First the arm, which itched under its thread and then itched worse without it, a tightness he was told not to test and tested anyway, alone, in the loft, making a fist until the new skin spoke up. Then the ribs, which measured slower. Ribs kept their own calendar. For a long while there were two kinds of breath, the shallow kind that cost nothing and the deep kind that cost. He paid for the deep kind sometimes just to know the price, and the price came down by the week. That was the calendar.

Nobody punished him.

He waited for it the way he had once waited for the sea. The law he had broken was the oldest law there was, older than the fire in its hole, and he lay those first days building what the punishment might be out of the materials he had — more work, less supper, some new stillness with no boat to give it a shape — and the days came up the ladder one at a time, and none of them had it. His mother changed the dressing on his arm with her mouth set and her hands soft. His father brought his meals up while the ribs still owned the ladder, set the bowl down, took the old one, said what there was to say about the goat or the weather, and went. The house was gentle the way the house was gentle after the long game, years ago: honey in the milk, and nobody saying why.

He had a school for everything else. He had no school for this. Guilt, like the dead man in the doorway, was a thing he had been given all the tools for and none of the names.

The knife was back on the wall.

He found it there the first morning he came down the ladder on his own — his knife, in its place, in the order of tools, and it had been cleaned. Not wiped. Cleaned, the way his father cleaned, back to the metal's own weather. Kael stood in front of the wall for a while with his ribs holding his breath down to the shallow kind, and did not touch it, and did not ask, and the not-asking was a thing he did with his whole body. Someone had gone down for it. Someone had brought it up the mountain and made it right and hung it where it lived. The wall said what the honey said, in the wall's language: *we know everything, and here is your knife.*

Those weeks his father sat by the fire in the evenings with the drawknife and a stack of seasoned ash, and made swords.

Not swords. Kael corrected himself the first night, and was wrong, and knew better by the third: they were swords the way the game was a game. Wooden, full-weight, the balance fussed over the way his father fussed over nothing else — shavings taken off in whispers, the thing hefted, turned, hefted again, held out level on two fingers to see where it wanted to tip. Two of them, one to Kael's arm and one longer. Then a spear with its head blunted round. Then, from the corner where it had always leaned being a hunting tool, the bow came into the evenings too, and got a second string made, and a dozen new arrows with their feathers set in his father's exact, unhurried spiral.

Nobody said what any of it was for. The boy healed by the fire, and the father built the things they were going to fight each other with by the same fire, and the wind came to the door and pushed, and the door held.

---

The audit began before the ribs were done, at the table, on a morning of hard rain.

His father put the door on the table. Not the door — the door's *work*: he set his hands in the air the way the hinges sat, and said, "Show me."

Kael showed him. There was no not-showing; a question from his father was a lifted stone, you got under it. He walked it through in the air the way he had done it in the dark — the iron tongue, the lift, the lean that took the door's weight off the hinge before the turn, so the hinge had nothing to say. His father watched with the stillness he watched everything, and at the lean he said, "Earlier." Kael moved the lean earlier. His father nodded once, and the nod was the size of every other nod, and something in Kael that had been braced since midwinter came off its brace so suddenly that he had to put his eyes on the table.

They did the whole of it that way, over days. The third board, and the count that went around it — "Slower past the count," his father said. "A count you hurry is a wish" — and the socks over the boots, and the going down, ground by ground. His father found four mistakes on the mountain. He named them the way he named a crooked elbow, one correction each, and each correction was a door quietly opening onto the size of the thing: his father was not teaching him to be caught. His father had never once, in any school, taught him to be caught.

Then, last, the yard.

"The first man," his father said.

Kael's breath went shallow, and not for the ribs. But the yard, in his father's mouth, was made of the same air as the door: work, on the table, to be gone through. So he went through it. The knee at its lazy place, two fingers of push. The second man, the wide hands, the short honest line where the grip lives. His father took him along both exchanges twice, moved the line on the wrist a half-thumb ("Here. It ends the argument the same and costs him less"), and asked no question about anything that was not movement, and Kael answered in movement, and between them the rest of the yard — the lamplight, the window, all of it — stood in the room like weather outside a wall, present and unentered.

"Then?" his father said.

"Then I spilled." The old word, the truest one he had. "The count broke and I spilled."

His father was quiet. His hand came over and rested on the stitched forearm — on the scar, exactly on it — the length of one breath. Then it went back to being a hand on a table.

"The body held," his father said. "We will build the rest."

And that was the sentence the wooden swords had been for. The new school started the day the ribs released him. His father put the shorter waster in his hands, hilt first, and watched him find the weight of it.

"Heavier at the tip than steel," his father said. "So that steel, when it comes, will feel like a gift."

Sword, spear, the bow at distance, his father beside him in everything the way his father had been beside him in the stillness, being it longer, being it better, corrections one stroke at a time. *You have the feet,* the school said, in the school's wordless way. *You have the eyes. Now we put something in your hands.* Kael took to it the way dry ground takes rain, and did not tell anyone, least of all himself, that some of the gladness was that in this school, at last, there was something to swing.

His mother's watching had changed too. He noticed it the first week he was well enough to lie awake properly: the window, the door, her feet in the dark — every night now. The boats no longer decided. He lay in the loft with the old arithmetic in his hands and nowhere to put it, because the pattern he had cracked was gone, and she had never said a word, and this was her answer the way the knife was his father's: *I know what you read. I have made myself harder to read.* He felt the cost of it arrive in him and sit down. She stood two watches now where she had stood one. He had done that.

And her answers went deeper. That was the strangest mending of that strange winter. He asked her, over the pairs of caps one afternoon, why the killer and the eater wore the same face — he had asked it before, years before, and gotten *so you never pick wrong*, which was a fence, and he had known it was a fence — and this time she stopped her hands, and thought, and answered him: what the two shared, what the flesh was doing differently underneath, how you could ask the difference to show itself, and when her answer reached the place where her answers had always gone gentle and then gone nowhere, it went on. It went all the way to *I don't know*. She gave him her not-knowing with both hands, like a tool, and he took it, and it was worth more to him than most of what he knew, and he could not have said why — except that you did not give a fence to someone you thought could not be trusted past it. Some of her schools stayed fenced. The newest school did. But the rest opened like the winter opening, and he filed the difference between the opened schools and the fenced ones, without knowing he was filing it, the way he filed everything.

---

The conversation happened in spring, in the evening, with the light long and the supper done, and it began with his father not going out.

There was an order to evenings, old as everything. His father went out last, checked what was to be checked, came in, barred the door. That evening he stayed at the table, and his mother sat down across, and Kael, between them, understood from the arrangement alone — two of them, both facing him, nothing in their hands — that this was a lesson with no school attached, and went still.

"Everything we have, you will have," his father said. "It is not everything there is."

His mother's hands were flat on the table, the way they had been the day of the knot. "The rest," she said, "is people. It cannot be learned here. We cannot—" and she stopped, and it was not the gentle stopping of the fenced schools. "It is not ours to teach," she said, which was a different sentence than the one she had started, and truer, he thought, in some way he had no floor under yet.

They did not ask him what he had seen. They did not say the word for where he had gone. The night stood outside this wall too, present, unentered, and inside the wall his parents opened the future by a crack — a school, down the mountain, where the island's children learned; a master he already knew; a road.

Kael sat with his heart going the way her heart had gone under the big wool, that first night, hurrying under a slow voice.

"When," he said, because *why* was too big to lift and his hands had gone to the question they could carry.

"Autumn," his father said.

---

The master came a month later, and Kael read the house the way he had read it at nine: the seed-bread rising in the night, the other shirt, the shoulders down. This time he knew the tells for tells, and knew they knew he knew, and no one performed anything for anyone, and the not-performing was its own strange comfort, a house full of readers all reading, at rest.

The talk after the meal happened without him. Wood wanted splitting; he went. And in the yard, in the shadow at the foot of the woodpile, the old option stood open — he knew the shapes voices made through that wall, he had pointed the stillness at it once and taken what it gave — and he stood with the axe a moment, feeling the door of that open, and did not go through it. He could not have said all of why. Some of it was the room he kept now, with its shut door, and what keeping it had taught him about the cost of doors. Some of it was simpler and had no dignity at all: the last time he had listened to that wall, the world had ended within the year. He split wood, honestly and loudly, the whole time.

At the table, after, the master spoke to him plainly, the way you speak terms.

"Down there," the master said, "you are mine. My charity case — a mountain boy I have taken on, fed books, taught letters. That is the whole of your story, and you will find it is enough story for anyone. What you know, you will be surprised by slowly, the way a boy taught in a kitchen is surprised. Do you understand?"

He did not, entirely. He understood it was a knot, and that he was being handed the tied side of it, and that everyone at the table could see the whole of it and he could see the outside. Rules were the floor you asked from. "Yes," he said.

Then his parents gave him the law, his father saying it and his mother's stillness saying it under him:

"Home to the temple. The temple to home. Never beyond."

No reason came with it. Reasons had never come with the laws; that was what made them laws. But this one sat differently in him than the split rock had ever sat, and he turned the difference over that night in the loft until he found its faces. The old law had been a wall around the itch. This one was a road cut through the middle of it. They had taken the thing in him that could not be out-waited — and aimed it.

---

He left on a morning in autumn, early, the light coming up grey out of the sea.

His mother put bread in the bag and a word in his ear, in the downhill language, low — a blessing or an order, built like both. His father walked him as far as the pens and stopped where his work stopped, and said, "Evening," which meant *by evening*, which meant *come home*, and placed his hand, briefly, on Kael's shoulder.

The master was waiting at the split rock.

He stood on the near side of it, on the path, leaning on a stick he did not need for leaning, and he watched Kael come down through the pens the way he watched everything, unhurried, and he did not say good morning. He said, "Who are you?"

"Kael," Kael said.

"Kael of what?"

"Of—" and there was nothing to put there. He had never needed anything to put there. The master let the nothing sit exactly as long as it wanted to sit, and then turned and stepped past the rock, and Kael put his hand on the stone as he went by — the way you touch the goat, the door, a thing of the house — and followed him onto the lawful road.

"You are Kael from the mountain," the master said, walking. "A herder's boy. I found you three summers ago with your letters already in you — your mother taught you those, that much is true, keep what is true, it carries the rest — and I have brought you books since, because I am an old man with money for books and no sons, and this year I have grown foolish enough to school you. That is your whole story. Tell it to me."

Kael told it to him.

"Again. You are not defending it, you are wearing it. A story is not a wall, boy; walls get climbed. *A story is a coat*. Loose. Warm. Nobody wonders at a coat."

He told it again, and the master stopped him three sentences in. "Who taught you letters?"

"My mother."

"You said it like a fact you were handing over. It is not a fact. It is *your* mother. Say it that way the whole way through."

He told it once more, all the way, the herder's boy, the books, the old man's foolishness — and it was different in his mouth this time, and he could not have pointed to where.

"There," the master said. "Now it hangs off you," and moved on as if from a finished chore. "You will be surprised slowly. You know what fire eats. You do not know what they call it. Their names for things will be new to you, and that is convenient, because it is even true."

The road bent, and below it, for the first time in daylight, lawfully, close: the village.

---

Roofs, and smoke standing up from them, and the harbor with its boats at their work, and sound coming up the slope loose and ordinary — a dog, a hammer somewhere, a woman's voice carrying two notes of something, gulls. People, small and clear, moving between the houses at the pace of chores. Kael's eyes went out, took the whole of it, came back — and his hands had gone quiet on the strap of the bag, too quiet, the stillness arriving in him unasked. Downshore, past the boat-sheds, a cluster of roofs stood with their smoke standing up like all the others, ordinary, indistinguishable, and he did not look at it longer than he looked at anything, and looking away cost what it cost.

The master, beside him, watched the harbor and said nothing at all. Then he said, "The fish here is better than the mutton. You will hear the opposite. It is villagers being loyal to sheep," and walked on, and Kael came off the stillness like a held breath let go, and followed, and was grateful in a direction he could not have pointed to.

"You like questions," the master said, further down, where the road left the village behind and ran out along the shoulder of the coast. "So. A question. You will meet children down here who have never carried water. Not once, from a well to a house. What do they know that you don't?"

Kael thought about it for the better part of a mile, because the question had doors in it, and every door he tried opened onto another, and each answer was true and none of them was the answer, and the master walked beside his thinking and let it work the way his mother let a knot work.

"I don't know," Kael said at last, and gave it the way she had taught him that winter to receive it: with both hands, like a tool.

"Good," the master said. "Hold that. It is the only thing I am worried you'll lose."

The road climbed a last shoulder, and turned, and there was the Temple.

He had seen it his whole life the way he had seen the moon — a pale shape south along the coast, a fact of the island, filed. From the road it was not a shape. It was walls out of the headland like the headland had decided them, courts inside the walls, roofs inside the courts, and over the whole of it a tower with the morning sun on its face — and it was full. That was the thing the shape had never carried up the mountain: thin smoke from its kitchens, banners moving, and in the outer court, figures crossing between the buildings, dozens of them, children, walking in twos and threes and clusters at the pace of belonging there.

The sound of them came up the road before the road went down to them. Voices, many, loose, and threaded through the voices, laughter — children laughing somewhere inside the walls, an ordinary morning's worth of it — and Kael's body answered before any of him was asked. His breath found the long shape on its own. His eyes went out over the court and came back having worked: the twos and the threes, which clusters moved and which held ground, where the walls gave and where they didn't — the yard read as a yard is read, by a boy who had only ever been in one. His hand had come up to the strap of the bag, near where the knife was not.

None of it showed. He knew none of it showed; everyone he loved had made sure of it. He stood on the road with the coat of his story on, ten years old, everything they had given him packed inside everything they had hidden, wanting to walk down into that court more than he had ever wanted anything, and ready, in the oldest part of him, for the ground.

"Come along, charity case," the master said, not unkindly, and went down toward the gate, and Kael followed him.
