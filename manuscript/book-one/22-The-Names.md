# Chapter Twenty-Two — The Names

The island filled.

Kael had watched the sea his whole life the way other boys watched a road, and he had never seen anything like this.

Hulls he had no names for. A ship with three banks of oars and a gilt beak that came in under sail alone and made the whole harbour rearrange itself to let it stand. Barges. Then more barges, unloading — poles, rope, canvas, and then, over four days, seats.

There were men in the lanes with tally-sticks. There was a horse, which Kael stopped and looked at for a full minute, because he had seen four in his life and never one that had been brought across water to be looked at.

Then the soldiers, and the soldiers did not stop.

They came off the transports in blocks and formed up on the shingle and went where they were sent, and by the fourth day there were more armed men on that island than Kael had known there were men. They took the quay. They took both roads off it. They put a post at the mouth of the village lane and another at the head of the Temple road, and Kael went past that one twice a day and was looked at twice a day by somebody who had been standing there since before it was light.

He counted them for a week out of habit and the count was the largest he had ever had.

---

They opened the games on a bright cold morning, and Kael came down the mountain at first light with his kit on his back and understood, at the bend above the village, that he had never actually seen a crowd.

He had seen the feast from a treeline when he was nine. He had seen the Temple's first court three deep at a wall. He had built his idea of *many people* out of those two things, and it had been like building an idea of the sea out of a bucket.

The arena stood on the flat ground east of the Temple, where four years ago there had been nothing but the drying-frames, and it had gone up out of poles and rope and cut stone in weeks: an oval of banked seating around a floor of raked sand, open to the sky, with the sea behind it on two sides. Kael had watched it built and had thought he had understood the size of it.

Full, it was a different object.

He came in through the competitors' gate with a Temple steward's hand on his shoulder and the noise arrived at him bodily, from above and on all sides, the sound of thousands of people who were not in danger and were not angry and were simply *there*, and every drill he had was the wrong drill. There was no count for it. His body went and got the settlement out of storage and offered it to him, unasked, the way it always did in a crowd, and he stood in the mouth of the gate and found that his hand had gone by itself to his knife.

He took the hand away, and stood there a moment with the noise coming down on him, because at ten he had come through the Temple gate with nothing on him anywhere and his hand had gone to that same place and found nothing at all, and he had not thought about that morning in years, and he thought about it now.

Then he made himself go through the exits: four, three of them narrow, the widest behind the boxes. His hands were cold and he was going to need them shortly.

The north curve was the Temple, because the Temple was hosting and the Temple had brought everyone it had — four hundred children in the school's colours, most of whom would spend the week in the lower bracket, fighting each other for a purse of gold and the right to have done it.

The other two schools had sent nobody who was not going to fight.

Five from the capital's Collegium, in red and black, standing. Five from the northwest, bigger by a hand on the average, in nothing in particular, half of them with their sleeves already off.

In the Gold league the Temple was six lines out of sixteen, and he was one of the six.

The host's box stood on the western curve, and the First Master was in it.
Here he was in robes Kael had no name for, with a chain across his shoulders that plainly had weight in it, sitting very still in a seat that had been built to be looked at, at the top of an island that had been taken apart and rebuilt around a thing he had asked for. *Four years ago, when I thought I understood what I was asking for.* He had said that himself, in the autumn, to a boy, without being asked to.

Then the horns went, and the noise came down like a lid, and everybody stood up.

A girl came in from the seaward side, which meant she had come by water that morning rather than sleeping on the island, and Kael worked out afterward that this had been decided by somebody so that the entire arena would watch her arrive rather than find her already there.

The party ahead of her took a full minute to cross.

The imperial guard went first, white and imperial blue, and they were what Kael had been walking past all week, and he read them the ordinary way: drilled, competent, unremarkable, men doing a thing they had done before and would do again on some other place.

Then twelve who were not that.

They came in a heavy dark grey with nothing whatever on it, six and six, and they were not marching, and not one of them looked at the crowd, and Kael's whole body sat up before he had any word for why. They were not arranged in front of her. They were arranged across the *distances* — the near ones close enough to take her weight off her feet, the far ones out at the edges of the ground, where a thing would have to come from, and every one of them was already standing where he was going to be standing before he got there.

It was the best thing Kael had ever seen a man's body do on purpose.

He looked sixty feet down the curve, because he could not help it. If one of those twelve had been Vel she would have had him by his walk at that distance in half that light. She was looking at the sand in front of her boots and did not turn her head once.

*Stand in front of me.*

He had taken it, at the time, for a cold way of saying something else.

Then her.

Four years in a school full of the empire's best-kept children had built Kael a scale for this without his ever having agreed to have one. Elarine was economical. Valeria was a working blade. Aurelian was beautiful and knew to the grain what it bought him, and the bench moved when he walked past it.

The scale had nowhere to put her.

She was fourteen or near enough, which put her a year above him. She came out onto the stone in a blue so dark it read black until the light moved on it, with gold at the shoulder and the hem in a quantity that had no function except to be a quantity, and her hair up off her neck in a construction that had plainly taken more than one person more than an hour.

Eleven thousand people stood up.

What impressed him was not the standing. He had seen a court stand for the First Master. It was the sound underneath it, which was not a cheer and was not a roar and went on a great deal longer than either, and which Kael understood some while afterward was the sound of a very large number of people being glad about a thing they were never going to be any nearer to than this.

He looked for what he always looked for. Reach, weight, what the shoulders had been taught, where the hands go when nobody has given them anything to do. He got nothing at all. It was not concealment — he knew concealment, he had been raised inside it and could watch his own mother doing it across a room. There was nothing under it to get. She had been made, from the ground up, to be looked at by exactly this many people at exactly this distance, and the making was total, and it was working on him while he stood on the sand and disagreed with it.

*The kind of beautiful that is also a uniform,* he thought, and had no idea where the thought had come from, and it stayed with him.

The herald had a voice that carried to the back of the seaward curve without any evidence of effort.

"Her Serene Highness the Princess Aeliana Cassivara Vessantine, of the House Vessantine, Daughter of the Emperor, Light of the Provinces, Beloved of the Throne and its Ward."

It took him six seconds. Kael counted them.

The First Master came to the rail of his box and said the words a host says, and she said the words that are said back, and the whole of it took a quarter of a minute. Kael watched the man who had told him at a gate that he was done carrying him bow his grey head to a girl of fourteen.

She sat, and the arena sat, and the sound went out of it like water out of a yard.

Then the herald came down onto the sand, and the sand was very white in that light, and read:

"The first bout of the Gold league. Arms as carried. Resonance without limit." A pause that was entirely form. "No mortal intent."

Everybody in that arena knew what the last clause was worth. You cannot weigh what a boy meant; you can only weigh what he did, afterwards, when it is already done. Kael had read the games' law twice over the winter looking for the part that protected anybody, and that sentence was the part.

What actually protected anybody was sitting in a box on the western curve with a chain across his shoulders — six days above that sand with earth in one hand and nature in the other, to stop what could be stopped and mend what could not be. Nobody said it out loud. Saying it would have meant saying the rest of it: that the empire had written a rule it could not enforce and had answered the difficulty by putting one old man in a chair.

Kael discovered, standing in the north curve with his heart already going, that none of that frightened him, and that the not being frightened was itself worth a look.

"Lucan, of the House Drusane, of the Temple of the Southern Isles."

Lucan went out. He had been ready; he had been holding his helm under his arm for a quarter of an hour, and he walked out onto the sand with the posture a paid master had spent years putting into him, and it was, Kael thought, entirely good. There was nothing wrong with it anywhere. The Drusane seats found their voice, and some of the Temple's, and the noise went up and around and came back off the seaward curve.

The herald looked at his board.

There was a pause.

It was not a long pause. It was about the length of a man reading a line, arriving at a place where a word should be, and finding that the clerk had written what the clerk had been given. Kael had watched a pen make that space once, four years ago, in a book the size of a door, and had carried the line it drew around with him ever since like a thing he owned.

His eyes went up to the western curve before he had decided to send them.

The First Master was already looking at him. He had been for some time — through the horns and the standing and the whole of the arrival, from the one seat under that sky with nothing in front of it to watch but an empty floor. There were two people in eleven thousand who knew what the herald's pause was, and the other one had stood over that pen himself and told the clerk to leave the space as it was.

Kael could not read his face. He had never been able to read that face and had given up expecting to somewhere around his eleventh year, and what he got now was what he always got, which was a man who had priced the thing in front of him and was not going to say the number.

"Kael," the herald read out, "of the Island."

Kael walked out onto the sand.

He was thirteen years old. He had wanted, since he was four, to be seen; had wanted, since he was ten, to be measured in front of people who could tell; had spent every hour of it being told what it costs to be looked at, by two people who had built him out of not being. He came out into the middle of a white floor with eleven thousand people over him and heard the first sound the world ever made about him, and the sound was a question, because they had just been told everything about the other boy and nothing whatever about him.

Lucan Drusane was already at the measure, with his helm on and his face doing arithmetic.

It had begun.
