# Chapter Twelve — The Telling

The storm-weeks shut the island at the heart of the cold term, the way they did every winter: the sea got up and walked, the yard went to standing water, and the drills moved in under the colonnade until the yard-master himself conceded the measure to the weather.

It was the stories' season. In the storm-weeks the stories came indoors where the audiences were, and grew to the size of the rooms.

The one that mattered came in with the carts.

A kitchen boy brought it to the middle tables, hot from the source and proud of the carrying: his uncle carted for the salters, his uncle had it from the village, and the village had it from no one, because it had happened to them. "Two winters gone," the boy said, the tray still on his hip. "The houses down past the boat-sheds. A whole crew of men, and the morning after the feast night every one of them dead where he sat. The one night of the year the whole village is down at the water." He set the tray down. "Not a mark of a fight on them. Not a thing taken. And no one left to say what came. Not one."

Kael was at the bench-end, being furniture, and furniture hears everything.

The arithmetic ran before he could decline it. Two winters gone — he had been nine years and a winter old. The houses down past the boat-sheds. The one night everyone was at the water. The sums arrived into rooms that were already furnished, the date and the place each walking to its chair and sitting down, the fact he had never had, two years of never having it: *the men died.* All of them. Dead where they sat, said a boy with a tray, at midday, over bread. The end of his own night, arriving by cart.

Kael went on eating.
The middle tables, meanwhile, had begun to work.

The raider theory stood up first and fell first: raiders mark what they touch and take what they come for, and this had done neither. Fever went down slower — somebody's father had once ridden past a fever port, and the telling of that took its own while — but fever did not empty a crew between supper and morning and leave the village standing around it. Somebody said bad fish, and got what he had asked for, a crop of laughter, quick and glad to be let off the strangeness, and it went over the dead men and was gone, and Kael's hands stayed quiet on the bread, and none of it showed.

"It was the *red eyes*," a girl said, from down the board. Not loudly. It did not need saying loudly; half the tables had been waiting for somebody else to say it.

And the comparing, when the comparing began, went where the trading of stories went now — to the middle of the hall. To Oss.

Kael watched his friend take the story the way his mother took mushrooms out of a basket. It was exactly that, and he filed the exactness and kept it: every telling of the eyes the hall owned, laid out cap beside cap against the carter's tale, spine matched to spine. Whole rooms of men, said the legend; a whole crew, said the cart. Doors and walls between them meaning nothing; dead where they sat, in their own houses, on the far side of their own walls. And the spine of spines, the one the legend never told without: no one left. "Everything else changes with the teller," Oss said, his hands doing the tellings for him, one after another, the same shape every time. "Where it walks. What it wants. That, it always has. No one walking away is how you know."

The hall had its answer then, and set about improving it. By the second telling of the afternoon the doors had been barred from the inside. By the third, the lamps in the dead houses had burned until dawn, which no one could have known and everyone now knew. A boy from the north stair supplied the light itself: a glow off the windows the color of a coal, seen from the boats — seen by no one, which was the seeing. The story ate from every bench and grew, the way stories grew in the storm-weeks, and the kitchen boy, watching his property improve in other hands, took it back for the finish that was his by carrying-rights.

"It came down off the mountain," he said. "On the longest night." And he pointed — through the wall, up, at the weather.

The hall looked where he pointed, at stone and lamp-smoke, at the mountain every one of them had walked beneath for years, and somebody said the obvious thing.

"The charity case lives up there." A beat, the length it took the hall to see it too. "Ask him to introduce it to his goats."

The laugh went the length of the hall, and it was a big laugh, the joke being the size of the gap in it: the goat boy, the empty purse, the smallest resonance the yard had ever measured, keeping house on the devil's own mountain. The order of the world held by laughing. Kael's breath found the long shape on its own; his face stayed at the hall's temperature; and not one face, in all the laughing length of the room, turned to him with anything in it but the joke.

He sat inside the laugh until it spent itself, and thought, in the cold clean place where the counting lived: the hall had a story with his night inside it the way a pearl has sand inside it, and no one in that hall knew but him.

He had done one other thing with the afternoon, the one thing that was his to do: he had listened to every version once, all the way through, the way you walk a snare line. He had been listening for two people.

The story had no girl in it. Not the carter's version, not any bench's. No woman either; *no one left* had gone through those houses and taken everyone, tidied the living into the dead for the sake of the spine. And the question he had carried out of that yard two winters ago, the one his night had never answered, stood up in him at the second telling and stayed standing the rest of the afternoon: whether the line had held. Four words would have answered it. *Was there not a girl?* Four words, and no way on earth to say them, because the boy who asked would be the boy who knew the story had people missing from it, and there was no such boy in the hall; there was furniture, eating bread. It was the hardest stillness anyone had ever asked of him, and nobody had asked. He held it.

And the story had no boy in it. In no version, from no bench, did anything see a boy — at a wheel, in a yard, on the long dark of the mountain road. The story had tidied him away with the others.

The road home ran up out of the weather with the day in its files. Where it bent above the village the roofs lay under their early lamps, and downshore, past the boat-sheds, the cluster stood the way it always stood, and he did not look at it longer than he looked at anything. The village had put his night up in salt, he thought, at the bend — wrong in every grain, and keeping.

It came down off the mountain on the longest night.

He did not stand near that one long.

The lamp was lit when he came in, and the slate was out, and he gave her the day: the colonnade drills, the drowned yard, the yard-master conceding the measure to the weather, a hall with nothing to do but talk. The day whole, and in the middle of it a hole, cut clean, the shape and size of one story. He was good at holes. And if her questions passed near the hole, nothing in her face or her hands said so, and something in him went easier at that, and he let it.

---

The storm-weeks ended overnight, the way they always ended, as if the winter had been called home. The sea lay down. The yard drained. Within days the first sails of the turn came up the coast — earlier than the fishing wanted, more of them than the fishing needed.

The news came with them, and it did not walk up the island. It ran.

Kael met it on the road before any master said a word: the village below the bend had changed its sound. New timber stood on the harbor front. Pitch rode the morning air with no boats hauled out to earn it. The village was readying — early, in every corner at once, for something not yet in sight — and the pattern had a hole in it exactly where the name should be.

Oss had the name by midday. Of course Oss had it; the news had come by water, and everything that came by water passed through a Vessaline ledger before it reached a Temple clerk.

"A Grand Tournament," he said, at the sea-wall, over the opened water, dividing the pastry. "Here. Two springs from now. All three schools — the capital, the north, us. The First Master will announce it soon." He handed Kael his half. "In my house it's called what it is: the biggest market this island will ever see."

Kael took the pastry and the fact together. Two springs. He would be thirteen.

"And after that it's done, you know," Oss said, to the water. "For us. A few more years and the Temple hands us on. Corvane to the armies. Drusane to the armies. Half the benches to the armies, and the ones who can't fight, to the coin and the councils. That's me, in case you were wondering. I fight like a sack of flour."

"You count well," Kael said, because it was true and somebody should say it. "You see where things stand before anyone moves. That's most of fighting."

Oss looked at him for a moment with his mouth still open around the next joke. "You're the only person on this island who would say that and mean it," he said, and looked back at the sea, and when he spoke again his voice had gone to the wall's register, low and true. "You know what it's all for, don't you. The yard. The boards. Cost before craft, cohort by cohort, year over year." He tipped his head back at the whole of the Temple behind them. "My father says it with the shutters closed: the empire doesn't keep three schools to make philosophers or simple resonants. It's a forge that calls itself a school. They're not teaching us, mountain boy. They're making us. And the tournament is the empire coming to look at the blades."

The gulls went over. Kael turned the sentence the way he turned a knot, and could not find the wrong in it, and filed it where the true things went.

"So," Oss said, lighter, but not much. "When they hand everyone on. No house holds your paper — you're the only boy here who gets to be asked. What do you want to be?"

Kael looked at the water, all the way out, to where it stopped being color and started being distance.

"I want to go past it," he said. He had never said it out loud before, to anyone, and the saying did something in his chest that the wanting alone had never done. "The sea. I want to see what's on the other side of it."

"And then?"

"I don't know." He gave the not-knowing the way she had taught him to give it, with both hands.

Oss was quiet for the length of a wave. "I'm going to stand in a counting-house in the capital," he said, "watching other men's cargo. And one day somebody will tell me some mountain boy wielding lightning went past the sea, and I'll say I split a pastry with him once, and nobody will believe me."

Footsteps came along the wall, exact and unhurried, and stopped.

"You're late," Elarine said.

"The bell hasn't rung," Kael said.

"It should've. It's late. You're late."

Oss waved them off grandly, restored to himself at once. "Go on, then. The secret school. What is it you two even do up there — hold hands with a kettle?"

"Learn," Elarine said, without turning.

"Terrifying," Oss said, and went back to the pastry.

"We trade," Kael said, because it was true. "What she knows for what I do." And Oss's laugh — the real one — followed them the whole length of the wall, and Kael filed that a joke had happened somewhere in the exchange, and could not find where.

---

The Temple assembled on the third day.

The whole of it was called to the first court, cohort by cohort in the roll's order, and the First Master came out onto the steps and stood there until the stillness had finished arriving. He did not say it grandly either. He said that in the second spring from this one the Grand Tournament would convene; that the three schools of the empire would gather to hold it; and that they would hold it here — the island, the Temple, this court and the yards and the harbor below. The words went out over the ranks plainly, one fact at a time, a man setting stones on a table.

"You have two years," the First Master said, at the end. "The tournament is sure to bring great glory to you, and your families. *If* you're prepared. Spend them well."

The court held its stillness one breath past him, and then broke.

It did not stop breaking for days. The benches reweighed themselves overnight; the boards stopped being twice-yearly weather and became a ladder standing against something; every hour in the yard had a date on it now. The best of the capital school and the best of the northwest were coming here — to be seen, and to see — and the hunger that had always lived in the cohorts quietly came up and walked around in the open. Aurelian Corvane took the news the way a banner takes wind. Elarine's stillness changed at the announcement and did not change back.

"Two years," she said, at the well, to no one in particular, pricing something far off.

"The biggest market this island will ever see," Oss said. "Well, I'll probably lose early." He smiled toward Kael. "If you make it all the way, do remember me when you're famous."

And Kael stood in the middle of the broken stillness of the court with the want standing up in him at its full height, and let it stand. Three schools. The whole empire's children, the ones its houses had been building since birth, brought to one island to be measured — and a dish had already said what he was, and the dish did not flatter and did not lie. He wanted it the way he had wanted the lights: with his whole body, leaning.

---

The mornings with his father had changed, and the one thing changed all of it: the threading.

"Lightning is a tricky element. It is the element most truly spent from the body," his father said, the first morning, in the voice of moving a lesson from one shelf to another. "The body is busy. You and the storm will learn to be busy together."

So now the wasters worked while small threads of lightning lived in his off hand — the smallest things he could hold, held — and the first mornings taught him the size of what he had been asked. The threads died at every hard parry. The sword slowed whenever the threads lived. His father watched the two of them fail to share one boy with no expression at all.

"If the threading dies, you are a boy with a sword," he said. "If the sword stops, you are a boy with a trick. Neither wins. Now look."

His father began. He threaded the lightning through his own hand, and put the hand through the trunk of a tree, wrist-deep, as if the wood were wool, and drew it out. The tree stood there with a hole through it.

Kael read the holding, not the hole. Lightning kept inside the shape of a hand, spent at exactly the rate the hand could bear, an edge finer than anything on the wall at home. His father had not carried a blade to that tree. He had made one out of a part of himself, and then unmade it.

The next question came up on its own, the way they did. If it could be done to a hand, it could be done to what the hand held.

*What could it not cut?*

By the turn of the month there had come one exchange — one — where the parry landed and the threading lived through it, and his father saw it, because his father saw everything, and nodded, one nod, a half smile, and called the next stroke.

---

That evening he carried the announcement up the mountain whole. The day was too big for holes.

He gave it at the lamp with his blood still up — the court, the steps, the three schools, two springs, the island itself the host — and his mother's questions came close and exact, the way they always came: who had announced it, and in what words. How many would come. How long the island would hold them. Which houses. He answered and answered, and somewhere in the answering he heard his own voice lean forward, heard the want get into it, and did not call it back. Two years was enough. Two years of the yard and the boards and the dawn school and the lamp — the dish had said what he was, and in two springs the whole empire would be standing in the one place built to see it.

His parents looked at each other, one look, level.

"You will not enter it," his father said.

The answer arrived whole, and instantly, and from both of them at once — his father saying it, his mother's stillness saying it under him — and it arrived fast. Faster than any answer he had ever been given in that house, to anything, as if it had stood ready behind the door for years, waiting only for the knock. He filed the speed of it, far down, under everything else that was happening in him, because the speed was wrong and there was no room in him to look at wrong things now.

"Why," he said.

No reason came. Reasons had never come with the laws; that was what made them laws. He had traded the floor for facts, board by board, year by year, and they had traded with him — the caps opened all the way, the not-knowing given like a tool — and now the oldest register in the house came down off its shelf, bare, and was set between them. It was meant to hold. It had always held.

Kael looked at the law lying on the table, and did not pick it up.

"Then I have other questions," he said. His voice stayed level, because they had built it to stay level, and his hands went flat on the table, because hers went flat when a thing mattered. "Nobody at the Temple learns what I learn. Nobody anywhere. Four languages no one on this island speaks — one of them you keep between yourselves and the master. I have no surname; the clerk drew a line where the other boys have fences. You are both Resonants, and in eleven years I never saw one thread until just recently. My mother sits the dark with a blade across her knees, watching a sea she has already counted." He set the items down the way his father set stones on a table, one at a time, his heart hurrying under the slow voice they had built him, and he did not stop. "Why?"

The fire ate what it was given. Nobody moved.

"What are we hiding from?"

His father looked at him for a long time, the way he did. Then he said, "Everything you have said is rational."

Kael waited. Waiting was a thing his father had taught him, and he spent all of it back now, at this table, on him.

"You have never been punished for a question in this house," his father said. "You will not be now. So hear the whole of what we can give you." His hands came together on the table and were still. "All of it is one answer. The languages, the rules, the watch, the No — one answer, not many. We are not saving it for when you are older. It is not a story. It is a weight, and it kills. It has killed people who carried it. It has killed people who only stood near it."

"Two springs from now," his mother said, "this island will be the most watched ground in the empire. Every eye the empire owns, in one court, for a season." Her voice was the voice of every lesson he had ever had. "This house lives because it is not looked at."

He looked from one of them to the other, and the itch and the want and the anger — he had not known until that breath that it was anger — stood together in him with nowhere to go.

"And if I need more answers?"

"If the day ever comes that you must have them," his mother said, "you will have them whole. And you will wish, that day, that you could give them back. Pray the day never comes."

And she let him see her face while she said it. All his life the fear had lived under the slow voice. Tonight she set it out where he could look at it, deliberately.

He ran it, because running things was what he was. Years of rules stood up one by one and walked to the answer and sat down around it — the fire in its hole, the boats counted, the blade in the dark, the smallness of every lesson — and nothing in him could find the lie. The answer had no face, no name, no shape, and it fit everything. That was the worst thing at that table: he believed them.

"You will stay enrolled, of course," his mother said. "The Temple. The yard. Every school in this house. Everything we should give you, you will have." He heard it all and yet he knew; the Temple, the yard, his parents' teachings: it is not everything there is. And he yearned for more. For more and more every passing day. He yearned so deeply it scarred him deep inside where no one could see.

He sat very still, and none of it showed, and both of them read all of it anyway.

"May I go up," Kael said.

He lay in the loft with the want still standing at its full height and nowhere to put it. There was nothing to count. No pattern to crack, no board to go around, no lazy place — because the wall was not a wall this time. They had cut him a road once, through the middle of the itch. Now the road ran two springs out to a gate, and the gate was them. And behind the gate there was something real now — shapeless, nameless, large enough to kill — and knowing it changed nothing about the want. Both things were real.

Below him, the two voices did not trade the day.

---

The master came up the mountain nine days after his own announcement, at dusk, and stayed past dark.

The boy was asleep when the three of them finally spoke. They had waited him out — the loft gone quiet, the breathing above gone long and even — and even then the master turned his cup once and listened to the house before he began.

"Two houses have written to me," he said. "Formal inquiries. The sponsorship, its terms, whether it can be bought out from under me." A pause the length of a swallow. "A third did not write. A third sent a man, who asked the clerks nothing at all and stayed three days."

"Asking what," Vask said.

"Everything one asks without asking. Where the boy sleeps. Who feeds him." The cup went around once more. "They ask after his mother and father."

The fire ate what it was given. Neris's hands lay flat on the table and did not move.

"He declared lightning in front of the whole roll," the master said. "Lightning is rare, and rare is a story, and stories get finished by somebody. You know that better than I do."

"Eleven years," Vask said. "The door has held."

"The door has held because nobody pushed it. In two springs the empire itself camps below your mountain. Officers. Courtiers. Men whose trade is faces."

"We told him no," Vask said. "He will not enter it."

"I know. I watched the no walk around my Temple on him for a week."

"He should have a life," Neris said. Her voice was even. "A safe one. Small, if small is the price. He was not—" She stopped. When she went on, it was a different sentence. "He will not be spent."

"The tournament comes," Vask said. "The tournament goes. The island empties. He finishes what schooling there is to finish, and then he is free of all of it — the coat, the counting, all of it. That is the whole plan. Sit still while it passes."

The master was quiet for a while. Above them the roof beams ticked with the cold.

"You have built better than you will let yourselves see," he said at last. "I read what he writes. I have watched him take apart children the empire spent fortunes making, using dirt and a kettle. He is stronger than either of you says aloud, and his mind—" he set the cup down "—his mind is not going to sit still while anything passes. You know what is in him. You put it there. He will go out into the world. Not because he disobeys you. Because he is even more than what you made, and what you made does not keep on a shelf. The only question any of us is fit to answer is how he goes. Held, or loose. Prepared, or surprised."

Neris answered him. She answered in the downhill language, low and fast, three sentences with no smile inside them, and the master listened, and replied in the same tongue, one word.

Whatever the word was, neither of the others took it up. Vask rose and barred the door, iron into stone, the sound the house made when it meant held.

And above them, past the boards, the boy slept the way the trained sleep — still, one arm over the blanket's edge — while below him, low, in the small orange light, the three people who had carried him this far moved the world by inches around his bed.
