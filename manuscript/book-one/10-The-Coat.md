# Chapter Ten — The Coat

All winter's turn the question had waited to be asked properly. At the lamp the candle had answered his mother's way and ignored the Temple's — the flame leaning a hair's width, exactly on ask, after a term of the yard's tallow giving him nothing — and he did not know which difference had done the work: the long feeling first, or the smallness of the ask, or the lamp, or the being home, or her. Too many things different at once. A thing that could be fixed.

So the trial went down the mountain with him in the morning, built, the way the count had once gone down with him: a thing finished in the head so many times that the hands would only be confirming it.

The workings bench kept its hour all that term, candles in the lee of the south wall, and the bench pushed, because pushing was what the bench had seen rewarded — small weathers up and down the line, gutter and stand, gutter and stand, the tallow weeping — and in the middle of the pushing sat one boy doing nothing at all. Kael felt where the flame carried, and went on feeling it, past sureness, past boredom, the way she taught: until the knowing is in your hands, not your eyes. The Temple's tallow. The yard's air. The cold, the bench, the hour — everything as the yard had it, and one thing changed. When the knowing had been in his hands a long time, he spent a thread.

The flame leaned. The width of a hair, once, exactly when he asked it and exactly where, and stood back up.

The boy on his left was fighting his wick with his jaw set. The girl beyond had gotten a gutter and was hunting it again. Down the line a candle did something worth a murmur, and the bench's faces went to it like gulls after one fish. Nobody had seen. Kael sat with the answer in him, enormous and weightless. Her way had won — not at the lamp, not at home, not with her across the table, but here, on the Temple's own tallow, in the yard's own air, with every other thing held still. Asked twice, one thing changed, answered. It was the first question he had ever put to the world with his own hands, and the world had answered him plainly — and the second half of the answer arrived a breath behind the first, the way thunder arrives behind its light: the answer could not be seen. A working her size did not show. The exactness was the hiding. Spend a thread, and the proof of the thread was yours alone.

He filed the two halves together, and could not have said why the filing felt like being handed something.

To the bench he was what he had been all term. The mountain's candle had done nothing again; the mountain's candle was a settled matter, like the weather of a place you don't live. The whisper had settled with it, gone from a thing said to a thing known — somebody's blood in that line somewhere — and it explained him so well that nobody needed to look at him to say it anymore.

The spring boards came and went. His name stood where the fouled slate had left it, one line lower than his sums could account for, and he read the column once and walked on. The boards were the Temple counting itself. He knew about counting.

What changed in him that spring was harder to see than a line on a board, because it was a thing not happening. In the philosophy room, in the chalk and the first flies of it, Priscian closed as he always closed, a question thrown to the benches, and the question stood there in the room with no one under it. Kael knew the weight of a standing question the way he knew the weight of the water pails; his whole life, a question standing in a room had been a lifted stone, and you got under it. He sat with his hands flat on the bench and did not get under it. The itch came up asking, the deep one, the one that could not be out-waited — and he did not out-wait it, because you could not; he only held it, the way he had held the wire in his leg beside the fence post, while another boy said the doctrine and Priscian tasted his thin soup and the hour ended. It cost more than the fence post had cost. He noticed that, and filed it, and paid it again the next time, because he had decided something without ever saying it to himself in words, and the deciding had a price, and he was learning what things cost.

Once that summer, in the colonnade with the heat standing in the court and nobody near, Elarine Sedrane fell into step beside him for the length of nine pillars. She did not look at him. "The mist," she said, as if the winter's room still stood open around them. "If nobody will say whose it is. How would you make it say?" And Kael, who had been handed a true question and knew only one thing to do with one, answered it: put the same kettle over two workings, a water-wright's and an air-wright's, change nothing else, and stand the two mists side by side and ask what differed — the mist would say, if you asked it twice. She was gone at the ninth pillar, no thanks given, nothing arranged. He filed the exchange under questions, answered, and thought no more about it. What she did with it, he never saw.

Lucan Drusane watched him that year the way you watch a sum that will not come right. He did nothing further; the slate had been done, and had worked. But in yards and doorways Kael would feel the long boy's eyes at their arithmetic, patient, unfriendly, a column being added and added and refusing its total.

Oss still sat down. That stayed true all year, and Kael kept it with the true things — but the sitting had moved, by degrees, the way a bench moves in a crowded hall: nearer the middle, where the listening was, and the laughing. Oss in a crowd was a boy at his trade. Kael did not judge it. He had a trade too.

---

He was eleven when the story came back up the island to find him.

The second cold term brought the storm-weeks, when the yard drowned and the cohorts were driven indoors between lessons, and the long hall in storm-weeks belonged to talk the way the sea-wall belonged to gulls. Stories were the coin of it, and Oss was rich.

It began three benches down, with a boy whose house supplied the Temple kitchens, and who therefore always had something half-true a day before anyone else. He had this one from a carter, and he laid it out with the storm doing his sound for him against the shutters.

"Two winters gone," the boy said. "The feast night — the longest night, when the whole village is down at the water till morning. The houses out past the boat-sheds, the ones that keep apart. Morning after, a man goes out with his mule and finds the whole crew of them dead. Every man. In the houses and out of the houses — doors between them, walls between them, and it made no difference. A whole crew of grown men, dead where they stood, and not a mark of a fight in the yard." He looked around the circle, collecting faces. "And no one left to say what did it. Not one."

Kael was at the bench's end, being furniture, and the count ran in him without being asked, the way it had run all his life. Two winters. The feast night. The houses past the boat-sheds, the ones that kept apart. There was no working-out to do; the story walked into him and sat down in rooms that were already furnished. And it carried the one thing in all its telling that was new, the one fact he had never had: the men had died. All of them. He received it the way you receive a weight handed over without warning, the whole of it arriving at once on hands that had been open for something else. He had gone up the mountain that night knowing nothing past the laughing, and had healed all winter knowing nothing, and had walked above those roofs twice a day for a year and a half knowing nothing — and a boy with a carter for a source had just handed him the end of his own night across a hall of children eating bread.

The circle drew in the way circles draw. Somebody said raiders; somebody's uncle knew the coast, and raiders take, and these men had not been taken from, and the word had weight for a breath and lost it. Somebody said fever, and was laughed down — fever does not do a crew in one night between the feast lamps and the dawn.

"It's the eyes," a small boy said into the pause, in the voice of a child playing his one good card.

The circle turned to Oss, because the eyes were Oss's; he had told that one up and down the cold term the year before, and he kept his stories the way clerks keep a roll. He did not play it at once — he never did; it was why rooms waited for him. He set the two tellings side by side first, the way you set out the caps, twin beside twin. "No one left," he said, counting it off on fingers his hands made graceful. "Doors and walls meaning nothing. A whole crew — whole houses, whole crews, that's the old telling, word for word." The last finger fell. "And nobody saw anything come or go. Well. That's not nothing, is it. That's the proof. Nobody ever sees it, because seeing one is the last thing you do. If one man had walked out of that yard to say what it was — then it wouldn't have been the eyes. No one walking away is how you know."

The circle took it in with the shiver it had come for. And the story, which had arrived off a carter's cart as plain and dead-eyed as a fish, grew there in the hall the way frost grows on a pane. Someone had heard the doors were frozen shut from the inside. Someone said the lamps in the houses had burned till dawn, which no one could know and everyone repeated. And the eyes came into it and stayed — red as coals, in the dark of the yard, seen by no one, which was the seeing — because a story that size needed eyes, and now it had them; and the thing the eyes belonged to came down off the mountain, because the mountain stood over the settlement and up was where such a thing would live. "It came down off the mountain on the longest night," the kitchen boy said, trying the sentence, finding it good, finding it the one he would carry — and there it was, made in front of him, a story with his night inside it the way a pearl has sand inside it. Not one face in the circle turned to him.

"No wonder nothing lives up that mountain but goats and the charity case," someone said, and got the crop of laughter such things got, and the talk ran on downhill. Kael's breath had found the long shape a while ago. None of it showed.

He listened the whole way through for a girl. The story had no girl in it. No girl, no woman, no one taken and no one loosed — no one left alive, said the story, and said it again, because it was the strut the whole tale stood on — and every time it was said, something in him stood at the edge of the words, looking for what was not there. He had carried one question out of that yard, had carried it a year and a half, filed where the unfinished things were filed — the wheel, the line, whether the line had held — and here at last was the night come back to tell itself, and it told everything, doors and lamps and eyes, everything except the one thing he had ever wanted to know. The story did not know she had been there. Stories kept what they needed. A girl loose was no use to a story about no one left.

He could have asked. The question stood in him the whole telling, fully formed, weightless, enormous: was there not a girl. Four words. He had never yet met a true question he could not put. He sat with his hands quiet and his face at the local temperature and did not put it, because there was no way to ask it that did not answer a larger one, and the not-asking was the hardest stillness anyone had ever asked of him, and nobody had asked.

And under that listening ran the other one: whether the story had a boy in it. Small, out of the dark, at a cartwheel. It did not. Whatever the night had left behind, none of it had his shape. He noticed what that noticing loosened in him, and noticed something under the loosening, and examined neither, and went on being a bench.

What he allowed himself, while the hall wore the story out, was the audit. The story was wrong the way stories were wrong, and he took a cold comfort in counting the ways, the comfort the vinegar gave — things that cannot lie. The doors had not been frozen. The yard had not been unmarked; some of the marks had been his. The lamplight had lain in the yard in one long blade, not burned in windows till dawn. And nothing with red eyes had come down the mountain that night, because what had come down the mountain that night was a boy. The story was garble; he had a shelf for garble now, the white that was just true and the eyes that were a wall-story with a grin in it, and this was the grin kind. It had his night inside it and knew nothing, not one true thing that mattered.

Except one.

He did not stand near it long.

---

He walked home under the tail of the storm, and where the road bent above the village the roofs came up at him one by one. Downshore, past the boat-sheds, the cluster stood apart with its smoke standing up like all the others' — ordinary, indistinguishable, a year and a half ordinary — and it was not the same. The story lay over the roofs now, and would lie there for good. The village had not buried that night, he understood, looking down through the grey; it had built on it, the way you build on rock. The night was a place now, a place a story lived, and stories did not die of winter. Salt keeps.

And out of the filed dark of him, unasked, one piece came up — a quiet the size of several men — came up whole, in its own old air, and stood there being the size it was. He walked. The road did what roads do. He put the piece back where the pieces lived, not touching, and by the pens he was a boy coming home from school, because that was true too, and it carried the rest.

His mother asked, because she asked everything now.

"A whole day indoors," she said. "What does the Temple do with weather?"

"Talks. Sums." He turned his bread. "Somebody's master had a cold."

The day he gave her had no story in it. He heard the hole in his own telling — he was good at holes; he had been apprenticed to them his whole life without either of them ever saying so — and she did not seem to hear it, and he did not examine his relief. The lamp was lit. The slate came out. Her questions ran under the Temple's answers, the way they ran every night.

In the loft, he opened the drawer that had no name and put the day into it, and the drawer would not shut.

It was heavy now. The temperature of a turned room. A face changed by a degree over a collection nobody paid. A bench with room in it; boots in a well-shed; a dead place under cord; one line on a board, permanent, official. And now a story loose on the island with his night inside it, red eyes grown onto it, pointing up his mountain. He looked at the whole of it the way she had taught him to look at anything that would not resolve — not with the eyes — and it resolved the way the knot had resolved, all at once, one loop carrying all the others. Everything in the drawer had come in through the same door. Every entry was the price of a moment when someone had seen him: a fast answer, a solved stone, a spar, a mountain with something in it. The drawer had been waiting for its name the way the new school had waited, and it had one now, in the grammar of the house he came from, sibling to the school that had killed the promise: what being seen costs.

And with the name, the decision he had been making all year without words finished making itself. He had thought, at the split rock, that the coat was the story — the herder's boy, the books, the letters from his mother. It was more than the story. The thread spent where no one saw it. The question left standing in a room. The word stepped around like a board that creaks. Smaller than you can. It was all coat, every piece of it, cut to his measure long before the gate by hands that had never once said the word, and it fit the way things fit that were made for you by people who know your size exactly. He put it on properly for the first time, lying in the dark, all of it, deciding it, and the deciding weighed what it weighed.

A story is not a wall, the master had said, on the road, the first day. Nobody wonders at a coat.

Below him the two voices traded the day back and forth — his day, most of it — and the wind came to the door and pushed, and the door held, and down the mountain, in every house the storm had kept indoors, the longest night went from mouth to mouth with its red eyes and its frozen doors, wrong in every detail it could be wrong in, keeping. And nobody wondered at the boy.
