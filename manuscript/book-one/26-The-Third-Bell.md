# Chapter Twenty-Six — The Third Bell

The road was full.

He had come down it alone in the dark for four years. Now there were carts on it, a family from the village walking in the ruts, a man selling something hot out of a basket to people who had slept on the ground. Somebody had painted a bed sheet and hung it off a cart rail. He could not read it from behind.

He went along the top of the ditch where the ground was firm, because that was where you went.

A boy of about six saw him first.

He did not say anything. He took hold of his mother's skirt, pulled it twice, and pointed with his whole arm. His mother looked. The man beside her looked. Then the cart stopped.

After that it went down the road ahead of him faster than he could walk.

He had come down this hill every morning for years past people who never once turned round, and it took most of a cart-length for the road to find out what was on it. Somebody said the word. Somebody further down said it louder. A man with a coil of rope over his shoulder stepped out into his path, and Kael's whole body went to work before his head had any say in it — the reach, the rope, the ditch behind, the four places he could put his feet.

What the man did was take his hat off.

"Go on, Ghost," he said.

That was the whole of it. He stood back into the ruts and let him by, and the road did the same thing the rest of the way down, opening in front of him and closing up behind. Somewhere in the middle of it a woman put her hand flat on his back as he passed, and he did not turn round.

He came level with the cart, and got past it, and read the sheet going away.

Two words, in whitewash, on somebody's bed linen, with a rope nailed through the hem so the wind would not take it.

He walked the last of the hill with his ears hot and a narrow cold sitting under his breastbone, and could not have said which of the two was bigger.

The arena from the landward side was a wall with noise behind it.

He sat in the cold under the seating with his back against new timber and the sound of thousands arriving over his head.

---

The sand had been raked flat overnight. The crater was gone. There was nothing on that floor at all — no wood, no cloth, no grass, not one thing that would take a flame and go on burning by itself.

Elarine Sedrane came out of the eastern gate with her hair bound and a sword in her right hand.

Her left hand was open and empty and held a little out from her body, because that was doctrine, and doctrine held that a Resonant kept one hand for the Art.

She came to her mark, set her feet, and looked at him the whole width of that white floor away, with the face she used on a page she had already read.

Their eyes met.

"Do not make me chase you," Elarine said.

"I'm going to make you chase me."

"I know." She set her feet. "I am asking anyway."

The third bell went.

A judge dropped his hand, and eleven thousand people made a sound, and she paid.

Both hands.

The fire came off her right and her left in the same instant and went three ways at once — one sheet low and flat across the sand at his shins, one wall climbing out to his left where a boy would have gone, and one that simply burst in the air in front of his face, because she had decided the air should burn there.

And she was coming forward behind it with the sword.

He went under the low one and it took the hair off his arms. He got his head out of the burst and the heat went into his ear and his eye began to water and did not stop. He turned off the wall and there was nothing left to turn onto, and the sword came out of the middle of all that light at his throat.

He got a hand on her wrist and it cost him. The edge went along the top of his shoulder and opened it, and her left palm came round flat at his ribs and he felt his shirt go and then his skin.

He broke and ran and she let him go, because she did not need him.

Out at the far end of that floor Kael stood with his shoulder running and his ear singing and the whole right side of him smelling of himself.

*Not with hands. Not enough.*

He had known it for days and had wanted very badly to be wrong about it.

He reached over his shoulder and pulled the wrap.

*Do not take them anywhere near a man who is trying to hurt you until I say.*

His father had said it in the dark on the ninth morning of the turn. Kael had carried it eight weeks and carried the blades through a whole day of these games without touching them, and it went off him now in the time it took to get one hand round a grip, because there was a girl at the other end of that sand who was going to beat him.

The arena understood before the blades were clear.

Eleven thousand people had watched this boy stand with his hands empty yesterday while a Drusane came at him with four years of a paid master behind it, and had made a noise about it that went round the building twice. Now the wrap came off and there was steel in both his hands, and the sound they made was not the same sound at all.

The threading went into the right-hand blade the way his father had taught it. Shoulder, arm, hand, steel.

It sat in there loose at the point and wanting to come home, and he had no time to make it better.

Elarine did not wait for him to be ready. She opened her hand and a fan of flame came across the sand at his legs, wide as a cart, and Kael did the thing he had built the last eight weeks out of.

He went at it.

The threaded blade came up through the middle of that fire and the fire came apart on it.

It did not go out. It broke — split along the line the steel took, went round him on both sides in two ragged halves that had nothing holding them together any more, and closed on empty air behind his back while he was already three strides past where it had been.

Somewhere above him a very large number of people stopped making any noise at all.

Elarine's face did something he had never once seen on it.

Then she was moving.

She threw fire off her left hand at his head and lunged with the sword under it in the same motion, which is a thing that should not be possible and is what a treasury with two years of drilling buys you. Kael took the fire on the threaded blade and it came apart, and took the sword on the other one and it rang, and both of his arms went numb to the elbow, and he was already inside her guard.

The heavy blade went into her thigh.

He felt it go in and he felt it come out and he was moving before she finished the sound she made.

*Cut her.*

*Keep cutting her.*

She burned him for it. She burned him hard, off both hands, at nothing range, and he got most of himself out and left some of his sleeve and a strip of his forearm in it, and the crowd came up off its seats in a wave that ran round the whole bowl.

They fought.

There is no other word for the next part of it. It was not exchanges and it was not passes. It was two children trying to end each other on white sand in front of the empire, and it went on for a long time.

She was fast. She was faster than anybody in that yard had ever seen her be, because nobody in that yard had ever made her be. Fire came off her in every shape she owned — sheets, walls, columns, bursts hanging in the air where he was about to put his head — and behind and through and under all of it came the sword, always, never once late.

He was faster.

That was the thing the north curve could not get over afterward, and the thing the boxes did not want to say out loud. She was a Sedrane with a treasury and a paid master and eleven years of it, and the boy with no house was faster than she was, and he had been faster than she was since the first exchange, and he was not tiring.

She burned him across the back when he mistimed one. He cut her forearm and then the same forearm again. She got a wall up under his feet and he took it on the boots and came out of it with his soles smoking and put the heavy blade through her guard into her ribs and did not get depth on it, and she paid the whole front of herself into his face at half a stride and he turned his head and lost his eyebrow and some of his hair and kept coming.

Blood went onto that sand from both of them. It went on going.

In the north curve Valeria Vel stood up.

She had not stood up yesterday, not once, not through any of it. She stood now with one hand on the rail in front of her, and she was not watching Kael's hands, and she was not watching his feet either.

She was watching their eyes.

---

Here is what Kael did, and eleven thousand people saw it happen without one of them being able to say afterward what it was.

He never stopped.

He would not stand and take a thing. Every single time she made him move, he came back — off the turn, off the boot, out of the smoke, at her, with one blade or the other, so that the fire she had just spent on putting him somewhere had to be followed immediately by more fire to keep him from arriving.

She could not stop answering him. He was always arriving.

And she was buying every answer out of her own body.

*Fire is not held. It is paid.* She had said it to him on the rim of a cistern when they were eleven with the whole superiority of somebody explaining her own art to a boy who had none, and she had been right, and there is no floor in an arena and no fuel in the air and nothing anywhere on that white sand for a flame to eat, so every wall and every sheet and every burst came out of Elarine Sedrane and nowhere else.

He was spending his legs.

She was spending herself.

The eighth time he came back at her the fire that met him came up at his knee instead of his chest.

He saw it. He had been waiting to see it, and it went through him with no pleasure in it anywhere.

The ninth time it came slower.

The tenth she did not throw at all — she cut at him with the sword, twice, fast and correct and entirely conventional, and Kael broke her line with the heavy blade and put the threaded one along her upper arm and opened it, and stepped back out, and let her have the distance.

He gave it to her on purpose. He wanted her to spend something to get him back off her.

She stood there breathing.

There was blood coming off her hand from the arm and blood on her thigh from the first cut and blood soaking down the side of her shirt, and her hair had come out of its binding down one side, and she was looking at him across a floor she had owned for the first four minutes of that morning and did not own any more.

She understood exactly what was being done to her. He watched her understand it. It was the worst thing he had ever seen on anybody's face and there was nothing in it he could use.

"Elarine," he said.

"Don't," said Elarine Sedrane.

In the royal box the princess had come forward in her chair with one hand flat on the rail. The woman behind her said something. She did not answer it and she did not sit back.

---

Elarine set her feet in the sand, put the sword down and took her hand off the grip.

Then she spent all of it.

The sand beside her turned to glass.

Not a wall and not a column — the fire came up out of the whole white length of the arena between them and rose and went on rising, and the sound of it went through the seating into the timber and up through eleven thousand pairs of feet, and every one of them stood.

Kael had seen the Art at candle scale thousands of times in his life.

Now the world showed him what the Art of fire truly was.

It came at him wider than the road home.

There was nowhere on that floor to be. No lane. No distance to get. About a second and a half.

He put both blades away, because he was going to need the hand.

Then he held the left one out in front of him, edge on, the way you hold a thing you mean to cut with.

Two at once.

The first was the earth — his cheap copy of the Vel's art, seven weeks and many failures of it. Not the arm. He could not do the arm. He had never once been able to do the arm. He put it into the one line down the outside of his palm, the thinnest place he owned, and he put all of it there.

The second was the threading, and it went into his hand the way it went into steel. Shoulder, arm, hand — and then nowhere left to go, because there was no blade on the end of it any more.

He felt his own minima come together along the edge of that hand, precise and small and absolutely exact, and it emptied him of every grain of capacity he had in about a fifth of a second.

The fire arrived.

---

There is very little to say about it.

It came apart on his hand the way the fan had come apart on the blade. It went round him on both sides and along the inside of his guard, and it took the skin off the back of his left hand and the whole outside of his left arm from the wrist to the elbow, and it went on doing that for a length of time that had nothing to do with a second and a half.

He did not put the arm down.

He held.

And underneath it all, in a part of him that was not doing anything else, a small flat voice that belonged to a woman at a table with two pails observed that fire eats what it is given, and that there was nothing on this floor and nothing in this air, and that she had bought this out of her own body and could not put it down and pick it up again.

The heat went off.

He came out of the far edge of it moving, with his sleeve gone and his arm the colour of a cooked thing and a blade back in each hand.

His own Art had been spent too. No more threading now flowing through his blade.

All he had left was steel, and every hour he'd ever spent learning to swing it.

She had the sword up before he was halfway to her, and her left hand was open and low and there was nothing in it, and there was not going to be anything in it again. He had made sure of that.

Both of them knew it in the same half second.

Whatever either of them had brought onto that sand in the way of the Art was gone into the air and the floor and each other's skin, and what was left standing at the middle of the empire's arena was two fighters bleeding, with edges.

And something came up through Kael that he did not examine and had no name for and would not have stopped if he had.

He was not tired. That was the strange part and he noticed it distantly, the way you notice weather. His arm was ruined and his shirt was stuck to his ribs and he had nothing left to spend and none of it had reached the part of him that was doing this, because the part of him that was doing this had been doing it since before he could remember, in the dark, on scree, against a man who never once hurried. The ability to fight was not a tool.

It was the first language he ever learned.

He came at Elarine Sedrane across that sand with a short blade in each hand.

And Elarine, backing off her lead foot and getting her point into line for a boy who was already inside where a longsword works, saw his face.

She would think about it for a long time and never once say it to him.

He was smiling.

Not a fighter's grin, and nothing to do with her, and nothing in it that was cruel about anybody. It was the face of a boy doing the only thing in the world he had never had to be taught to want. There was blood on his teeth from somewhere in the last two minutes and he did not know it was there, and he did not know his mouth was doing anything at all.

Elarine had spent four years learning that the Art is a tool and a house is a ladder and the games are a market. She had never in her life met somebody for whom the fighting was simply the good part.

She met him anyway.

She was very good. She was better with that sword than anybody in the Temple except the girl on the sea court, and she was faster than she had ever been, and she put the point into his forearm on the first pass and along his hip on the second, and it did not matter at all, because he did not stop coming and she had to give ground to keep the length she needed.

He took the length away from her.

That was the whole of it, and everybody in that building watched it happen and most of them could not have said what they were watching. He came in and he stayed in. Every time she made the room a longsword wants, he closed it before she could stand it up, and there is nothing a long blade can do about a short one at the distance of a table, and there were two of them, and he was faster.

She got one more real thing away — a cut off the back foot that would have opened his face if his head had been where his head had been.

Then he took her wrist.

Not the sword. The wrist. He turned it against itself the way his father had shown him on a mountain in the dark before he was ten, and the fingers came open because fingers do, and the sword went out of her hand and stood up in the sand three strides away.

Elarine Sedrane stayed on her feet for about two seconds.

Then her legs went, and she knelt down in the middle of the empire's floor with her hands hanging and her head down and her breath going in and out of her like something being dragged over stones, and did not get up, because there was nothing left anywhere in her to get up with.

Kael stood over her with a blade in each hand and waited to see whether she would.

The First Master was already on his feet.

"The bout is decided."

The noise came in over the top of it and buried the rest, and it came out of the standing ground first and off the seaward end and up out of the cheap seats where the island was, and then out of the north curve, and then out of the whole building at once.

"Kael, of the Island."

Kael got about four strides toward the gate before his own legs found out what he had spent, and then he sat down on the sand an arm's length from her, with a blade still in each hand.

---

The First Master was coming down.

Not to the rail. Down. Out of the host's box, along the front of the western curve, through the gate the judges used: an old man in robes and a chain crossing the whole width of that white floor at a walk, while the noise came off that building in a wave that did not stop.

He went to Elarine first.

Kael sat on the sand with his ruined arm in his lap, no more capacity in him than a wrung cloth, and watched the First Master of the Temple of the Southern Isles kneel in the middle of the empire's arena beside a thirteen-year-old girl and put both hands on her.

Nothing showed. That was the part nobody in the seats was ready for. No light. No sound. Nothing came off him at all — and the great slow noise of eleven thousand people came down, and down, and stopped. A building that size can be quiet if it wants to be. It wanted to be.

Her fingers moved. Then her feet. Then she said something Kael did not hear, and the old man answered it without stopping what he was doing.

He was there a long time.

When he came to Kael he took the arm and turned it to the light with a thumb well below the burn. A yard-master had done that a day earlier. A father had done it that morning. All three of them had learned it in the same trade.

"Look away, or don't," said Severin. "It makes no difference to the arm."

Kael looked.

It went cold first, then past cold into something with no name in any of the five languages he had. The skin along his forearm crawled, and knitted, and turned over the space of about forty seconds from the colour of a cooked thing to the colour of a healing one. It hurt more coming back than it had going away.

"You will keep some of this one as well," the First Master said. "Nature is not tidy. It is only quick."

"Is she—"

"She is very strong and she went to the bottom of herself in front of the empire." He did not look up. "She will be walking by this evening. She will be no good to anybody for a week, and there is nothing in this world that would make her admit it."

He did not let go of the arm.

"Were you aware of it?" he said.

"Of what?"

"You were smiling."

Kael stopped looking at the arm.

"Through a good part of the end of it," said the First Master. "While you were cutting her. I do not believe anybody in those seats could see it from where they were sitting. I could."

"I didn't know I was."

"No." The old man turned the wrist a little and went on with what he was doing. "I did not think you did."

The noise was still coming down off the building. Somewhere behind them they were getting Elarine onto her feet.

"Your father and your mother taught you to fight," Severin said, in the voice of a man going down a list.

"Yes."

"They did not teach you that."

Kael went after it the way he went after everything, and found nothing at all where the answer should have been. There was no day it had been put into him. No hour, no lesson it came at the end of, nobody standing over him saying *again*. It had been there before the parts of him that kept things.

"No," he said.

"No," said the First Master.

He finished the arm and laid it down across Kael's knee, and did not take his hand off it straight away.

"I am not going to tell you it is a bad thing," he said. "I do not know that it is. I have been wrong about people for longer than you have been alive, and I have been wrong in both directions." The hand came away. "But you will want to be able to see it."

"What do I do with it?"

"You watch it. The way you watch everything else, which you do better than any child I have ever put in that yard." He got his knees under him.

"All right," Kael said.

He meant it, and the old man heard that, and something went across his face and was gone before it finished.

Severin sat back on his heels for a moment before he got up. Kael saw the hands, because he saw everything and had never once managed not to. They were not entirely steady. It was the second child the old man had mended on that sand before noon.

---

They got Elarine up at the eastern gate, sitting, her back against the timber. Somebody had brought water. She had refused to be carried.

Kael came and stood in front of her because there was nowhere else he was going.

Somebody had put her sword down beside her, and she had not looked at it once. Her hands lay in her lap with the palms turned up, the way you rest hands that have been doing one thing for a long time. She looked up at him for a while without saying anything, and there was something happening in her face that he could see with total clarity and could not read at all.

"You were not kind to me," she said.

"No."

"Good." Her voice was a ruin. She took a mouthful of the water and held it before she swallowed it. "You made me buy too many things that were not there."

"Yes."

"I knew by the fourth."

"I know," Kael said. "I saw you know."

"Then you understand that I paid for the fifth one anyway." She put her head back against the timber. "That is the part I would like explained to me at some point in my life. Not today."

Somewhere above and behind them the horns went for the next bout. The noise started up again. Neither of them looked round at it.

"I needed it," Elarine said.

Kael waited.

"I am not going to say the rest of it, because I am extremely tired and it is a great deal less interesting than people think." Her eyes came round to him. "My house chooses the school and the seat and the man. But if I had won it. Perhaps I could have proved to myself that I was still able to write my own destiny."

She said it evenly, the way she said everything, priced and set down.

"But you? The city, the glory, you did not want any of these at all," she said.

"No."

"You wanted to win."

"Yes," Kael said.

"Yes," said Elarine Sedrane. "I have known that about you since we met. It is the only thing about you I have never once had to work out."

She shut her eyes.

"Go away, Kael," she said. "Come and find me tomorrow. It will be the match you really wanted."

He had a half-smile as he went.

---

He was most of the way up the hill before he noticed he was climbing it.

The sun had gone round; the sea was doing the thing it did in the afternoon. Below him the island was full of people, and the arena sat on the headland with the noise coming off it in soft handfuls. The games went on without him. They were four bouts from the end of the second day, and tomorrow there would be four children left.

His arm was a long pink new thing that would be a scar by the turn of the year.

His capacity was a dry hole. Any more spent, and his hair would surely have turned as white as the clouds above him.

He had beaten a house on the first day and a treasury on the second, in front of every family that mattered in the empire. There was a girl sitting against a gate down there who had needed it, and who would be sent wherever her house sent her. He had taken it off her because he wanted it.

*I wanted it.*

He turned it over the whole of the last mile and could not find anything underneath it. There was nothing beneath the wanting. There never had been. Everybody he knew had a *because* standing under theirs: a house, a name, a Line, a city. He had gone down that hill this morning with nothing at all under his, and won.

Then the house came in sight. Both bundles were still by the door, and there was smoke coming off the roof-hole the way there had been every evening of his life.

He stopped on the path.

Then he went up and in, and told them all about it.
