# Chapter Sixteen — The Terms

He kept coming. To watch. To learn. But, something more than that was true.

What he wanted to see had a beginning, and the beginning was too small to see from ninety strides. He gave it four mornings. On the fifth he came down.

He came down the way he had been taught to come down anything, which was slowly, on the outsides of his feet, reading the ground before he gave it his weight. The old sea court sat at the Temple's seaward edge where the wall gave out, three sides of broken colonnade around forty feet of flagstone that the masters had stopped teaching on before Kael was born. There was cover to within twenty strides of the stones. He had priced it from above for two weeks and it priced the same from below.

It did not once occur to him that there was anything strange about any of this. He had been taught to look at what he needed to know about from where it could not look back.

And then she did the thing he had come for, which was nothing.

She set her blades against the base of a column and walked out to the middle of the stones and stood there. That was all. From the trees it had read as waiting, as a girl arriving early to no purpose, and he had filed it as the one wasteful quarter-hour in a schedule with no waste anywhere else in it. Up close it was not waiting. Her weight was wrong for waiting. Her breath was wrong for it. Her eyes were open and aimed at nothing at all, and her face had the look his mother's face had over a slate, and whatever she was doing she was doing it hard enough to be tired at the end of it.

Kael leaned forward one inch to see her hands.

The stone under his knee was flat and dry and made no sound, and he made no sound, and he had not made a sound since he was old enough to be told not to.

"Nine strides," Valeria Vel said.

She had not turned around.

"You are too light to be one of mine," she said, to the empty court in front of her. "They come in at forty and they land on their heels. Stand up."

There was nothing to be gained by anything else. Kael stood up out of the gorse with his hands where she could see them, because that was a thing his father had taught him and he had never yet had a use for it, and walked down onto her stones.

She turned around.

He watched her put his face on the shape she already had, and the two things fit, and she did not look surprised. She looked the way she had looked at the end of the exhibition, with the awake thing in her eyes.

"How long have you been watching me."

"Fifteen—"

"Fifteen what?" She said it flatly.

"Fifteen mornings."

She sighed.

"You have yet to miss one."

"No."

"Where."

"The treeline. Two places. The gorse above the west corner mostly, because the sun comes in behind me there after the first hour and it makes the court easier to look at." He heard himself giving the account in the order he would have given it at his own table, and did not stop, because the alternative was to start choosing what to say, and he did not have anything to choose from. "The other one is lower and closer. I only used it twice. It's better for your feet and worse for your face."

"My face."

"I wanted to see when you resonate."

Something crossed her that was gone before he could take hold of it. "And did you."

"No. That's why I came down."

Valeria Vel considered him for a while. Somewhere up the hill a bell went for the first cohorts and neither of them moved.

"You will have to tell me why," she said, without any weight on it whatsoever, as a man says the tide is out.

"You beat me."

"I have beaten a great many people."

"You beat me *whole*," Kael said, and it came out with more of him in it than he had meant to spend. "I've lost twice in my life."

Twice. The thousands to his father he had never thought to count.

"The first time, something in me broke, and I've been building against it for three years. The second time nothing broke. Everything I have, worked when I called it, and it wasn't enough, and I've been trying to find out what the missing thing is, and I can't find it. So I came."

"And what have you found."

"That it isn't reach and it isn't speed the way I understand speed." He was aware, distantly, that he had not spoken this many consecutive words to another child in his life. "You spend before the exchange starts. Everyone I have ever fought spends when the trouble arrives. You have already paid by the time I ask the question, so the question is the wrong price when it gets there."

The silence went on long enough that he began to price the run back to the treeline.

"That is correct," Valeria said.

"I know."

"It took the capital's third master eleven weeks to say that to me and he had my instructors' notes." She walked to the column and picked up her blades, one in each hand, and came back out to the middle, and stopped at the measure without drawing it. "Sit down."

He sat down on the stones.

"There are things I am going to say once," she said. "First. You cut my arm."

"Yes."

"Do not apologise for it. Three people have apologised to me for that arm and every one of them was apologising to my house." She turned her forearm over. The line ran a hand above the wrist, healed pink, exactly where he had put it, and he found that he had known its exact position for four months without ever once having chosen to remember it. "The First Master wanted to stop the match. My chaperone wrote to my father." A pause, exactly the length it needed to be. "My father's letter asked how a Vel came to be standing where a waster could reach her, and instructed me to write back with the answer." She let the sleeve fall. "Nobody asked me what it was."

"Wet light," Kael said.

"Yes. Four hundred people decided that in the time it took a woman to cross a yard with a box." Her voice did not change at all. "You put an edge on a piece of wood. I have been in rooms with the six best-taught children of the capital and not one of them can put an edge on anything, and the empire has looked at what you did and agreed among itself that the sun was on the sea. I have thought about it every day since the spring. I am not going to stop thinking about it because you answer like a door."

"I'm not going to tell you what it is."

"No," she agreed. "You are going to show me."

He opened his mouth and found nothing in it.

"That is the second thing," Valeria said. "You have had fifteen mornings of me. That is an account and I intend to collect it. You will come at the same hour every day, and you will get as close as you can before I have you, and I will tell you the number. Then we fight, and you show me what you can show me, and I show you what I can show you, and neither of us explains anything, because I do not have the words for mine either." Something moved at the corner of her mouth and was gone.

"The same hour," Kael said.

"Every day. I will not be surprised twice." She said it as a fact about herself rather than a threat, which he was learning was how she said everything. "Random is for people who are hoping. I am not hoping. I want to know how close you can get when I am waiting for you."

He was already running it. The hour after the yard was the only hour he had; the road home took what was left of the light; and *Evening* stood at the end of every day of his life like a wall with a word written on it.

"After the yard," he said. "I'm home before dark or I'm not anywhere."

"Then after the yard." She looked at him with her head very slightly on one side.

"Yes," Kael answered, a half-smile forming.

"Very well," Valeria Vel said.

She lifted the left-hand blade to the guard she used and stood there waiting with the bored eyes coming awake.

"Do you know what I have been doing," she said, "for the quarter-hour."

"No."

"Good," she said. "Find out."

---

The terms held for eleven weeks.

He came at the hour and she took him at the door. That was the shape of it, and it never once got easier, and by the third week he understood that easier was not what was on offer.

Her rooms were in the guest range above the second court, and the way to them ran up an open stair, along a colonnade with no cover in it worth the name, and past an outer room where a woman with a chained book sat all day reading letters that were not hers. Kael priced the whole of it on the first afternoon and priced it again on the fortieth, and the answer never changed, which was that there were exactly two ways in and both of them were bad.

The first day she said his name at thirty-one strides.

"Thirty-one," she said through the door, before his foot came off the stair.

"That's the stair."

"That is the stair," she agreed. "It is stone all the way to the sea wall and it is the loudest thing in this Temple and you weigh nothing. Come in."

The second week he took the wall instead and got twenty-two. The third week he got nineteen, then twenty-four, then nineteen again, and lost the whole of the fourth week to a run of days where she had him at the bottom of the stair every time and would not tell him why, and he lay in the loft at night going through it and could not find it, and on the fifth day came up the stair on the outsides of his feet with his weight in the wrong place on purpose, walking like a heavier boy, and got to fourteen.

"Fourteen," she said, and there was something in it. "What did you do."

"Walked like Corvane."

"Say that again."

"You've never met me," Kael said. "You've met a weight and a rhythm. Corvane's is nothing like mine and there are two hundred boys in this Temple whose weight you've never taken, so I borrowed one." He shrugged. "It'll work twice more."

"It will work once more," she said.

It worked once more.

After that nothing worked, because he had run out of body to lie with. He sat with that for four days, and then he stopped trying to be quieter and asked the only kind of question he had ever been any good at, which was not *how do I hide from her* but *what is she actually doing.*

He ran it the way his mother had taught him to run anything, which was twice, with one thing changed.

On the Tuesday he came the whole way along the top of the sea wall and down the packed earth of the walk, off the flags entirely. Twenty-six. On the Wednesday he came the same route at the same speed and stepped onto the flags for the last thirty feet. Eleven. On the Thursday he crossed the second court on the flags at a crawl, slower than he had ever moved in his life, making no sound a human ear could have found in an empty room. Nine. On the Friday he went up the outside of the guest range instead, hung off the sill of a vacant room two doors from hers with his boots off the building altogether, and stayed there for a quarter of an hour.

She said nothing at all.

He hung until his fingers went, then came in through the vacant room and walked four strides of corridor to her door, and she said, "Four," through the wood, sounding for the first time in nine weeks faintly annoyed.

He lay in the loft that night and did not sleep, because the whole thing had turned over.

She was not listening. He had spent the Thursday proving a boy could be silent and still be counted. She was not seeing either; she had her back to a door.

She was reading the floor.

It was the same law. It was the law he had used at eleven years old on a rain-wet yard against Elarine Sedrane, a thread of earth spent off his own heel and run out along the packed dirt to her foot, because the ground that touched him touched her, the way sound runs along a beam. He had used it to reach. She was using it to know. Two people at opposite ends of one road, and he had spent nine weeks walking up it on his own two feet and calling it a stair.

The road was the problem. So break the road.

It took him nine days to do it once.

The idea was small and the doing of it was not. A thread of earth into the stone at the instant his weight arrived on it. Not to move the stone, not to lift or split or throw anything, nothing that would show. Only to trouble the grain of it directly under his boot, so that what went in had nowhere onward to go. Let the step die where it stood.

---

He took it to the cistern court, because he had learned over the summer that two other people thinking was faster than one person thinking, even when the one person was him.

He gave it to them with the entire reason cut out of it.

"I want to cross a stone floor," he said, "without the floor knowing."

There was a pause of a particular quality.

"Say that ag—"

"Do not," Elarine said. "He will say it the same. He always says it the same."

"Without the floor *knowing*," Aurelian said. "Floors do not know things."

"Lie down."

"I beg your pardon."

"Lie down, put your ear on the brick, and don't move."

And so the heir of House Corvane, who would one day hold a fleet, lay face down on the wet brick of a disused cistern court with his cheek in a puddle while a herder's son walked circles around him, and after the fourth circuit said, in an entirely different voice, "*Oh.*"

"Yes."

"I can feel where you are." He got up on his elbows with his hair full of moss. "I can feel where you are with my *face*. That is revolting. Do it again."

"Sit up, you look like a dropped bag," Elarine said, and did not look up from the flame standing on her palm, which had not moved in four minutes and was the only tidy thing in the court. "It is not that the stone knows anything. It is that the stone is all one thing. From the sea wall to that arch it is one piece of ground with joints in it, and you are hitting one end of it, and he is lying on the other end of it. Stop it being one thing and you have your answer."

Kael turned to look at her.

"What," she said.

"Say the middle part again."

"Stop it being one thing." She looked up then, and her eyes did what her eyes did, and she filed something and put it away without a word, exactly as she had been doing since the summer. "Corvane, get off the floor. You are enjoying this too much."

"I am enjoying it exactly enough." Aurelian sat back on his heels and considered the brick with real attention, and then said, "Very well. Put something between you and it. A rug."

"He cannot carry a rug."

"I did not say it was a *good* answer, Sedrane, I said it was an answer. It is more than either of you has produced." He looked at Kael. "What. Why are you doing that with your face."

"I can make a rug," Kael said.

"You cannot make a rug."

"I can make a rug the size of a boot, out of the floor, for about a quarter of a second." He was already crouching, already turning his hand over. "It doesn't have to be a rug. It only has to be a gap."

"Then it is not a rug," Elarine said.

"It's a rug if it works," said Aurelian Corvane, with enormous dignity, from the floor.

It cost him more than anything he had ever done. It was the smallest thing he had ever tried and it had to be built six times in six seconds, each one exact, each one under a shifting load, and every one of them came out of a purse that had never in its life had anything in it. The third afternoon of it put him flat on the bricks with his ears ringing and Aurelian's hand under his arm hauling him up, saying, "Enough. *Enough.* Whatever this is for, it is not worth being carried down that hill."

"It's worth it."

"Then tell me what it is for."

Kael did not say anything.

Aurelian looked at him for a moment. Then he let go of his arm, and said, "Right," in a voice with no injury in it anywhere, and went to fetch the water, because he had decided some time in the summer that this was a person he was going to be owed nothing by, and had not revisited it since.

Elarine watched the whole of it from the wall and said nothing at all.

---

On the ninth day he came up the last of the colonnade in six steps with a thread in every one of them, and put his hand flat on her door.

Nothing came through it.

He stood there with his palm on the wood and the grey coming up at the edges of everything, and counted to five, and then the latch lifted under his hand and he nearly went into the room after it.

Valeria Vel was a foot away, looking straight at him, with an expression he had never seen on her.

"Where are you," she said.

"Here."

"I can see where you are. I am asking where you *are*." She did not move out of the doorway. "There is nobody on my floor. There has been nobody on my floor for six steps. I have been holding it since the bell, the corridor is empty, and you are standing in it."

"I broke it," Kael said.

"Broke what."

"The floor. Under my foot, only while I was on it." He was leaning on the frame and had not decided to. "It's your thing backward. You're not listening to me, you're listening to the stone. So I stopped giving the stone anything to carry."

Valeria Vel stood in her doorway for a considerable time.

"Again," she said. "The last part. Slowly."

She had it diagnosed inside four days, which was the part he had expected. What he had not expected was the week after that, and the week after that one.

"You are not absent," she said, on the Thursday, winding her wrists and not looking at him. "That is the mistake and it is an interesting one. You are a *hole*. Every stone in that corridor talks all day — the woman at the desk, the boys on the stair, the sea, the cart on the upper road. And then there is a piece of corridor eight feet long saying nothing whatever, and it moves." She wound the other wrist. "Nothing is not quieter than everything else. Nothing is only quiet. I should be able to hunt you by the silence."

"Should."

"Should."

Kael sat down on the floor of her room, which he had never done, and put his head back against the wall and waited, because he had learned by then that she finished her own sentences and did not need help with them.

"I cannot," Valeria Vel said.

She said it the way she said everything, flat, priced, without one grain spent on how it landed, and it was the first time in eleven weeks that he had heard her say those two words in that order about anything at all.

"The floor can tell me what the floor is doing," she said. "You have taken the floor out of the conversation, so it tells me there is a quiet piece of corridor, and a quiet piece of corridor is also what a quiet piece of corridor looks like. To find the hole I need something that is not the floor." She turned her hands over and looked at them, and for a moment she was not in the room. "There will be something. Air moves. Air is a medium and it touches everything and it is never still, and if a man walked through it there would be a shape in it where he had been. I would need years and a school that does not exist and an element that is not mine."

"That's a lot of work to catch one person."

"Yes," she said, without any humour whatsoever, and then, a half-count later, with all of it: "You cannot beat me with a sword. You have beaten me with a floor."

"I can't beat you with a sword. Yet."

And there it was, the small hard fact of it, sitting between them on the boards: nine strides down to nothing at all, and she could not have it back, and both of them knew it, and neither of them said one further word about it, that day or any other day of that autumn.

It was the first thing he had ever taken from her.

He walked home that evening turning it over, and what came out at the end of the road was not the winning. He had been nothing to look at his whole life and had thought of that as the safest condition a person could be in. A girl with her back to a door had just explained to him, at no charge, that in a room where everything talks, the silent thing is the loudest thing in it — and that the only reason it was not going to cost him yet was that nobody alive had built the ear for it.

Yet.
