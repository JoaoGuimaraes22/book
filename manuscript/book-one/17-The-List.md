# Chapter Seventeen — The List

The terms ran on into the autumn, and somewhere in them he learned what they were costing her, because he could not help learning it. She was slower to her guard on the days she had held the sense longest. Twice she came to the court after and did not eat the bread she brought, and once, in the ninth week, on an afternoon when he was doing nothing clever at all, he watched her fail to find him at nine strides in an open colonnade in flat light, and stand very still for a moment afterward with a look on her face he had seen exactly once before, on his own face, in a pail of water, the morning his own lightning had come home through his wrist and taught him what the bottom of a purse felt like from the inside.

He did not tell her to stop. It did not once occur to him to tell her to stop.

"How far down does it go," he said.

"I do not know."

"You've never been to the bottom."

"I have been told where it is." She was standing very straight, which he understood, because he had been taught to do it too. "Nobody has ever asked me to go and look."

"Then go and look," Kael said. "Not today. Tomorrow, at the start, before the yard, where I can watch. Spend until it stops working, and tell me what it does on the way down."

Valeria Vel looked at him for a moment, and the tiredness went out of her face and was replaced by the other thing.

"Yes," she said. "That is the correct question." She bent and picked up her blades. "Do you know what happens to a thing you own and never spend? It becomes a story your family tells. Come on."

They did it on the Tuesday. It took a little under two hours, and at the end of it she could not hold a resonance the width of a hand, and had to sit down on the steps of the sea court with her head hanging. He crouched in front of her and asked her nine questions in a row, and she answered all nine, in order, in a voice going thinner as it went.

Then she was sick behind the column, and came back, and said, "Ask the rest of them."

He asked the rest of them.

At no point in the morning did it occur to either of them that this was an unusual way for two children to spend one.

He wrote the numbers in the blank book that night, in his own hand, under a heading, the way he wrote down everything he was ever given.

The fighting was the other ledger and it ran the wrong way first, which he had not expected and should have.

Eleven exchanges in the spring, in front of four hundred people. Nine, on the third day of the terms. Nine again. Eight.

"You got worse," she said, standing over him, not unkindly.

"You know me now."

"Yes."

"In the yard I only ever had to be strange once." He got up in the order his father had built into him, and something in his shoulder disagreed with the last part of it. "Everyone I've fought for three years, I beat them the first time and after that they were beaten before they started, because the second time they came in trying not to lose the way they lost. You don't do that. You come in trying to find out what I did."

"That is the only interesting part," Valeria said.

They fought at the sea court where nobody went, out of the way of masters and rules and the healer's box, and they did each other real damage, and neither of them ever once discussed it.

The Temple's yard had three laws and Ruthen standing over all of them: nothing above the shoulders, nothing the healer could not mend by supper, and stop when I say stop. There was no Ruthen on the old court. There was a girl who had refused to have a bleeding arm looked at in front of the First Master of the Temple, and a boy whose father had spent the summer coming at him in the dark to find out what broke, and between the two of them the question of whether it was allowed to hurt did not arise, because neither of them had ever met the idea.

She split his lip in the fifth week and looked at it with professional interest while he bled.

"Your head goes to the left when you commit," she said. "It always has. It is very small."

"How small."

"Two fingers."

"Then it's a tell and it's mine and I've had it all my life," Kael said, holding his sleeve to his mouth, "so it's going to cost something to take out. Go again."

"You should have it stitched."

"It's a lip."

"Yes," Valeria said. "Guard."

He gave her the threading in the sixth week and never once said a word about it.

It happened the way it had happened in the spring, which was in the middle of a losing exchange with nothing plain left that would arrive in time, except that this time he had come up the stair knowing he would spend it, and had known for four days, and had not decided so much as stopped declining. The steel took the thread the way steel took it now, whole, end to end, unasked, and the parry came exactly where her parries came, and the blunted edge went along the inside of her forearm and opened it.

She looked at it. She looked at the blade in his hand. Then she stepped back one pace, and put her own point down, and stood there in the middle of the sea court with her arm bleeding onto the stones, and said, "*There* it is," in a voice with more in it than everything else she had said in eleven weeks put together.

"Yes," Kael said.

"Again."

"You're bleeding."

"I have been bleeding since the spring, and I have been thinking about it since the spring, and you are going to do it again while I am watching for it." She brought her point up. "Slower. Do it slower."

"It doesn't have a slower."

"Then do it more."

He did it eleven more times that week and she took every one of them, and by the end of it there were four new lines on her forearms among the old white ones, and she had never asked him a single question about where it came from, and he understood — not quickly; he understood it in the loft, three nights later, the way he understood most things about people, late and in the dark and in the wrong order — that she was not going to ask. That she had made a decision about him in a treeline conversation in the summer and had not revisited it since. That there were, in the world, people who could hold a shut door without knocking, and that until now he had only ever met three of them and they had all made him.

He went cold in the middle of it and did not know why, and lay awake, and got up in the dark and went out to the block and put his hands on the steel until it was time.

There was one thing he did not do.

It came in the ninth week, in a bind, both of them locked at the forte with the blades grinding and her weight arriving through the steel like a door being closed on him, and he felt the thought stand up in him fully formed and utterly clear. *Metal is a road. The road does not stop where my blade stops. There is steel touching steel between us right now.*

He had written it in the blank book in the summer, on a page of its own, with two technical objections under it. The objections were still true. There was a third thing under them that was not technical, and it was a girl's forearm opening in a spring yard in front of four hundred people, and it had been under everything for four months.

He broke the bind and gave ground and lost the exchange.

"You had that," Valeria said afterward.

"I had something."

"You had something and you put it down." She was winding her wrist, not looking at him. "Why."

"Because I don't know what it does," Kael said, "and you're the only person on this island worth doing it to."

She stopped winding for about half a count. Then she went on winding.

"Fifteen," she said. "Today. That was fifteen exchanges."

He had counted, because counting was his. He had known it was fifteen before his back hit the stones.

---

The thing about the hour was that it belonged to somebody.

He worked that out in the eighth week and did nothing about it for three, which he would afterward recognise as the most characteristic three weeks of his life. The hour after the yard was the cistern court's hour. It had been the cistern court's hour since the spring, and the cistern court now met twice a week and found him not in it, and nobody said anything, because one of the two people in it was a boy who paid his invoices at full rate and would not have known how to raise this one, and the other was Elarine Sedrane, who did not repeat her work.

She let it run eleven weeks. Then, on a wet afternoon in the turn toward the cold term, she was standing at the mouth of the cistern court when he came past it, which was not on his way to anything.

"Your lip is healing badly," Elarine said.

"It's fine."

"It is not fine, it is closing over dirt, and you have a green bruise under your jaw the size of a plum, and last week you carried your left arm for four days and told Corvane you fell." She had her arms folded and her voice was at the register she used for corrections that mattered. "I have been counting. Eleven weeks. Twice a week is twenty-two hours, and I have had four of them."

Kael said nothing, because everything he had was a shape.

"I am not asking you where you go," she said. "I asked you a question in the summer and you gave me a reason with nothing in it, and I decided not to ask twice, and I have kept that, which has cost me more than you will ever be in a position to know." Her eyes did what her eyes did. "I am telling you that the court has an hour and you are spending it somewhere. That is all. That is the entire complaint."

"It's the Vel girl," Kael said.

There was a silence in the mouth of the cistern court of a particular kind.

"Say that again," Elarine said.

"Every day. After the yard. Since the summer." He heard how it sounded arriving and could do nothing about the order it arrived in. "She caught me watching her train and instead of having me disciplined she set terms, and I've been keeping them."

"You have been *fighting Valeria Vel* every day for eleven weeks."

"Yes."

"And in the summer, in that court, when I said we should ask her, you said no." She said it very evenly. "You said no in a beat and a half and could not put one word in the gap."

"Yes."

"Why."

And there it was again, the same gap, in the same court, with the same person standing in it waiting, and this time he made himself stand in it too instead of walking around it, because he had spent eleven weeks with somebody who did not walk around things and something of it had got into him.

"Because I wanted it," he said. "Just me. I've never had anything that was only mine before and I didn't want to hand it to a court." He looked at her. "That's the reason. It isn't a good one."

Elarine Sedrane looked back at him for a long moment.

"No," she said. "It is not." And then, having priced it exactly, and having decided something, and letting none of it onto her face: "Bring her tomorrow."

"She won't come."

"I did not ask you whether she would come. I said bring her." She turned to go, and stopped, and did not turn back. "That girl has been on this island eight months and has not once been in a room with anybody her own age who was not being paid to hit her. I am aware that you had not noticed. You notice everything and you had not noticed that."

"I noticed," Kael said.

Elarine paused half a step.

"Huh," she said, and went in out of the rain.

---

He asked her the next afternoon, badly.

He had built the sentence four times on the stair and it came out as none of them. What came out was, "There's a court. Sedrane and Corvane. Nobody comes there and nothing said in it goes into the hall. It's the hour after the yard, which is this hour, and I'd have to give it up to keep it, so." He stopped. "So."

Valeria Vel stood in her doorway with a blade in each hand and looked at him for what felt like a considerable portion of his life.

"Corvane," she said.

"He's better than he was in the spring. He'd tell you so himself, at length."

"I put him on the stones in six exchanges."

"He hasn't stopped talking about it since," Kael said. "It's the first thing that ever happened to him."

She was quiet. And then something occurred which he had no framework for at all, which was that Valeria Vel, who priced a court of six hundred in one pass and had never once in his sight taken longer than a breath about anything, stood in a doorway and could not decide.

"Why," she said at last.

"Because you're better than all of us and it hasn't done you any good," Kael said, and immediately wished he had built the sentence a fifth time.

She looked at the blades in her hands.

"Yes," Valeria said. "All right."

And her face, for about a quarter of a second, did something he could not read at all, and if he had been able to read it he would have seen a girl who had just surprised herself so badly that she had to look at her own hands to make sure they were still hers.

---

The cistern court in the cold term at the day's end was three walls of old brick, a bench nobody sat on, and enough light for another half hour.

Aurelian Corvane got to his feet when she came in.

He did it without deciding to, the way you stand up when the room changes, and then he was standing, and then it was too late to sit down, and Kael watched the tallest, loudest, best-born boy of the cohort stand in a disused cistern court holding his own hands and say, "Vel," in a voice that had somehow acquired a bow inside it.

"Corvane."

"I would like to say," Aurelian said, "that the sixth exchange was not representative."

"It was representative."

"Yes," he said, after a moment, and something went out of his shoulders, and something else came into them that suited him a great deal better. "Yes, it was."

"Sit down, Corvane, you look like a footman," Elarine said from the bench. She had not stood up. She had not, so far as Kael could see, moved anything except her eyes, which had gone over Valeria once and come back with everything. "Sedrane. You will be Vel. Two things, and then we can all stop performing. One: nothing said here is said in the hall, ever, including by me, and I am the worst of the three of us for it. Two: in here you are not the Line of Vel, because I am not a Sedrane in here and he is whatever he is everywhere, which remains the only honest position available."

Valeria looked at her for a moment.

"What are you in here," she said.

"Better than everyone in this Temple at one thing and bound hand and foot regardless," Elarine said. "You?"

"The same," Valeria said. "Different thing."

"Yes," said Elarine Sedrane. "I thought so."

And that, as far as Kael could ever afterward determine, was the entire negotiation.

They fought for twenty minutes and it was a shambles, because four people who have never trained together are four people, and Aurelian threw a fan of fire so large that Elarine put her face in her hands, and Valeria took him apart twice without appearing to be present for either of them, and then made him do the second one again slowly, four times, with her hand on his elbow, until he did it right and looked at his own arm as though it had been lent to him.

Kael sat on the bench with his lip stinging and watched it happen and did not have a word for what he was looking at.

It was Aurelian who brought the rest of it, afterward, in the last of the light, in the voice of a man reading a manifest.

"The tournament. The prize is a city," he said.

Elarine's head came up.

"My brother's letter came Tuesday. It's Ammareth, in the east." He said the name the way his people said names, as an object with a price. "Taken eighteen months ago. The empire has been sitting on it deciding what to do with it, and what it is going to do with it is give it away in the spring. Revenues, governorship, the seat. It goes to the winner's house, held in the winner's name until he comes of age."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"A city," Elarine said.

"A city."

"For a *child*."

"For a child's house," Aurelian said. "Which is the point."

"Who lives in it," Kael said.

They turned and looked at him.

"Ammareth. It's a city." He was aware of having changed the temperature of the court and could not tell in which direction, which was ordinary, so he went on. "There are people in it. There were people in it eighteen months ago and there are people in it now. What happens to them?"

Aurelian's face did something Kael had never seen on it, which was blank incomprehension without any contempt anywhere in it. He was not being cruel. He was explaining a road to somebody who had never once been on a road.

"Nothing happens to them," he said. "They go on living in it. They pay to a different name, that is all. You do not empty a city — there would be nothing left worth having. You take the top off it and put a new top on."

"And the old top?"

"Depends what the old top did." Aurelian shrugged.

Kael filed the shrug. He filed it whole, in one piece, the way he had learned to file things he had no drawer for, and he did not take it out again for a long time.

"Which is exactly the point." Elarine had come off the wall, and there was a colour high on her cheeks that Kael had seen twice before and both times it had meant something was about to be said properly. "Do you understand what that is? That is not a prize. Nobody gives a province to a thirteen-year-old because he is good with a spear. They are not choosing a champion, they are choosing an *order*, and they are doing it in one place, in one week, in front of everyone, so that nobody can argue with it afterward. Three schools. Every house in the empire that matters, sitting in the stands with a list." She turned on her heel and turned back. "The boards, Kael. It is the boards. It is the boards for the whole empire and it is written once and you carry it your entire life."

"Yes," said Valeria Vel.

They all looked at her.

"My father's letter said the same thing in fewer words," she said. "It said: they will be pricing you. Do not be cheap." She was cleaning her blade on a cloth, unhurried. "In the capital they have been drawing the lists for a year. Who will be watched. Who will be placed. Which of the coastal houses can be raised without offending which of the old ones. The tournament is where they write it down." She looked up. "It is not a secret. It is only never said in front of the children, and I have been in the room, because nobody counts me as one."

"So we are stock," Elarine said.

"You are being priced," said Valeria Vel. "Yes."

"And that does not trouble you."

"No."

Elarine turned round properly for the first time since the conversation had started.

"It is the whole of the point," Valeria said. She had not raised her voice and did not appear to understand that anything had changed in the court. "The empire will need officers in nine years and generals in twenty, and it cannot wait until then to find out which of us is worth a province. So it looks at us now, in one place, at once, and it writes down what it sees. That is not a cruelty. That is a state doing the one thing a state is for." She wound the cloth around her hand. "My line has been read that way for eleven generations. We have never once been asked whether we consented to be useful. I do not know what I would say if I were asked. I have never been able to think of an answer that was not an insult to my father."

Nobody said anything at all.

"You are all speaking as though being looked at were the injury," she said. "Being looked at is the only reason any of this is worth doing. If they stopped looking, what would you be? You would be four children in a wet yard." She finished the cloth and put her hands down. "I intend to be looked at. I intend to be written down first. I will win it."

And she said it exactly as she had said it on the steps in the spring, without a grain spent on how it landed, and this time nobody in the court made a sound.

Kael sat on the bench in the dark with his lip stinging and looked at the three of them: a boy who would inherit a fleet and had just discovered what the fleet was for; a girl whose house had chosen her school and would choose her seat and her man, and who did not pretend the rope was a sash; and a girl forged from birth by a line eleven generations deep, who had heard the same facts they had, and priced them the same way, and come out the other side holding them like something handed down. Two of them had just found out what they were. The third had always known, and was proud, and could not follow the other two anywhere from here.

And not one of the three had answered him about Ammareth. It had not been refused, or laughed at, or turned aside. The question had gone up into the air of the court and found nothing anywhere to land on, and the conversation had walked over the top of it on its way to the part that mattered, which was what the empire was going to write down about them.

Oss had said it a year and a half ago at a sea wall with his hands drawing in the air — *they're not teaching us, they're making us* — and had been laughed at by nobody, because Kael had been the only one there, and Oss was three tables away in the hall now and had not spoken to him in a year.

"Well," Aurelian said. "It changes nothing. I was going to enter and win it before, and now I am going to enter and win it while understanding what it is for." He said it lightly and it did not come out light. "Sedrane?"

"Obviously I am entering," Elarine said. "My house has entered me. There was no conversation."

"Vel."

"You have my answer."

Every one of them had answered with a *because*. Kael went looking for his own and found the work standing where the because should have been.

And then the three of them turned, in the ordinary way of a circle going round, and looked at Kael.

He had known this was coming for eleven weeks. He had known it, in some form, since a winter night at a table with a lamp on it when his father had said seven words in the fastest voice he had ever used. He had eleven weeks and four months and a year and he had never once built the sentence, because there was no sentence; there was a fact, and the fact had the mountain inside it, and everything he could say that was true would open the door on it.

"I'm not enrolling," Kael said.

The light in the cistern court was almost gone.

"Say that again," Aurelian said.

"I'm not enrolling."

"You are three lines from the top of the boards."

"I know."

"You lasted eleven exchanges against—" Aurelian stopped, and started again, at his own pace. "I have spent a year getting worse on purpose so that you could take me apart, and you are telling me you will be standing in the crowd."

"Yes."

"Why?"

"It isn't for me," Kael said.

Nobody said anything.

Elarine Sedrane did not move at all, and Kael, who could not read a room to save his life and had never once been able to, understood with total and unwelcome clarity that she had heard this sentence before, in this court, in the summer, about this girl, and that she was at this moment laying the two of them side by side and finding that they were the same shape, and that she had just been handed the second one and would not be handed a third.

She did not say it. That was the thing he took home. She looked at him with her face perfectly still and she did not say one word of it, and the not-saying went into the drawer with everything else and made it heavier.

"That is a shame," Valeria said.

Kael looked up.

"I wanted the rematch," she said. "In front of them. With everything." She slid the blade home into the case and closed it, and her voice did not change at all, which was how he had learned to tell when it mattered. "It is the only thing on the list I actually wanted."

She picked up the case and went to the arch, and stopped there, and looked back at him across the dark court, and Kael waited for the question, because everyone asked the question, because there had never once been a person in his life who had not eventually asked the question.

Valeria Vel looked at him for three full seconds, and turned, and went out into the rain.

The bell went for the last meal. Aurelian swore softly and went after her to say something about the sixth exchange. Elarine gathered her satchel with more care than it required.

"Tomorrow they nail the lists to the colonnade," she said, at the arch, not looking round. "First court. After the boards. Every name that is going, in earned order, for everyone to stand in front of and read."

Then she went out too, and Kael sat on in the dark with the rain coming into the cistern court, and found that he had known for a year that he was not going, and that it had been a rule, and a law, and a thing his father had said, and a door he had shut himself.

It had never once been a list with his name off it.
