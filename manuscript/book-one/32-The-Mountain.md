# Chapter Thirty-Two — The Mountain

His father did not slow until the split rock.

Then the ground went up in the way Kael's legs had known since he could walk, and the rest of him had stopped being able to feel anything about it at all.

"Kael."

"Yes."

"Breathe out. All of it."

He breathed out. It went about two-thirds of the way and stopped, and something on the right side of his chest put a hand up.

"Again."

He did it again. It stopped in the same place.

"Say a long sentence."

"There were four men in the yard and one of them was standing on the threshing floor when I came over the wall."

"Good," his father said.

They went on up, Kael's head against his father's shoulder. He could not have lifted it if the house had been burning at the top of the path. The coat under his cheek smelled of wet wool, of other people's blood, of the mountain. It had smelled of the mountain his whole life.

"Are you all right," his father said.

Kael did not answer, because he was working out what had been asked him.

It was not a question anybody could do anything with. It did not price a thing or set a thing or fix a thing.

"I don't know," he said.

"No," said his father. "Nor do I."

They came up out of the last of the trees and the wind found them, and Kael turned his face into the coat and got it out anyway.

"Is mother by herself."

"Yes."

He waited for the rest of it. There was not any rest of it.

"Father."

"Yes."

"Nothing," Kael said.

Above them the cloud had come down onto the top of the mountain and stayed there, and the underside of it was orange from one end of the island to the other, and it moved the way water moves.

The door was standing open.

It had never in Kael's life been standing open at night. The bar went into the stone every night. The wind came and pushed at it. The door held. That was a thing his mother did last, with her hand flat on the wood, and it was the sound the end of the day made.

His father did not remark on it. He turned side-on and carried his son in through his own door without breaking stride.

---

The fire was up higher than it had ever been allowed to go.

That was the first thing. The fire in that house lived down in its hole where the wind could not find it, and it was kept small because small was what you kept, and it was standing up now nearly to the height of the pot chain, and the whole room was moving with it.

The second thing was the water. Every vessel in the house was full and standing in a line along the hearth stone, the two pails, the pot, the wash-basin, a thing Kael had only ever seen used for salting.

The third thing was his mother, on her feet, with both hands on the end of the table.

She turned her head. She had her hair pushed back off her face with the flat of her wrist, and there was a long moment where Kael watched her go through the whole of it — the two of them in the doorway, the blood, the way his father was carrying him, the fact of him being carried at all — and get to the end of it and find that he was alive.

He had seen his mother pleased. He had seen her satisfied, and once or twice, when a thing he had built came out true, he had seen her proud all the way through with nothing over it.

He had never seen this. She made a sound that had no word anywhere in it.

Then a pain took her. She went white round the mouth and got her weight forward onto her arms, and her chin went down, and she was not in the room any more.

Kael watched his mother go somewhere he could not follow, and stay there, for a count of forty.

"Put him down," she said, when she came back. "By the fire. Not on the boards, get the fleece under him."

"Neris—"

"I heard you come up. I have been listening to that path since the light went." She was already moving. "Put him down."

---

She went over him the way she went over everything.

Fingers along the ribs, one at a time. She found it on the fourth and did not stop when he made the noise, because stopping teaches you nothing. Both hands flat on his chest to feel the fill. Her thumb pulling his eyelid up, and the lamp brought over so that the light came across and not into.

"Three," she said. "One of them is not where it should be and it is sitting on the lung. Has there been blood in your mouth."

"No."

"Say it again."

"No. I checked."

"Good boy." She went down his arm, and along the burn, and past it. "This is old."

"Yesterday."

"Yesterday," his mother repeated. Something went over her face and went away again. Her hands got to the thigh and he came off the fleece about an inch. "And this."

"It stopped answering on a wall. It has not come back."

She sat back on her heels.

His father was standing over the both of them. He had put the longsword against the wall by the door on his way past and had not let go of it until it was standing. Now his hands were empty, half-curled, and he did not seem to know where to put them.

"Vask," his mother said. "Hold his shoulders."

"Neris."

"Hold his shoulders."

There was a space then that Kael did not understand at the time and understood perfectly well about four seconds later, in which his father did not move, and his mother did not look up at him, and neither of them said anything at all.

Then his father knelt down on the boards and put his hands on his son's shoulders.

---

Kael had felt his mother carry something exactly once, on a flat rock above the pens, with the sea going on underneath them and her palm empty and the whole thing so small that he had spent a year afterwards not being sure.

This was not small.

It came up under his ribs like a hand pushing at a door that had swollen in the wet, and it did not go through, and it came again, and the third time something inside him moved and went on moving, and it hurt worse than the breaking had by a great deal. He got most of a shout out. His father's hands did not tighten and did not let go.

It was not clean. That was the thing he had nowhere to put. He had stood eleven feet from the First Master of the Temple the morning before last, in front of eleven thousand people, and watched him take two children apart and put them back together, and nothing had shown. No light. No sound. It had been like watching a man decide something.

This showed.

His mother's breath was going in short through her teeth. There was sweat standing out along her hairline that had not been there when she knelt down, and her hands were shaking on him, and she went on anyway, slowly and badly, the way you do a thing with the wrong tool because it is the only tool you have.

"Mother," he said. It came out with no shape on it.

"Be quiet," his mother said. "I am counting."

There was a piece of her hair hanging down beside his face. He had been looking at it because it was what there was to look at from where he was lying, dark and thick and coming loose out of the wrist she had pushed it back with, swinging a little every time she leaned.

While he watched it, the colour went out of it.

It did not go grey. Grey happens to a man over twenty years. It comes in stages. It is the colour of the sea in the cold term, of wet stone, of his father's temples. This was not that. It came up out of the roots and ran down the length of the piece the way frost runs across the top of a pail. Then it stopped, and it was white. Not the white of anything alive. The white of the seam in a stone when you split it.

Kael knew exactly what he was looking at.

"Stop," Kael said.

She did not stop.

"Mother. *Stop.* I can breathe. It's enough, it's—"

"You will be running tonight," his mother said, "and you will be doing it on a mountain, in the dark, and I have seen how you go up that scree when you are whole."

A second piece went. It went while she was talking about the scree, in the ordinary voice she used for facts, and she did not seem to feel it happen.

Then it was over. Her hands came off him and she went sideways, and his father took her weight without any part of him appearing to move first, and got her down onto the fleece beside her son.

The next one came on top of that, before she had got her breath back at all, and it was a bad one, and it went on for a long time.

Kael lay on his back six inches from his mother's shoulder with his chest filling all the way to the bottom for the first time since a threshing floor, and could not do one single thing.

---

When it let her go she turned her head on the fleece and looked at him.

"There," she said. "That is better. Say a long sentence."

"There were four men in the yard," Kael said, "and one of them was standing on the threshing floor when I came over the wall, and my father came in through the gate at the height of a man with the whole of him lit up and I have not got any idea at all what I was looking at."

His mother lay on the floor of her own house and laughed.

It was a wreck of a laugh and it went straight into a cough, and his father made the small sound in his nose that was as far as his father ever went, and Kael found that he was doing it too, and that it hurt, and that he was not going to stop on account of that.

"Come here," his mother said.

He got his arm over her, and she got her hand up into his hair and held the back of his head the way you hold a thing you have carried, and neither of them said anything, and his father sat down on the boards beside the two of them with his back against the bed frame.

In eleven years Kael had never once come into that house and found his father sitting. Three nights ago he had come in and found him in a chair. It had gone through him like cold water at the time, and he had not understood any of it.

His father sat on the floor of the room with his son and his wife and did not say a word about any of it.

Nobody was pretending. That was what Kael could not get past. His whole life had been kept in a certain shape by two people who were extremely good at holding a shape, and the shape was off, and what was under it was his mother's hand in his hair and his father sitting on the floor and nothing whatever in between.

He got that far and then he could not go any further with it, so he set it down, and lay still, and looked at the room.

---

"Mother."

"Not now."

He looked at her belly.

"Two hundred and forty seconds."

There was a silence with a lot of breathing in it.

"Say that again," his mother said.

"Between your pains. Two hundred and forty seconds from the end of the one before to the start of the one that has just had you." He had started the count the first time her hand tightened in his hair and had not put it down since. "The pains themselves ran a hundred and six and a hundred and two."

"What— how are you counting."

"The way you taught me."

"With everything else that is happening around us..."

"It doesn't stop," Kael said. "It has never stopped. I went on counting people this morning while I was—" He shut that one. "It doesn't stop."

His mother lay on the fleece with the light going across the white in her hair and looked at the ceiling of her own house.

"Kael."

"Yes."

"The baby is coming. I can feel it." Her head turned on the fleece toward him. "At the worst time."

Kael did not have anything to put there.

"I want you to know something, and I am going to say it once, because I may not get a better minute than this one." Nothing at all happened in her voice while she said it. "Whatever happens in this house tonight. You and this baby. You must live."

Kael could not answer that, and she did not wait for him to.

---

His father was at the door.

He had been at the door for some while. He stood in it the way he stood at the block, side-on, with one shoulder to the frame, and he was not looking down the path, because you do not look down a path in the dark; you listen to it and you watch the sky over it.

"How long," he said, without turning round.

"Hours. Three, four. It could be one." Kael put his head back on the fleece.

"Can she be moved."

"No," said Neris, from the floor.

His father took that. He came back into the middle of the room and stood there with his thumb going along his fingers, which was what he did when he was counting a thing that was not in the room.

"Then we hold this house," he said.

He said it the way people say their own name.

"They came off the boats onto every beach on the west and the south. They have worked inland all day. They took the village. They took the district road. What went past us on the east this evening was going over toward the goat ground. They will not have stopped for the dark. Men who intend to hold ground stop for the dark. Men who intend to clear it do not." He put one hand flat on the table. "Moving her in this state, would be dangerous itself. So it happens here. We hold this house until she is finished. Then we go over the shoulder and down the far side to the water, and we put her and the child in that boat. I do not care what we walk through to do it."

Then he went out into the yard.

He came back with two short blades.
He did not say where they had come from. 

These two did not match at all. One had a straight guard and the other had none. The grips were wrapped in two different colours of cord, both worn shiny, and the shorter one had a nick out of the edge two inches from the point that had been dressed out by somebody who knew how.

His father crouched down and put them on the boards beside his son's right hand where he could reach them lying down.

He put them down where the boy could reach them, and stood up, and went back to the door.

Kael did not say thank you, because it would have made it a smaller thing.

---

The noise came up the path a little after that.

His father was off the frame and out into the yard before Kael's own body had finished telling him about it. Kael got up and went after him with a blade in each hand and his ribs sitting inside his chest like a stack of plates.

Outside, the dark was full of wind and orange cloud. The pens went down the slope in front of them, grey and empty. And on the path below the pens, coming up in a line, were people.

Two lamps, badly held. Kael got to the shoulder of the yard, gave it four seconds, and had the number.

Fifteen. Two of the grey coats were being held up between other people's shoulders.

The second lamp was at the back of them, which is where the second lamp goes. There was nothing behind it.

You do not put a lamp at the back of fifteen people.

The first eight had order.

His father did not move. He stood in his own yard with his hands empty at his sides and let them come up.

At the front of it, out ahead of the lamp, climbing that path at the pace of a man who has been walking since noon and has decided not to stop, was the First Master of the Temple.

He was hatless. The robe was gone; he was in a coat somebody had given him, and it did not fit. He came up the last of the pens and stopped in front of Vask. Kael, standing four feet off with a blade in each hand, saw his face come out of the dark into the light off the house, and had to look at it twice.

Kael had the sentence ready. He had been carrying it since a gap eighteen inches wide in a wall at the wrong end of the island, where he had spent it whole on a man it did not reach: *he said I was not standing in it by myself, not me and not my house.* He had it in his mouth complete and he had had it in his mouth for nine hours and he opened it to put it down in front of the old man at last.

"Where is she," Severin said.

"Inside."

"How long has she been at it."

"Since the third bell."

Something happened in the old man's face and did not stay.

"Alone?"

"Yes," said Vask.

Severin looked at him for one beat. Then he went past both of them and into the house without another word, pulling his sleeves back as he went through the door, and by the time he crossed the threshold he was already saying his mother's name, and it was not the way anybody else said it.

Kael stood in the yard with his sentence still in his mouth, and put it away.

---

They came up past him into his yard, and he stood at the shoulder of it and read them going by because reading them was the only thing he had ever known how to do with people.

Eight in dark grey, no colour on any of them, and they did not bunch. They came up the last of the path and put themselves out across the yard without one word being said, each of them standing where a thing would have to come from, and the small one in the middle of them never had more than two feet of open ground on any side of her.

She was in the same shoes she had worn on a raised floor in a room with ice in a silver bowl. Somebody had put a soldier's cloak over a gown with gold along the hem. The hem was ruined to the knee. Her hair had been up in a construction that took more than one person more than an hour, and it was down now on one side. She was fourteen years old, she had walked six miles up a mountain in those shoes, and Kael watched her come into his yard and stand where they put her.

She did not look at the house. She looked at the cloud over it, and at the orange in it, and she went on looking at that.

Behind the guard, at the bad end of the line, the rest of it, and there was nobody in the rest of it he did not know.

Elarine Sedrane came up that path with her hands empty and her hair still bound severely and every part of her emptied out. She had been mended in front of the empire two mornings ago and had had nothing left in her then. She got to the top and stopped. She saw him standing there with two blades and blood dried to his elbows, and said nothing whatsoever. He could not read her face at all. That was so exactly the ordinary state of things between them that something in him turned over.

Aurelian Corvane came up behind her with his left arm held against his body. He looked at Kael. Then he looked at the house, at the pens, at the mountain going up behind it. Kael watched him understand where he was standing, and watched him decide not to say any of it.

"Kael," Aurelian said.

"Yes."

That was all either of them managed.

Lucan Drusane came up two behind him, walking on his own, badly, with a hand on the wall of the upper pen for the last of it. Two days ago Kael had knocked him unconscious in front of eleven thousand people and watched him carried out on a board without once looking round. He came up the path into the yard of the boy who had done it, and stood in it, and got his breath.

And three back from him, in a coat that was too good and had never been the right coat, his face open and turned and everything on it, was Oss.

Kael's whole body stopped.

Oss saw him. Kael watched it arrive: the recognition, and the thing after the recognition, and the hand that came half up off his side and did not finish.

There was nothing to say. There was a woman in labour eleven feet away and an island on fire underneath them and a year of three tables' distance in the hall, and there was not one single sentence in the world that would go into the gap between two boys standing in a mountain yard in the dark.

Oss got about half a step forward.

Then the line moved him on, the way a line does, and somebody behind him needed the ground he was standing on, and he went up past the woodpile with the others, and that was that.

Kael found he had turned to watch him go, and made himself stop.

Valeria Vel came up last, because of course she did.

She came up off the path at the back of the whole column with her twin blades on and both her forearms black to the elbow, walking the way she walked, which had not changed at all. She got to the top. She looked at him — at the blades in his hands, at his chest, at how he was standing on the leg — and did the thing she did, which was to price it in about a second and give nothing back.

"You are up," she said.

"Yes."

"You were not going to be."

"No."

She was quiet for a moment. Then she looked past him, at the house.

It was a small house. One room, a loft, a door that held. There were fleeces in the yard, a woodpile, a block with an axe standing in it, a line of pens going down the slope with nothing in them. It had a fire going too big in it and light coming out of the doorway onto the stones. It looked like every other poor house on that island. It did not look like anything at all.

Valeria Vel stood in front of it and looked at it for a long moment.

She had never in her life asked him one question about that mountain. Not when he came down off it with marks on him, not in a treeline, not on a sea court, not on a road with her hands full of other people's blood. She had had a year to ask.

She did not ask now either.

"Which way is the water," she said instead.

"East and down. Six miles over the shoulder."

"Good," said Valeria.

---

The argument happened in the yard, in front of everybody, and it took about four minutes.

The captain was a grey-headed man with a countryman's face and he did not raise his voice once.

"First Master. We do not stop here."

"We stop here."

"There is a woman in that house who cannot walk. That is very hard. I am sorry for it. It is not a thing I am able to weigh." He said it without any cruelty in it at all, which made it worse. "I came off that wall with twelve. I have eight, and I have six children behind them who are not soldiers, and I have lost every one of the rest between here and the far gate. Every hour we are standing still is an hour somebody uses. Give me the road to the boat and we will go. You may follow when you are able."

"No."

"Sir—"

"You do not know the road," Severin said. He had come into the doorway with his sleeves back and his forearms wet to the elbow, and he did not appear to be in any hurry about it. "It is not a road. It goes off the shoulder onto goat ground, off the goat ground into a runnel that is not visible from above at any hour of the day. There is a place in it where you must go down before you go up. Take the obvious line instead and you will come out four hundred feet over the water on a face nobody has ever got down. In the dark, with children, at speed. There is no describing it to you. There is no drawing it for you. I have been over that ground eleven times since the autumn. I will take you along it. I will not be sending you along it."

"Then send a man who knows it."

"There is no such man. That was the point of it."

The captain stood in the yard and looked at the old man for a while.

"How long."

"I do not know. Hours."

"That is not an answer I can use."

"No," Severin agreed. "It is the only one there is, and I am not going to improve it for you by inventing a number."

"Three hours," Kael said.

Every head in that yard came round.

"Three hours at the outside. It may be one. The gap between her pains was two hundred and forty seconds when I started counting and the last two were a hundred and sixty and a hundred and fifty. It has come down every time I have had two to put beside each other. Under eighty and it is close." He heard his own voice arrive in the middle of a yard full of the empire's people and kept going, because his mother had told him not to soften anything. "It is a number off five gaps. Do not build anything on it that will not hold if I am wrong."

Nobody said anything for a moment.

"Who is this," said the captain.

"That is the woman's son," said Severin, and went back inside.

---

They put the children against the north wall of the house, and the guard round the princess in the angle where the wall of the house met the wall of the store, which was the only corner of that yard that could not be come at from two sides at once.

The captain began laying out the rest of it off what he could see, which was the path, the pens, the open ground above the yard. He did it well and quickly. He got it wrong. Kael stood four feet away and listened to him get it wrong with his mouth shut, because he was thirteen and this was a man who had spent his life doing this.

"Ask my son," said Vask.

He said it flatly, from the door, without any particular weight on it, in the middle of the captain's own sentence.

The captain stopped. He looked at Vask. Then he looked at the boy standing beside him with two mismatched blades, no house, nothing on him anywhere, and he had every right in the world to say what a man in his position says to that.

"Go on then," said the captain.

So Kael gave them his mountain.

"There are three ways onto this ground and only one of them takes numbers. The path is the one you came up. It is made ground for the last quarter mile and it comes up into the pens, and it is the only line on this whole face that a body of men can use in the dark, because the other two cannot be done at a walk.

"The second one is the scree off the north shoulder, there, above the upper pen. You can come down it. I have come down it in the dark since I was eight. You cannot come down it quietly and you cannot come down it in a group, because the first man down it moves the whole face and everybody after him arrives at the bottom in whatever order the mountain feels like. If they use it at all it will be two or three men trying to get above us, and they will announce themselves the entire way.

"The third is up out of the runnel on the east and along under the olive line. It is the way I would come. It is slow, it is single file, it is blind from this yard for the last two hundred strides, and it puts you out there —" he pointed with the short blade, "— at the corner of the lower pen, forty feet from that door, behind everything you have put on the path. Put men there or the rest of it is decoration."

He did not stop.

"Do not stand on the woodpile. It is stacked green from this winter and the whole face of it will go with three men on it. Do not put anybody inside the pens; the gates open inward, the walls are four foot, the far one is dry-laid and coming down anyway. The ground between the yard and the upper pen is the only flat on this mountain wide enough for numbers to spread out on, so they will want it. It slopes toward the house by about one in nine, which nobody notices in daylight and everybody finds out about in the dark. Make them come to it uphill. Make them come up the path.

"And the path bends twice in the last hundred strides. It bends because of the rock under it. There is no way round either bend. At the second one the wall is above you on the left, the drop is on your right, and four men cannot walk abreast. Two can. That is the whole of it. Everything you have goes to the second bend and the corner of the lower pen. Nothing anywhere else at all."

He stopped.

The wind went across the yard. Somewhere down in the house his mother made a sound through the wall and he registered the time it happened and put it in the count without any part of his face doing anything about it.

The captain looked at him for two full seconds.

"Second bend and the lower pen corner," he said. "Four of mine to the bend. I want the two heaviest at the front of it, stood at the narrow. Three on the corner of that pen. One stays on Her Serene Highness with the Vel." He was already moving. "You. Boy. Stay where I can find you."

Nobody argued with any of it, because Vask had not argued with any of it, and every man in that yard had watched him not argue.

Aurelian Corvane, sitting against the wall of the house with his arm across his body, said, to nobody in particular, "He has been telling us for four years that everything is measurable and we have all been extremely funny about it."

Nobody laughed. Elarine Sedrane, beside him, with her eyes shut, said, "Yes."

---

Kael went up onto the woodpile, because it was the highest thing in the yard and he had no intention of standing on it in an hour.

From the top of it you could see the whole of the lower ground: the pens going down, the bend in the path, the black of the runnel cutting in from the east, and past all of it the island, and the sea beyond the island with the orange sitting on the underside of the cloud all the way out.

He had stood up here his whole life. He had stood up here at four years old and looked down at the lights in their corner and been certain they were something else entirely, and he had been wrong about that, and it had taken him five years to find out.

There were lights on the mountain.

They were below the second bend and off to the east of it. Four of them. They were not moving fast: he watched for a hundred and twenty and they went perhaps thirty strides.

Then there were seven.

Then, further down and further east, on the ground above the runnel where the olive line ran, there was a string of them coming along the terrace one behind another, and he counted that string twice and got nineteen both times.

He got down off the woodpile. He went to the door of his house and put his head in.

Severin was on his knees with his back to the room. His mother had both hands round the leg of the table and her forehead against her own arm, and she was breathing the way you breathe when you have decided about something.

"Ninety-four," Kael said. "The last two were ninety-eight and ninety-four."

"Ninety-four," his mother said, into her arm. "Good. Say the other thing."

He had not said he had another thing.

"They are on the mountain," he said. "Four, then seven. Nineteen on the terrace under the olive line and more coming behind those, and there is something down at the bottom of the path with a lot of light on it that I have not been able to count yet."

"How long."

"Forty minutes. Maybe less."

His mother turned her head and looked at him from the floor with the fire behind her and the white going all the way through her hair now, in two places, quartz-clean, and Kael stood in the doorway of the only house he had ever lived in and understood that both of the things he was counting were getting closer, at the same time, on purpose, and that he was the only person on that mountain holding both numbers.

"Then we had better both be quick," said his mother.
