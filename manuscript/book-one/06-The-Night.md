# Chapter Six — The Night

Below the split rock the mountain stopped being his.

It was the same slope. His feet knew scree, and this was scree; his breath knew the cold, and this was the cold. But every step was a step no one had made sure of, and the difference went up through his boots like the difference between the pond ice you had tested and the pond ice you had not. He went slowly, the way his father went when the ground was new — you did not learn this ground, you asked it — and the night was long, the longest of the year, and he had all of it.

Partway down, the world began to smell.

That was the first thing, and no one had told him it would be a smell. Tar. Fish, but old fish, fish that had been kept — salt, he thought, the keeper — and under it middens, and animals that were not his animals, and woodsmoke that was not his woodsmoke, many smokes braided, from fires he had never fed. The mountain's air had always been one clean thing. This air was crowded. He stood in it a while with his mouth open, tasting it, and his heart went strange and quick, because a smell that crowded could only be made by numbers of things, and the things were people, and they were near now, and real, and no rule stood between him and them anymore except all the rules he had ever been given.

Then the lights, close up, through the last of the pines.

They were lamps. He knew this now the way he knew the sea would not freeze — he had traded the stars for lamps, promise for fact, and had thought the trade was finished. It was not finished. Because no fact had told him about the *people*. The shore ran with them. Lamps end to end along the waterline, doubled and trembling in the black water exactly as they had always been from above, and moving among the lamps, dark against them, more people than his numbers had ever been asked to hold. They made a sound all together that was not any sound he had a name for — under the pieces of it, voices, laughing, a thing that might be singing done wrong or done by many — a low warm roar like the fire would make if the fire were happy. The plural of person. He crouched at the treeline with his knees to his chest and his hands quiet and watched them the way he had watched the six-oared boat, except that the boat had been one thing, and this was everything.

He wanted to go down into it so badly that his body leaned. And yet, he did not go down into it. The wanting and the not-going were both at their whole strength, neither winning, the way the wind pushed the door and the door held; and he understood, crouched there, shaking a little in his warm weathers, that he was afraid. Not game-afraid. He had no drill for this. Every stranger he had ever met was one old man, vetted and arrived on a known path, and here were hundreds, and any of them could turn, and see him, and *speak* to him, and there was no count for what came after that.

So he did what his training did with everything it could not enter: he went around it.

He worked along the rim of the lit world, high, in the dark the lamps made deeper, learning the village from outside the way he had learned the wall from inside — its shapes, its lanes, where the light pooled and where it failed. And it was there, at the failing edge, that the pattern snagged him.

A cluster of roofs stood apart from the rest, downshore, past a stand of boat-sheds. No lamps. On the night of all lamps, the night the whole world had carried its fire outside, a handful of houses sat in their own dark, and the dark had a shape, and the shape was wrong. He knew wrongness in a pattern before he knew anything else about it; it was the family trade. One star had gone out while he watched, once, and he had felt it in his chest, a little cold spot. This was the cold spot. He was moving toward it before he had finished deciding to, because a gap in a pattern was the most interesting thing in the world, and everyone he loved had made sure of it.

The first wrong thing was a door.

It stood open. Winter, night, and a door standing open, and no one closing it. His whole life a door was a thing that held — that was what a door was *for*, the way a floor was for standing — and this one hung loose on its dark, swinging a little when the sea-wind found it, knocking softly against its own frame like a thing asking to be let into itself. Kael stopped at the corner of a shed with his breath gone long and thin. The wrongness of the door went into him deeper than the crowd had, deeper than the smells, because the crowd had been a new thing and this was an old thing broken.

The second wrong thing lay in the doorway's spilled dark, half in, half out.

He knew dead. He was not confused about dead; he had unmade too many hares. The man lay the way nothing alive lies, the weight all given up at once, everything the body did to hold itself, stopped. Kael read him from the shed's corner, coldly, exactly, in the vocabulary he had — the dark under him had poured out and finished pouring some time ago; the cold had already gotten into him — and it was the exactness that was terrible, though he could not have said so. He had a school for this. He had never once been told what the school was for.

And the house was not quiet.

Voices, several, men's. And laughing — and laughing was a good sound. It was his mother when the bird got the song wrong; it was his father's one spent coin. It lived on the list of true things: laughing meant the day had gone right. The laughing came through the wall with the lamplight, and under it there was another sound, smaller, and the bones of it were wrong, and he had been taught his whole life that when a thing was wrong you did not turn from it. You looked. The eyes go up, go out, come back. He went along the wall to where a shutter hung as broken as the door, and he looked, because everyone he loved had made sure he would.

There were men in the lamplight, more than his two hands wanted to count, and they had a woman down on the table the way — and here his drawers began opening and closing, fast, finding nothing — the way a goat is held for shearing, except a goat was held quiet and kind and this was not; the way his father pinned him in the grammar of falling, except drills had a shape and a teacher and an end, and you tapped the ground and the weight came off, and she was tapping the ground, her hand was doing it against the table, open, again and again, and no weight came off. The men who were not holding were drinking and watching. One sat on the table's edge with his back to her head, loose, easy, the way you sit on a wall in the sun. She was making the small sound. It wanted to stop being made. It was being made anyway.

Kael's body did something then that no one had trained into it. His stomach rolled over slowly, like something turning in deep water, and his hands went cold in a way the winter had never managed, and a feeling came up through him that had no name and no drawer and no school — a hating, arrived whole, aimed at everything in that room and at nothing he could point to, because he did not know what he was seeing. He could not file it. It would not wait. He tried to out-wait it the way you out-waited an itch and it did not stop asking, and that was how he knew, without knowing he knew, that it was not an itch. It was the first thing his body had ever known before he did that no one had put there.

His eyes came off the window not by decision. They came off the way a hand comes off iron in the cold — pulled loose by the body, for the body's own reasons — and he was against the wall in the dark, breathing wrong, breath short and high and refusing the count, and the lamplight lay in the yard in a long yellow blade, and the laughing went on inside exactly as before, because nothing that had happened in him had happened anywhere else.

He should go. Every rule he had, from the split rock down, said go. He was turning to go when he saw the girl.

She sat against the wheel of a cart in the yard's dark, knees up, smaller than him or his size, he could not tell — and she was tied. Wrists behind, to the wheel, a line taken twice around and finished low where her hands could not follow it. Kael's eyes did what they had been trained to do before he could stop them: they assessed the knot. It was a good knot. Tied by a hand that had tied it many times, economical, no waste, the finishing loop set exactly where fingers could not — and something in him rose against the assessment like water against a wall, the same nameless hating, aimed now at a knot, at a good knot, at the goodness of the knot. His body objected. It would not say to what.

The girl's eyes found him in the dark. They were the first stranger's eyes to ever land on him, and they went wide, and her mouth opened —

— and closed. She looked at him and was silent, silent the way he knew how to be silent, and later, much later, he would understand what it cost a taken child to make that choice in that yard, and would not be able to think about it.

He was at the wheel without deciding. That was the truth of it, the truth he would keep afterward in the room with the shut door: there was no moment when he chose. There was a knot on a person, and he was the boy who untied things, and his fingers were already reading it, finding the architecture, the lazy place, the one loop that carried all the others — *there* — and he had it, he had the whole of it, the line already giving —

Light fell across the yard. A man stood in the open door.

For one breath nothing happened. The man was big the way the stone in the gate had been big, and he held a jug, and he looked at Kael crouched at the wheel with the line half-drawn, and his face did something slow, and he said a word Kael did not know — a word that needed no knowing — in a voice gone loud.

"What," said a voice from inside, unhurried, a chair going back. "What is it."

"A boy." The big man had not moved. He was looking at Kael the way you look at a tool lying where no tool should be. "At the wheel."

"Whose is he?" The doorway was growing men, dark against the lamplight, one and then three.

"Nobody's." A short laugh came out of the light behind them. "Look at him. He's nobody's."

They were not afraid. His body filed that even then, under everything, the way it filed weather: the voices were the voices of men sorting a strayed goat, a fallen rail — a small thing, found where small things get found, already sorted. And then the yard was full.

What happened next happened the way scree happens.

The first man reached for him, and Kael was not where the hand arrived — his feet had already answered — and the man's weight went on past its own lazy place and Kael helped it, two fingers of push at the loaded knee, no more, and the man met the ground with all of himself at once and did not immediately get up. It worked. It was the standing game and it *worked*, on a man, the mountain tricked, and somewhere far under the roaring in him a cold clean voice was counting.

Somebody laughed at their own man on the ground and the laugh was still in the air when the second man came with his hands wide, herding. There was iron in Kael's hand — his own short knife, arrived from his belt — and the knife-lesson met the world: *not the body, the carrying*, the cabled place above the wrist where a man's grip lives, and the blade told the truth there, one short honest line, and the man's hand stopped being a hand that worked and the man made a sound and folded around it. The eyes go up, go out, come back. His father's voice, calm as bread. Two men down. The cold voice counted two.

And then the wall of the house gave him the small sound again — the one that wanted to stop being made — and Kael *heard* it, heard it all the way, and the count broke.

It broke like the pond ice you had not tested. One breath he was inside the game, and everything was carrying and lazy places and lines; the next he was a nine-year-old boy in a yard of grown men in the dark of the world, and he could not find the long breath, and his eyes stopped seeing where the men carried and started seeing the men — their faces, their bigness, their number, the jug still rolling where it had dropped — and behind the men, the table, the lamplight, the open hand tapping, all of it at once, everything spilling, he was spilling, the whole held house of him coming down —

a hand had his collar. He went where it sent him. The ground came up without his father's hand between them, the first time in his life, the whole weight of the world arriving at once, and the sky swung, and something with a man's weight in it found his ribs and the air left him and would not come back. He did the drills. Somewhere in him the drills were still trying, hands moving in the grammar of falling, cover, turn — but the machine needed the count and the count was gone, and they were angry now, at the two of theirs on the ground, at the smallness of what had done it, and the anger came down on him in boots and fists with nothing between him and it but the body they were breaking. Iron came into it somewhere — a line across his guarding arm, cold and then very hot, the blade telling its one truth the way his own had told it — and even that got filed, far away, by the cold voice counting things that no longer helped.

He thought: *not enough.* He thought it without words, in the place where the lists lived. All of it, the scree and the stillness and the knife and the standing game, and it was not enough, he had spilled, he had —

Someone was laughing still. That was the last whole thing: that one of them was laughing, and his body hating it, all the way down into the dark.

After that, the night came to him in pieces, and the pieces did not touch.

A sound that stopped in the middle of itself.

Weight gone off him. The cold arriving where the weight had been, and the ground very close to his face, one ear full of his own blood-beat, and in the yard, nothing — an absence with edges, a quiet the size of several men, and no part of him could lift to look, and no part of him needed to.

Hands. He knew them before he knew anything else again; he had known them before the ground, before the slate, before the blade's want went out of the blade. They went over him the way they went over a tool, finding what was bent, and then he was up, in against wool and woodsmoke, the world tipped into a rhythm of walking, and the walking was climbing, and the cold got enormous and clean again and the crowded smells let go of the air one by one until there was only the mountain's one smell and his father's, which were nearly the same smell.

He surfaced once, partway. Stars — the true ones, cold and high. He could find himself by the stars. He was too broken to find himself and it did not matter; he was found; he was carried, the way everything on the mountain that fell was carried, and the arms were not gentle and were not rough, they were exact, and they did not shake, and he went under again inside their count.

The split rock stood up out of the dark, and past it, against the paler dark of the sky, his mother.

She did not run to them. She stood where the path ended, at the edge of the oldest law, straight and still, the way she sat at the window, and as his father carried him past the rock Kael felt her hand arrive at his head, and stay, the whole way up — one hand, resting there, riding the rhythm of his father's steps — and nobody said anything, the whole climb, and the silence was full of the third voice without any voice at all.

Then warmth. The door, closing, with him on the inside of it. The bar going down into its stone with the sound that meant held.

They laid him where the light was and their hands worked on him together, exact, unhurried, four hands that knew what was bent and what was broken and what would keep. His mother's needle went through the arm in small exact bites, herb-smell and thread, the mending school arrived at last at the thing it had always been for, and he watched it happen as if the arm were the table's, or a hare's, or the goat's. And the fire sat small and orange in its hole, eating what it was given, and Kael lay under their hands with his eyes closed and could not stop shaking. He was not cold. They had made sure of the cold years ago. He shook anyway, in small runs, like a line paying out, and behind his closed eyes the lamplight lay in the yard in its long yellow blade, and no one told him to stop, and no one said it was over, and downhill, far down, in the corner where the land met the sea, the feast burned end to end all the long night — every lamp lit, every light carried down to the water — and one small cluster of roofs sat apart from it in its own dark, and nothing about the dark, from up here, looked any different than it ever had.

He never learned whether the line had held.
