# Chapter Nine — The Ledger

The yard took the young cohorts together. It belonged to the master with the seam of white through his dark hair, a man built like old rope, lean and hard and used, the yard's weather in his face and knuckles like a counting-frame, who walked the way a cadence is counted. His name was Ruthen, which Kael heard exactly twice all winter, both times from a clerk. The children said *the yard-master* the way you said *the sea*.

He did not begin with weapons, though the racks stood along the south wall with wood enough in them to arm the island. He began with the roller.

It was a drum of grey stone the length of a man, kept for flattening the yard after rain, and the yard-master put his hand on it and said, "Move it to the wall," and stepped back.

They went at it the way the benches answered Priscian: severally, loudly, and with the first thing that came. Boys shoved, in twos, in fives, slipping on the frost, until Aurelian Corvane came through them and made it work: he picked the six biggest, set them low along one side, gave them a count — a real count, well given; Kael filed that — and on the third try the roller ground the width of the yard, all seven of them roaring, and reached the wall. The cohorts cheered. Aurelian stood off it with his breath tearing and his face bright, a boy standing where he stood best.

"Good," the yard-master said. "Again. Alone."

The bright went off the face by degrees, and the high carriage came down with it, half an inch, the way a banner comes down when the wind quits. It could not be done alone, and everyone knew it, and the yard-master let the knowing sit exactly as long as it wanted to sit. Then he said, to the line: "Anyone."

Kael went out, because, of course, a question was standing. There was a pry-bar in the well-shed and a chock of wood by the racks, and the rest was the spring he was eight: where a boy who came up to a thing's shoulder stood so that the thing's own bigness went to work against it. The roller came back across the yard in stiff little tips and shoves, one boy walking it the way you walk a barrel, and the yard went quiet in a way it had not been for the seven.

He set the bar down. He was not much out of breath, and he heard how that sounded in the quiet.

"Now the arithmetic," the yard-master said. "Seven of you spent everything you had, and it worked, and tomorrow your backs will tell you what it cost. One of you spent almost nothing, and it worked, and he could do it again all day." He did not look at Aurelian, and he did not look at Kael, and somehow that was worse for both of them. "Strength is a purse. Every one of you was born with one and no two are the same size. The ones who like spending always think the purse is the man."

In the line, Kael did his own arithmetic, because a piece was standing in the yard that the yard-master's had not counted. The roller had crossed the yard the first time because six of the biggest had pushed as one body, and they had pushed as one because Aurelian had set them, and given the count, and been Aurelian while he gave it. Kael could have given that count. He knew it was good; he had filed it. And nobody would have pushed. He set the fact where it belonged, beside the pry-bar: six backs, one voice — that was a purse too, and no eye could see into that one either. His own, he filed, plainly, the way he filed the weather, was empty.

Then the yard-master straightened his back, and proceeded.

"In two years, three for some of you, your art will come in, and you will want to spend it the way you wanted to shove that stone. So before I let any of you spend a grain, you will learn what spending is." He walked the line slowly while he said the rest, even, unhurried, a thing worn smooth by being true. The art was spending. Every Resonant carried a purse of it, and no eye could see into the purse. Spending was effort. Deep spending was pain. And spending near the bottom — the well-rope's creak came into the quiet — the body wrote down forever: the hair went white, from that day, in the measure of how deep the spending had gone, and it never went dark again. The body kept the receipt. And past the bottom there was no pain and no receipt and no Resonant, because past the bottom was death, every time, however large the purse or beloved the house.

Nobody looked at the seam of white. Forty children stood in the cold and did not look at it so hard that the not-looking was a thing they did together, one animal, and Kael stood in the middle of them not looking with everyone else, and understood that this too had been taught, without one word spent on it.

---

The first resonances came at the turn toward spring, when the yard-master judged they knew what a purse was.

They were given candles. The whole apparatus of the empire's art, that morning: a bench of tallow candles in the lee of the south wall, one child before each, and the instruction, which was to reach for the flame the way you reached for warmth through cloth, and push.

"Your art is in you already, leaning," the yard-master said. "It settles when you are grown — twelve, fourteen, when it settles it settles — but it leans from the start, and the strong lean hard. Today you spend one grain, so your body learns the door." And then, flatly, a wall being pointed at: "The art runs in the blood, and the blood runs in the houses. That is why the Temple takes who it takes." His eyes went down the bench and passed over Kael neither faster nor slower than over anyone, and the bench understood the exception without a word.

The candles began.

For most of the bench, nothing came, or almost nothing. But down the line the leanings showed, small and unmistakable, like the first minutes of weather. A girl from a river province made her flame gutter and stand, gutter and stand, and the tallow wept too fast, and her sleeve came away damp when she wiped her face, and she looked at the sleeve. Elarine Sedrane, three down from Kael — ink-dark hair bound severe, nothing on her that did not work — made hers do exactly what was asked of it and nothing more, one push, one bright answering stand of flame the size of the push, and folded her hands.

And Aurelian's caught.

Not the wick — the wax. The whole cup of the candle's top went up in a soft orange sheet, a fistful of fire standing off the bench, sudden enough that the boy beside him went backward off his heels; and the bench had turned to him before the flame finished standing, and for one breath, before the yard-master's hand came down over it with a wet cloth that was clearly always ready, the fire lit Aurelian's face from below, and it was the face from the roller morning with the world put right again. The bench cheered him — the roller's cheer, Kael filed, come home to its owner — and something in the yard settled back into its accustomed place with an almost audible click.

"A large purse," the yard-master said, neither warmly nor otherwise. "Learn the price of it." But the bench had seen fire, and had stopped listening to arithmetic.

Kael's turn had come and gone before that, third from the end. He reached the way she had taught him to reach without ever calling it reaching: he found where the flame carried, which took no time at all; it was a candle, it carried where candles carry. He pushed, one grain, the way you press a fingertip to a pail lid to know it is down.

The flame did not move. Something happened — he would have sworn it and could not have said what: a prickle across the back of his hand, a taste in the air for half a breath, gone before it was anything — and the flame stood there, and that was all. The child beside him had already looked away. Behind him someone said, not quietly, "What did the mountain expect," and someone else said: "Somebody's blood in that line somewhere. Someone's bastard, then." A short crop of laughter, many throats. His breath found the long shape on its own; none of it showed.

The filing took longer than usual that evening, because two entries would not sit still. The doctrine said the art ran in the blood and the blood ran in the houses. And up the mountain sat a woman born in no house any book of the Temple's had ever heard of, who had been teaching him to feel where a fire carried since before this yard had seen his face. The two facts stood in him side by side and did not touch, and he did not put them together. He put them behind the shut door, and did not stand near it long.

---

Twice a year, it turned out, the Temple wrote its children in order.

The midwinter examinations lasted four days — letters, sums, the chronicle, the natural philosophy, a day in the yard — and on the fifth day a clerk nailed the standings to the colonnade of the first court, cohort by cohort, every name in earned order, and the whole Temple came past before noon the way the village came to its water. Kael came late, in the crowd's ebb, and read up his cohort's column from the bottom, and found his name at last four lines from the top, above names with three parts to them, plain and short as a nail among rings.

In the ebb behind him a voice said, "Which one is Kael?" and a second answered it — "The charity case" — and a third read the arithmetic aloud, "Four lines from the top," in the voice you use for a wrong answer that will not stop being right.

The bench that had filled itself around him began, that same week, to have room in it.

It was not one thing. The bench. His boots, gone from where boots went during yard-work, found in the well-shed. *Charity case* arriving now from older mouths, the new word riding under it: *the bastard*, said like a thing everyone had agreed on while he was elsewhere. His body priced each as it came and declined to spend, and the declining, he began to understand, was itself the answer they could bear least.

Oss kept sitting down. That was the fixed thing in those weeks: the bench emptied around Kael and filled around him at the same time, one boy's worth of filling, arriving with the day's news and the day's names. It was Oss who told him, low, across the bread, which of them actually did things: "Corvane doesn't fetch and carry — that's what the four are for. And of the four, the one to watch is Drusane. Lucan Drusane, the long one. He works; the other three laugh where he points. Watch the pointing, not the laughing." Kael took the sentence with both hands, and it proved, like most of Oss's sentences, to be exactly true.

The spar happened because the yard ran spars, wasters out of the racks, pairs called by the master's flat voice, and in the natural course of a term the voice called "Drusane. The charity case." The yard found reasons to slow down and watch.

Lucan Drusane came to the middle a careful dresser even in yard clothes, all reach, a fencing-master's posture worn like inheritance, and did a thing with his waster: brought it up before his face, hilt at the chin, a small crisp ceremony of it, and stood waiting. "When you're ready, charity case," he said, into the waiting. "There's a form to it."

Kael stood, holding his wood the way you hold a tool between jobs, and waited for whatever the thing had been to finish, and it appeared it had finished, because the yard made a low sound and Lucan's face went from ceremony to appetite. Kael filed the gesture with the roll-book's chains: a fence, built out of movement this time. Nobody on the mountain saluted the weather.

He was good: a proper guard, a proper measure, lunges that came off a straight back like bolts off a crossbow. And at first he was strange — wasteful, Kael's eye said: the back held straight where bending was free, the blade kept to one line while whole roads stood open. But the number came back wrong, and he knew it was wrong, because the movements repeated too exactly to be waste. Waste wanders. This was built. The measure was priced for a blade with an edge and a length; the straight back was reach, spent along a lane both men were supposed to share. It was a man moving very well inside fences Kael had never stood in — a school built to fight its own graduates, asking only the questions its graduates asked. So Kael gave ground and let the school spend itself, and learned it while it spent. And when the longest lunge came — the form's best question, arriving on one straight beautiful line — Kael was a half-step off the lane the school assumed, and his boot was against Lucan's planted foot, at the ankle, where all that weight stood on one narrow decision the form had never been asked to defend. He pushed. Lucan Drusane went over his own front foot and met the yard with all of himself at once, waster ringing away over the stones.

Lucan sat up where he had landed, and did not reach for the waster. "What was that?" he said — not to Kael; to the yard, to the form of things. "That wasn't anything."

"Luck," the yard said, in several voices, deciding quickly, because the alternative did not bear standing near — and it was agreeing with him. And under the deciding ran the second offense: nothing the charity case had done had a name. No guard anyone could point to, no answer to the salute, no shape a fencing-master had ever sold. You could forgive a boy for beating you. It was harder to forgive him for doing it in no language at all. Kael did not argue with the yard. The yard-master, who had watched from the well with his arms folded, did not argue with the yard either; he looked at Kael for the length of one breath, the unhurried look, and called the next pair.

Kael went back to the line with the thing he had actually taken out of the middle, which was not the fall; the fall had been one push. It was the school. A second language of the body, whole, provable, learnable — and the itch was already at it. The yard had watched him win and decided he had no language. He had gone into the middle with one and come out carrying the first words of theirs.

Lucan's answer came in pieces, none of them signed.

The first piece was a waster with a cracked core, put back in the rack where Kael's height took its wood from: the crack seated deep under a whipping of cord, waiting for a hard parry to let it go. Kael found it in his hands in the instant of taking it up: the balance sat wrong, a dead place answering along the wood where live ash spoke whole; his father's fussing by the winter fire had taught his palms the difference. He worked that day's spar left-side-forward, keeping the dead wood out of the hard knocks — a herder-clumsy day of it — and won anyway, gracelessly, and put the waster back crack-up, so the next boy's hands would have a fair chance of finding it, and watched no one while he did it. In the corner of everything, a long boy watched him, face working through something it did not like.

The second piece landed. On the morning the spring examinations opened, Kael found his slate — the term's fair-copy sums, taken in first — wiped and fouled, the work gone, a smeared obscenity where it had been, done early and simply. There was no rebuilding four weeks of fair copy. He handed in the slate as it was, took the mark a fouled slate took, and said nothing, because he had no witness and knew exactly how far the sentence *the charity case says a lord's son crept into a room* would travel. The mark pulled his standing down one line, and the line stayed pulled, written, official — a small permanent receipt for someone else's spending.

He filed it, in the drawer with the room's changed temperature and a boy's changed face and a bench with room in it and a dead place under cord — the drawer with no name on it yet, getting heavy.

That night his mother asked what the marks had been, because she asked everything now, and he told her the number, and not the why. Both things were real. It was the family trade.

---

Spring filled the philosophy room with flies, and Priscian lectured through them.

The morning it happened he was on the elements — the six of them, their tidy fences — and he closed, as he closed every morning, with a question thrown at the benches. "The kettle, then. Water over the fire, and the water becomes air. Tell me the resonance of it, in the minima, properly. Anyone."

Elarine Sedrane answered, and her answer was the best thing Kael had heard in that room since the first day. She built it the way a wall is built, course on course: the fire's minima quickening the water's; the water's loosened, rising, taking the airy nature; each element keeping to its fence. It was complete. Priscian nodded along the length of it the way a man nods at good masonry, and had his mouth open for the next thing, and Kael — because her wall was so good; a wall that good showed you exactly the shape of what it stood on —

"Then whose is the mist?"

The room turned. He was under the stone now, so he kept going, at his own pace. "When the kettle breathes, there's mist over it. If a water Resonant can call mist off the sea, and an air Resonant can call mist out of a valley" — doctrine, both elements claimed it, the room had recited it a week since — "then the same mist comes from two arts. So when the mist is standing there, whose is it? What does the mist know that it is?" One breath more, because it was still coming: "The fences are drawn around the resonances. But the mist is only the mist. It has never heard of them."

Silence, one breath of it, with a temperature he was coming to know.

"The mist has never heard of them," Priscian said, slowly, not to Kael, not to the benches. Then he gathered himself, said, "The elements of the art are the elements of the art; we will come to the mixed resonances at the proper time," which answered nothing, and knew it, and let the benches go with the flies.

Elarine Sedrane had not moved through any of it. What Kael carried out was her stillness, which had changed when he asked, and then changed further, going in, the way his own went in when a pattern would not sit. She was not looking at him. She was looking at the fence-diagram on the great slate the way you look at a knot, and her face gave out nothing it had not priced, and the pricing was still running when the room emptied.

He was last out, being furniture. From the door, in the corner of everything, he saw her stylus come down on her own slate — quick, small, five words' worth of moving — and stop, and her hand lay flat over what it had written, the way you put your hand over a coin on a table.

---

The sea-wall walk was Oss's discovery, or Oss's gift; with Oss it was never fully one or the other.

It ran along the Temple's ocean side from the kitchens to the old beacon stump, out of the courts' sight, and nobody went there in the cold term but gulls. Oss took Kael out along it one grey afternoon on the strength of a mutton pastry extracted from the kitchens, and that was the afternoon Kael learned how Oss talked when there was no one to be seen talking well in front of, which was more, and lower, and true.

"You know what they say about my house," Oss said, to the sea, in the voice of a joke carried so long the joke had worn off it. "Vessaline. You can hear it, everyone can — a name bought to sound like the crown. My grandfather chose it off a list. I've seen the list." He stepped over his own house's name the way he always did, fast, a known bad board, and kept walking. "The old houses have been laughing at it since before I was born. You get good at knowing where you stand with laughing people." He said it lightly, and it was not light, and he did not look at Kael, and Kael filed, not judged, the true answer to a question he had never asked: why, on the first day, one boy had sat down.

"What's it actually like?" Oss said, at the beacon stump. "Up there. The mountain." And he stopped, and waited — really waited, the words all the way off him for once, his face open and turned — and Kael stood with the whole of it behind his teeth, the fire in its hole and the split rock and four languages and the woman at the lamp, everything true and none of it wearable, and what came out, after a silence one breath too long, was, "Quiet."

"God, I'd die," Oss said, relieved, refilled, off again like water finding its slope, "no, truly, dead in a week, the quiet alone—" and he was away, and the moment closed over itself, and both of them were served, and neither of them was met, and the gulls went over.

It was on the sea-wall, that same afternoon, that Kael got the stories. Oss kept ghost stories the way he kept house-gossip, sorted and current, and the cold term was their season. He did the witch-white one first, offhandedly, a coin already spent — "that one's just true, mind; my uncle's seen a hedge-wife gone white to the roots at forty; they spend too deep and the hair writes it down" — and then, warming, lowering, with the sea behind him doing the sound a story wants: the old one. The eyes.

"Red eyes," Oss said. "The eyes of the devil, the fishermen say — and my grandmother's people say the same from the far side of the archipelago, so it's everywhere, it's old. A Resonant's spending writes white in the hair. But there's a thing that isn't a Resonant, and when it works, the eyes go red as a coal — and where it walks, whole rooms of men die, doors and walls between mean nothing, and it never leaves one alive to say what it was. Every telling has that in it: no one left. Whole houses. Whole crews." He was enjoying himself immensely. "No one's ever seen one, of course. Because" — the grin of a boy delivering a story's oldest hinge — "seeing one is the last thing you do."

Kael watched the sea do its enormous grey work while his mind, without asking him, built a shelf and put the eyes on it: the body's two marks, one taught in a yard by arithmetic, one told on a wall by a grin. White, the receipt of spending. Red, the rumor of something that was not spending — that the fences had never heard of. He did not know why it was a shelf. Both things had gone onto it the moment the second arrived, and that evening it rode up the mountain with him, not heavy, present, all the way home.

---

"From the beginning," his mother said, when the lamp was lit and the slate was out. "Who taught?"

"The yard-master."

"How long did he sit you, before he let you spend?"

"Most of the hour."

"And his word for it?"

"Reach. Reach for the flame the way you'd reach for warmth through cloth. Then push." Kael turned the instruction over. "But it isn't reaching. It's more like listening — for where it carries."

Her stillness changed, the way still water changes when something passes under it far down.

"Does he sit you at it every day now, or just the once?"

"The once, so far. One grain, he says, until the body learns the door." He came back along his telling — the guttering candles, the weeping tallow, the fire that stood up off the wax — and then to his own. "Mine did nothing. I found where it carried and spent a grain on it, and nothing."

Her stillness changed again, at the same place, and her face gave out nothing.

"Show me," she said, in the voice of her ordinary evenings.

The candle came to the table, and his hand came up.

"Nothing yet," she said. "Feel it. Only that."

He felt it. It was a candle; it carried where candles carry; the knowing arrived inside a minute and sat there.

"Still nothing. Until the knowing is in your hands, not your eyes."

The lamp burned. The knowing went old, and boring, and sure.

"Now," she said at last. "The smallest thing you can do. Smaller than that. Spend a thread."

The flame leaned. The width of a hair, once, exactly when he asked it to, exactly the direction he asked, and stood back up.

In the yard it would have been nothing. At the lamp it was the most exact thing his hands had ever done: the yard measured what came out; she measured what it cost and where it landed. Two schools, one candle. He looked up with the next question already forming — *why smaller, when the yard wants bigger* — and met her face, and did not ask it.

"Smaller," she said, gathering the bowls, as if it were a remark about the weather. "Always smaller than you can. The ones who spend big learn what things cost. The ones who spend small learn what things are."

In the loft, one part of the day would not stay filed. The candle had answered her way and ignored the Temple's — and he did not know which difference had done the work: the long feeling, the smallness of the ask, the lamp, the being home, her. Too many things different at once.

He rolled over to sleep on it, and the dry wool crackled its small winter crackle against him, a prickle and a nothing, the way wool did.
