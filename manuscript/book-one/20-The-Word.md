# Chapter Twenty — The Word

There were four places on the road where a man could stand and not be seen from below.

Kael had known about all four of them since he was seven, the way a person knows where the doors are in his own house, and he had never once in his life thought of them as places where a man might be.

He went down by the goat line and came onto the road under the split rock, which cost him eleven minutes and put him on a stretch nobody walked before the light was properly up. He did the four places first, out of order, from above. Then the fold. Then the bend.

By the time the roofs of the village came up under their smoke he had priced nine things and every one of them had come back nothing, and he had learned that pricing nothing nine times takes exactly as long as pricing something.

At the boat sheds a man straightened off a hull and looked up the road at him.

Kael spent four minutes of a morning he did not have proving the man was a cooper. He got his proof — the wear on the outside of the right sleeve, the way the shoulder came round before the feet did, a man who spends his life putting a hoop on something that does not want it — and he put the proof away, and went on, and understood while he was going on that he would spend the four minutes again tomorrow on the same man, because a cooper on a Tuesday is not evidence about a cooper on a Wednesday.

His father had done this every day for eleven years.

Kael had grown up inside the result of it and had thought of it the way you think about a man being tall.

The Temple was where it had been. The gate stood open. The first court was full of boys complaining about the cold in the accents of six provinces, and a clerk was rehanging a lamp that had come down in the night, and nothing anywhere in it had changed at all, and that was the part he could not get level.

He had turned everything up. That had taken about ten minutes and had been almost easy.

What he could not work out, going in under the arch with his boots wet, was how you turned it back down.

---

She was in the cistern court twenty minutes before anybody had any reason to be, sitting on the bench nobody sat on, with her satchel on her knees and her hands on the satchel.

"Sit down," Elarine said. "I have four questions and then I am finished with it."

Kael sat down.

"Was the entry lodged before or after the standings closed."

"Nine days before."

"Does the provision require the boy's consent."

"No."

"Does it require the boy's guardian's consent."

"No."

She was quiet for a moment. The rain had stopped in the night and the court smelled of wet brick.

"Can a judge of the games be removed by a First Master."

"No," Kael said.

"Then that is all four." She did not sound satisfied. She sounded like somebody putting down a tool that had turned out to be the wrong tool. "I sat up three nights on this. Do you know what I found."

"No."

"Nothing. There is nothing wrong with it anywhere." She turned her head. "That is what is wrong with it. A thing that is done badly has a place you can put your hand. This was done properly, by a man with the right seal, under a clause that has been sitting in the law since before my house had a name, and every step of it would survive being read aloud in the capital by somebody who hated him. I have been looking for four days for the crooked part and there is no crooked part."

"The First Master said something like that."

"Then the First Master and I agree, which is not a comfort." She looked at the wall opposite. "I do not like it. I want to be clear that I do not like it, since nobody else appears to be saying so. A man arrived on this island two years early and spent two years being pleasant and then reached over the head of the man who runs this school and took you, correctly."

"He asked for nothing."

"Yes," said Elarine. "I had got as far as that too."

Kael did not say the rest of it. There was a quantity of rest of it — corridors, doorways, two years of meetings that had never once looked arranged — and he took it out and looked at it and put it back where it lived, and the putting back took no effort at all any more, which he noticed and did not examine.

She did not ask. He watched her not ask. She had a particular way of doing it, which was to let a silence run one beat past the place where a question would have gone, so that you knew the question had been available and had been declined.

"You didn't have to sit up three nights," Kael said.

Elarine Sedrane turned round on the bench and looked at him properly for the first time that morning.

"No," she said. "I did not."

"I only meant—"

"I know what you meant. You meant it as an apology and it came out as an accounting, which is a thing you do." She put the satchel down on the bench beside her. "I am going to say something now, and you are going to do something strange with it, and I have decided in advance not to hold that against you."

Kael waited.

"You are my friend," Elarine said. "It is not a complicated word. I have been using it about you privately since the summer, and I did not say it, because I could see exactly what you would do with it." Her chin came up a fraction. "That is why I sat up three nights. There is no other reason. I would like it written down somewhere that I did not do it because it was interesting, although it was interesting."

The cistern court was very quiet.

He did not respond immediately. He sat with his hands on his knees and did the only thing he had ever been able to do with a thing he could not price, which was to take it apart and see what it was made of, and he found that he did not have the word. It was not that he disagreed with it. It was that the word had never been handed to him, in the way that certain other words had never been handed to him, and he had reached twelve years old with a very great deal of vocabulary and a hole where that one went.

So he went and got the evidence instead, because evidence was a thing he did have.

"Corvane," he said.

Elarine's eyebrows went up half a hair.

"I think Corvane might be one as well," Kael said. "I don't know when that happened. I've been trying to work out when it happened and I can't find the day."

"There will not have been a day."

"There's always a day."

"Not with him. With him there is a Tuesday when he decides a thing, and then a year in which he behaves as though he had always decided it." She was, he realised, almost enjoying herself. "Do not tell him."

"Why."

"Because he will put it in a letter," said Elarine Sedrane. "To his brother. At length. With a heading."

Kael laughed.

It came out of him before he had any say in it, one short sound in an empty brick court, and it startled him more than it startled her, and it went away again, and what came in behind it was Oss.

He had not gone looking for him. That was the part that stayed. He had been running the word across the people he had, the way you run a rule across a row of figures to see which of them it fits, and the rule had come to the end of the row and gone straight on past the end into a boy three tables away in the hall who had not spoken to him in a year.

Who had given his name away in the first hour, for nothing, before Kael had known that names were things a person had at all. Kael had priced that on the spot and priced it correctly: it cost nothing, so it was worth nothing.

He had been right about the price. He had never once looked at the other column.

Was he.

Kael turned it over and could not get it to sit still. *Was* he — which put the thing in the ground with a date on it, finished, a fact about a year that was over. *Is* he — which was worse, because if that one was yes then it had been yes the whole time, three tables away, at every meal of a year, while Kael sat with his back to it and got on with his work.

He did not know how to find out. That was the entire difficulty. He could price a man off the set of his shoulder at forty strides in bad light, and he had no idea whatever how a person walks across a hall.

The bell went for the first lesson. She stood, and got the satchel onto her shoulder, and stopped at the arch with the light behind her.

"One more thing, and then I am finished with it for good." She did not turn round. "If they draw us against each other in the spring, you will not be kind to me."

"I wasn't going to be."

"Good. Because I intend to beat you." A pause, exactly the length of a pause she had decided on. "And if you hold back I will know, and I will never forgive it, and I am extremely good at not forgiving things."

"I know," Kael said.

---

It took him seven weeks.

He had been carrying the question since the first evening he had gone up the headland and watched grey coats put numbers on a girl, and it had lain in the blank book for most of a year under a heading with nothing under it: *what she does with it after it goes in.*

The trouble was that everything he knew about the Art was about getting something out. A thread went from him into a waster, into a floor, into the wet air in front of a boy's face. Hers did not go anywhere. She spent, and the spending stayed, and a flagstone came apart under her foot while nothing whatever had crossed the space between her and the stone.

He could not do what she did. He had worked that out in the first fortnight and it had not been a disappointment, because it was arithmetic. To do what she did you flooded a limb, and to flood a limb you needed a limb's worth, and everything he had ever had would not fill a cup.

So he stopped trying to do what she did.

*Everything heavy is lazy.* A gate does not resist you everywhere. It resists you in one place, and if you find the place you can move a thing that ought to be beyond you with your hands in your pockets. He had been eight when his mother put a stone in a gate and walked away.

A parry is not the whole arm either. A parry is a line: the point of contact, the edge of the hand, the two bones, the elbow, the shoulder, the hip, the ground. Weight arrives along that line and the line either holds or it folds, and it does not fold everywhere at once. It folds at one place.

He did not reinforce his arm. He had no idea how to reinforce an arm.

He reinforced the line.

It went wrong for seven weeks. It went wrong in eleven distinct ways, all of which he wrote down, because a thing that fails the same way twice is a fact and a thing that fails eleven ways is a subject. Then on a grey afternoon at the end of the cold term, on the old sea court with the water going white out past the point, Valeria Vel came through his guard with her weight behind the steel the way she had come through it four hundred times, and it did not open.

It held for about two seconds.

She stopped.

"Again," she said.

"I can't."

"Again."

"I can't," Kael said, and sat down, because his legs had been consulted and had returned an answer.

It came for him about four heartbeats later, on the stones, the way it always came: the drop, the ears going, the grey coming in from the corners. Six threads in six seconds had put him flat on the bricks of the cistern court in the autumn. This had been one thread, and small, and exact, and it had emptied him to the floor of the purse in the length of one exchange, and he lay on the cold stone of the sea court with his hand shaking against his knee and could not have lit a candle to save his life.

Valeria crouched down in front of him.

"What did you do."

"Wait."

She waited. He got about half of himself back and gave her the whole of it, because there was no version of it that could be given in pieces, and because she had asked the way she asked things, which was without one grain spent on whether he would say yes.

He told her about the flooding, and about the cup. He told her about the gate. Then he put his hand flat on the stones and drew the line for her with one finger — contact, hand, bones, elbow, shoulder, hip, ground — and showed her the place, one place, an inch across, where the line folded when her weight came into it, and told her that he had not strengthened his arm at all, that his arm was exactly the arm it had been that morning, and that he had put everything he owned into an inch of it for two seconds.

Valeria Vel looked at the stones for a long time.

"You did not do it the way I do it," she said.

"No."

"I have never thought about where it folds." She said it slowly, which she did not do. "I have been doing this since I was four years old. They put me in a doorway and put weight on me and I held it, and when I could hold it they put on more. Nobody has ever asked me where it folds, because I have never needed to know. There is always more."

"There's never more," Kael said. "That's the whole—"

"Yes." She looked up. "I understand. I am telling you that eleven generations of my house have not had to have your idea, and that is not the same as my house having had it and set it aside."

Then she said, "You will never be able to do it the way I do it," and it was not unkind, and it was true, and he had known it for seven weeks and had gone on for seven weeks anyway.

"I know."

"Good." She sat down on the stones beside him with her forearms across her knees. "Your wrist was late. It went in a hair after the contact instead of a hair before, which is why you got two seconds instead of four. Do it before."

"How do I know when before is."

"That," said Valeria, "is the part they cannot teach you either."

The light was going out over the water in the long flat way it went in the cold term. Neither of them got up. She had blood on one knuckle from something earlier and was not attending to it.

"You are still shaking," she said.

"It does that."

"How long."

"Couple of hours."

"And then you walk home."

"And then I walk home," Kael said, and heard himself, and found that having started to answer a question he was going past the end of it. "I don't sleep much any more."

Valeria did not say anything.

"I lie down and it starts," he said. "It's not — I'm not frightened. That's the part I can't get right when I say it. I lie there and I go back down the road and I check it. All of it. Every place I went past, in order, and what was standing there, and whether it was standing there yesterday. And when I finish I've usually found nothing, so I start again, because finding nothing once isn't finding nothing." He turned his hand over and looked at it shaking. "I turned it up. In the autumn. I turned everything up as far as it goes, and it worked, it works, I'm better at it than I have ever been at anything, and I can't get it back down. It runs all night on its own. It doesn't need me."

The water went on out past the point.

"I do not sleep either," Valeria said.

He looked at her.

"I am cold," she said. "Not the weather. All the time, and for as long as I can remember, and when I was small I thought it was the stone, because we live in a stone house and I was on the floor of it a great deal." She was looking straight out at the water. "It is not the stone."

"How long is all the time."

"I have just told you."

"No, you—"

"I am not complaining." It came fast and flat and closed. "I want that understood. I am telling you a fact about my body in the way that you have just told me one about yours, and if you make it into the other thing I will stop."

Kael shut his mouth.

"You have been going to say something since the summer," she said, after a while. "I have watched you not say it about forty times. Say it now, or put it down and stop carrying it about, because it has begun to be in the room."

There it was.

He had had it whole since a morning on the headland, when he had counted the same quarter of an hour four days running and got the same number, and had understood that nobody came early with her and nobody left late with her and nobody stood at her shoulder while she did the hardest thing she could do. His father had been beside him for everything. Beside him, and longer, and better, every morning of his life.

"Nobody stands beside you," Kael said.

She did not move.

"On that court. Not one of them. They call the number and they adjust you and they call the number again, and none of them ever gets up and stands in it with you." He was doing it in the wrong order and could not find the right one. "Everything my father ever asked me to do he did next to me, first, and he was always better at it, and that is the only reason I can do anything at all. That isn't kindness. That's the method. It's how you build a person. And nobody has ever once done it with you, and you are still the best of us, and I have spent a year trying to work out what you would be if somebody had."

The water went on out past the point.

"Say the rest of it," said Valeria Vel.

"That's the rest of it."

"No. The rest of it is that you have decided my father does not love me." Her voice had not risen anywhere. "You have thought it since the summer and you have been very careful with it, and watching you be careful with it has been like watching a man carry a lamp slowly around a room he has already set fire to."

"That isn't—"

"There is no word for it in my house," she said. "For any of this. Not the one you have been circling for a quarter of an hour, and not the other one. My father has never in his life said a sentence to me that was not an instruction." Her chin had come up somewhere in it and stayed up. "I have never wanted him to. You do not understand that and I do not require you to. An instruction from that man is worth more to me than every kind word anybody on this earth could put end to end, because he does not give them — so when one arrives, it is *true*. I would not trade one of them for a hundred of the other kind. Not one of them."

Kael did not say anything.

"He wrote to me in the autumn about an arm you opened. You know what the letter asked." She was looking straight out at the water. "I sat up past the middle of the night getting the answer right. And when it went, I was pleased with it." She let that stand exactly as long as she meant it to stand. "That is what my house is. It is not a sad story and I will not have it told back to me as one. It works. There are eleven generations of it and it works, and I am the best of them since my grandfather, and there is nobody on this island who can stand in front of me for eleven exchanges — and you were one of them, and to manage it you had to go away and invent something that nobody has ever thought of."

"I know."

"Then do not stand beside me," said Valeria. "Stand in front of me. It is worth more."

Kael sat on the cold stones and could not tell, in any direction, whether he had just been corrected or whether he had put something into her that would not come out again. There was no way of finding out. He had spent his whole life being able to find out.

They sat. The wind came off the water and went up the court and got in under everything, and neither of them moved, and somewhere behind him in the last of the light the road was going dark all the way up the mountain, one bend at a time.

"I don't have a word for this," Kael said.

"For what."

"This. Any of it. Sedrane sat me down in the autumn and told me I was her friend, and I couldn't say it back. I took it apart instead, to see what it was made of." He was aware that he was doing it badly and went on doing it, because doing it badly was available and stopping was not. "And Oss gave me his name in the first hour I ever knew him. He said everyone says Oss and it's faster. He handed it over before I knew people had names to hand over, and it didn't hold anything, and I think it was supposed to."

"It was supposed to."

"Yes." Kael looked at the stones. "So I've got no word. I've got everybody's word for everything else on this island and I haven't got that one."

"Then do not use a word," said Valeria Vel.

He turned his head.

"You have just told me you do not have one. So do not stand there waiting for one to arrive." She had not moved and her voice had not changed. "Use something else."

The court was nearly dark.

"Val," Kael said.

She did not answer.

It went on not being answered for long enough that he heard it going, the way you hear a stone going down a shaft and keep waiting after the sound should have come back. He sat with his hand shaking on his knee and did not take it back and did not add anything to it, because there was nothing to add to it, and because his father had spent eleven years teaching him that a thing you say twice is a thing you have started to bargain about.

"Nobody has ever called me anything," Valeria said.

Then she got up and got her case and went in off the sea court, and did not say his name either, and Kael stayed where he was on the cold stones with the shaking going out of his hand by degrees.

He could not tell whether that had been permission.

He sat there working on it long after there was any light left to work in, and the lamps came up along the water one at a time, and the last bell went somewhere behind him in the Temple, and *Evening* stood at the end of the day where it had stood at the end of every day of his life.

For the first time, he was going to be late for it.

He stayed a while longer anyway.
