# Chapter Thirteen — The Coat

The fever did not break with the spring. It set.

The tournament was two years out, and the Temple lived as if it were two months: the boards mattered in a new way, the yard mattered in a new way, and boys who had never volunteered for anything now stood extra hours in the rain because somewhere in the capital and the northwest, other boys were standing them too. The masters fed it. Ruthen ran the duels longer. Priscian assigned the chronicle's accounts of tournaments past, and for once nobody dozed. Aurelian Corvane trained like a banner in a steady wind, and his tier trained around him, and the whole island leaned toward the second spring the way a bench of candles leans toward a draft.

Kael climbed the boards one line that spring and held it, and worked, and said nothing, and the want stood in him at its full height all year and was given nothing to eat.

The dawn school fed something else, and it fed on him.

The threading, it turned out, was a school with a price of admission his father had never paid. A thread held quiet in a still hand was one thing; a thread held through the shock of a parry was another animal entirely, and that winter he learned what the difference cost. His father's purse bought margin: a thread of his could waver, lose its line for half a breath, and the purse absorbed the slop the way a big man absorbs a stumble. Kael had no margin. His threads were the smallest things the Art could make — that was the whole of his style, smallness made exact — and a small thread that lost its exactness under impact had nowhere to go. It did not die. It burst.

The first time, it took him at the wrist. His own lightning, arriving home — a white bite up the forearm that dropped the waster out of his hand and dropped him after it, and he sat in the frost with the arm ringing like struck iron and his eyes streaming, and learned in one lesson what the stakes of this school were. The burn was small. It was the size of the thread. That was the small purse's whole mercy and its whole cruelty in one: he would never have enough to maim himself. He had exactly enough to pay, and pay, and pay.

His father stood over him and did not help him up.

"I can be wrong by a finger's width," his father said. "The purse forgives me. Nothing forgives you. So you will be exact instead."

He was burned three hundred and twenty times that spring. He counted, because counting was his. Little white-pink speckles came up on the wrist and the back of the hand, the Art writing its own receipts.

---

The judge came with the first ships of the season, two springs early, and the Temple found this perfectly natural, because preparations had made early natural: surveyors on the headland, factors in the village, and now a judge of the games, come to walk the ground the empire would be measured on. His name was Halden. The hall said it once and then said "the judge," the way you said "the sea."

Kael met him in the first court, which was to say the judge met Kael, in the way that things kept happening to Kael that had the shape of accidents and the timing of appointments. He was a broad, comfortable man with grey at his temples and a voice like a warm room, and he stood watching the young cohort's drills from the colonnade until the yard-master called water, and then he was somehow beside Kael at the trough, remarking on footwork.

"You put your feet down like a man twice your age," the judge said, pleasantly. "Where does a boy learn that?"

"Goats, mostly," Kael said. "Scree. If you put a foot wrong on the mountain you learn, and if you don't learn you stop being someone who puts feet anywhere."

The judge laughed — a real-sounding laugh, easy, the kind that invited you to spend one back. Kael gave him the smile the coat wore, which cost nothing and said nothing.

It should have ended there. It did not end there. The judge turned up the way weather turns up — at the edge of the yard, in the colonnade between rooms, once at the gate at day's end with the road behind him — and he never asked anything a coat could catch. He did not ask where Kael slept or what his mother was called. He talked. He talked about the capital.

He talked about it the way Oss talked about the Temple — a map drawn in the air, a country explained by a man who owned the explaining. The library with more books than the island had people, where a reader could be lost a year and counted well spent. The bridges lit at night, lamp to lamp, so that from the water the city lay doubled like a second city drowned. The halls where the empire's judges trained, and the men of the Art who could be watched working at scales the island would never see. He was from nowhere himself, he said — a wool town in the north, a father who could not write — and look: a judge of the games, who had seen every province the empire kept.

"The empire is hard on the small," he said, comfortably, watching the drills. "Everyone will tell you so, and everyone is right. But it is also the only ladder ever built that goes all the way up. A boy with a rare Art and good feet — the capital finds uses for a boy like that. Finds patrons for him." He said it to the yard, not to Kael, the way you set food down near a careful animal and look elsewhere. "If such a boy ever wanted the road shown to him, there are men who show roads. I am one of them. That is nearly the whole of what a judge is."

And the want — the want that had been standing all spring with nothing to eat — turned and looked.

Kael said something that fit the coat, he could never afterward remember what, and walked up the mountain that evening with the whole conversation filed in three places at once. Beyond the sea. The words had not stopped being his oldest compass because a stranger had gilded them. Somewhere far down, in a drawer he did not visit, something had noted that the man's warmth ran level no matter what Kael answered, the way a lamp burns the same for anyone who walks past it; the note went in the drawer, and the drawer stayed shut, because there was no room in him to look at wrong things standing next to the one right thing anybody had said all year.

At the lamp his mother said, "And the new faces? The tournament men?"

"Surveyors," Kael said. "They measured the yard for stands. A judge watched drills." And then the day's remainder, whole, warm, in order — with a hole in it. She would never allow it. He did not decide this the way you decide things; it was simply present, load-bearing, older than the conversation: the capital was on the far side of a law, and the law had a face now, two faces, and the faces had said it kills. To pour the judge out at the lamp was to watch the door close on the one direction the want had left. So he was good at holes.

He did not examine what he had just done. There was no room in him for that either.

---

The arrangement, by summer, had a shape: the hour after the yard, twice a week, at the old cistern court nobody used — her fans, his reading, and a bench between them with whatever the day's argument was laid out on it.

"Again," Elarine said. "Smaller."

Kael stood with his palm up and a thread of fire on it the size of a coin, which for him was not small, which was the argument.

"It wobbles because you are holding it," she said. "You hold everything. Fire is not held, it is *paid* — you set the rate and it spends itself. Watch." Her own palm opened and a flame stood up on it, exactly coin-sized, exactly still, insultingly casual. "I am not holding this. I am refusing to buy more than this. That is a different hand."

"That's the purse again."

"Everything is the purse. You fight like a man spending his last coin because it is your last coin. I fight like—" she considered herself, fairly — "a treasury with discipline. Which is why your fire wobbles and mine is bored." The flame went out, all at once, the way a door closes. "Your turn. The back foot."

"Your back foot tells everyone everything," Kael said. "You reset to the same print, every time. It's why the ground could take you. Anyone who watches four of your exchanges owns your fifth."

"Nobody watches four exchanges."

"I watched two."

The look she gave him would have withered the well. "The correction is *good*," she said, in the voice of a person paying an invoice, and moved her foot, and made him drill her lanes until the light went — and somewhere in the middle of it, dry as dust: "The judge asks about you."

The word went through him and did not reach his face. "Everyone asks about me. I'm the goats' delegate."

"Hm," Elarine said, in the voice of someone declining, on principle, to be amused, and there was the almost-laugh anyway, one corner of it, priced and permitted. "He asked me what you *read*. Judges score bouts. Bouts do not read." She spent one more fan, smaller than the last, exact. "I told him you move your lips over the easy words. Mind the rate. You're buying again."

---

The joke happened at midday, in the autumn, in the long hall, and Oss told it.

He told it well, because he told everything well: up on the bench-end with his hands drawing, the middle tables turned to him, some story about the Temple's ghosts — and then, riding the laughter of the last one, easy as a man dealing cards: "Ghosts, nothing. You want mysteries? Watch the charity case and Sedrane. Twice a week, gone — *phht* — the two of them, off past the cistern court, very grave, very secret." His hands made a solemn little procession. "Nobody knows. Kettle-worship. A society of two. Ask them what they do up there — no, truly, ask them, I've been asking for a year and I get *Learn*." He did Elarine's flat voice, and did it perfectly, and the hall went up.

It was a crop of laughter, many throats, one animal, and it came down on Kael at the bench-end where he sat being furniture, and for the first time in a year the furniture failed.

Heat arrived in his face. His breath missed the long shape, caught it, missed again — the count, the actual count, needed for the first time since the first winter — and his eyes did the thing he had trained out of them and went to the room: the turned faces, the pointing, a girl saying something behind her hand to another girl, both looking at him. At the far table Elarine sat straight as a mast with spots of color high on her cheeks, and the laughter was running over her too — *Sedrane and the goat boy* — and that was the part that put the iron in it, the part his body priced before he could stop it: she was being spent, and the spending was his fault for existing where he was not supposed to exist.

Aurelian's tier poured it on for days, in the way of children who have been handed a new coin: the solemn procession mimed in the yard, kettles left on their bench with flowers in them, *the betrothal of the century, a herder and a house*. Lucan Drusane did not laugh; he watched, and added lines to a column. And Aurelian himself — Kael filed it from the middle of the noise, without a place to put it — Aurelian laughed once, the first time, shortly, and then not again, and by the third day was visibly bored with it in a way his tier took a while to notice.

What Kael carried out of the hall was none of that. It was Oss's face in the half-second after the laugh crested — the performer's smile holding its line while the eyes went somewhere else entirely, the way a man's eyes go along a wall when he is standing in a place he has chosen not to leave. Kael read the wrongness whole and could not read one word of it. He filed it as the herd, finally, winning. It did not sit right in the drawer, and he shut the drawer anyway.

He tried at the sea-wall two days later. Oss was there with two boys from the middle tables, and Kael came along the wall the way he had come along it for two years, and Oss saw him — Kael watched him see — and turned, laughing at something, and led the two boys off toward the kitchens at exactly the pace of a boy who had not seen anything at all.

He tried again the week after, alone, at the hall door. "Oss."

"Later, mountain boy," Oss said, bright, past him, already gone. There was no later. Kael stood with the word in his hands, and then filed it, because there was nothing else to do with it, in the drawer with the room's old temperatures — the drawer that had no name and was getting heavier by the season.

The seat at the bench-end stopped standing empty. It stopped being Oss's at all. It was just a seat.

---

"I despise them," Elarine said.

The cistern court, the cold term, the two of them and a bench of theory nobody was reading. It was the first thing she had said in a quarter hour that was not a correction, and she said it the way she said everything: exactly, with the price already computed.

"All of them. The laughing ones especially. Babies wearing names — they will inherit fleets and provinces because of their grandfathers, and not one of them has ever held anything they could not afford to drop." She was not looking at him; she was looking at the flame on her own palm, the coin of it, steady as a nailhead. "You think I chose fire? I did not choose the Temple. My house chose. My house chooses everything — the school, then the seat, then the man. I am as bound to my fate as they are to theirs. The difference is that I do not pretend the rope is a sash."

Kael sat still. Some things you did not interrupt for the same reason you did not step on new ice.

"You are worried the rumors cost me," she said. "You have been worried for a month. You worry like a man doing sums — it is very loud." The flame shrank one size, for discipline's own sake. "I'll say it once, and then we will not do this again. They cost me nothing I price. My pride does not take instruction from people I despise. I chose this arrangement because it is the only hour of this Temple where anyone says anything worth the fire it's written by, and I do not un-choose things because babies laugh." She closed her hand; the flame died like a door shutting. "You are the only person on this island who has ever beaten me with his mind. If they knew what that was worth, they would not laugh. They cannot know it. So: their loss, twice."

It was, he understood later, the kindest thing she knew how to say, and she had said it in full, and the collegial fiction that it was all rates and corrections held over it like a coat, and both of them let it hold.

Which is when Aurelian Corvane stepped into the cistern court.

He came alone, in the open, without his four — walked in the way you walk into a room where you are not certain of your welcome and are certain you deserve that — and stopped at a distance measured to the inch: close enough to speak low, far enough to be refused.

"Sedrane," he said. And then, to Kael, with the whole stiff weight of a boy to whom plainness costs more than blood: "Charity case." A breath. "Kael."

Elarine's eyebrow priced him. Kael said nothing.

"I am sorry," Aurelian said. "For the hall. For the kettles. For — the years of it, mine and my people's. Some of what has been done to you lately was done in my house's name, and I did not order it, and I did not stop it either, and Corvane pays what it owes. So." The word sat there, doing the work of several sentences he clearly did not have. He had rehearsed this, Kael realized; he had rehearsed it and it was still costing him at full rate, and he was paying anyway, in front of a witness, which was the part that made it real. "I am not asking you to like it. I am telling you the account is known to me."

"And?" Elarine said, because there was visibly an *and*.

"And I want to train with you." His chin came up on it, pride arriving to collect its due — "Both of you. The tournament is in fifteen months, and the yard teaches me nothing: they salute me, and then they lose to my name before they lose to me. You two are the only people in this Temple who work. I know what I bring. Capacity, and the yard moves when I say move — you have neither, either of you." It was said without cruelty, a man reading a manifest. "And I have watched the charity case win with a boot heel and a kettle, and I am tired of not knowing how."

Silence, one breath of it, with a new temperature.

"Conditions," Elarine said. "Your four stay wherever your four go when you are not performing. What happens in this court is not told in that hall. And here you are not a Corvane, because I am not a Sedrane here, and he is whatever he is everywhere, which is the only honest one of the three of us."

"Agreed," Aurelian said, too quickly, and caught himself, and said it again at his own pace, like a signature.

They both looked at Kael.

He did the thing he did: he priced it. The mass, the reach, the fire, the command — a purse he would never own, standing in front of him asking to be studied at close range. The risk — the coat, the court, the hall. The face — and the face was the thing: he read it the way he read weather, and there was no lie standing in it anywhere he could find, only pride and shame holding each other upright like two drunks, which, he had learned at a table on a mountain, was roughly the shape the truth made when it was carrying weight.

"What do you want to learn first?" Kael said.

Something crossed Aurelian Corvane's face that his tier would not have recognized. "Whatever you actually are," he said. "I've been fighting it for three years. I'd like to finally see it."

"No," Kael said, mildly, and felt the coat settle on his shoulders with its full winter weight. "You'll get what the yard gets, plus the why. The why is worth more anyway." And Elarine made a sound that was almost her laugh, one corner of it, and the court had three people in it now, and that was the shape of that winter.

---

The fever climbed all through the cold term, because now there was a number: at the spring, one year would remain. Lists went up. The older cohorts drilled at dawn — his hour, invaded, so that he watched other boys strike sparks off the wet air where he had learned to hold a thread through a parry, and none of them held anything through anything, and every one of them was allowed to be there in the open, with their names on a list.

And at dawn, in the black above the world, on a morning deep in that cold term, it happened at last: an exchange where the parry landed and the thread did not burst and did not die — it rode the wood. A stitch of white down the waster's length at the moment of the knock, there and gone, unasked, bought with a year of burns and three hundred and some receipts on his wrist. His father looked at the wood, and at him, for the length of one breath.

"Earlier than I thought," he said, which from his father was a speech, and they went again.

Kael sat at the end of the bench in the long hall, at midday, in the middle of the loudest winter the Temple had ever kept, and was furniture.

He was getting so good at it. That was the thing nobody would ever know, which was the point, which was the coat: a year of paying his own lightning its tuition in the dark above the world, and the first stitch of white riding steel where no one would ever see it; an hour twice a week where the empire's two best children of the Art paid his mind its exact worth — and then the bench-end, the small purse, the goats' delegate, the boy the judges' lists would never carry, sitting inside the roar of everyone else's coming glory with his face at the room's temperature. The want stood at its full height. It had stood so long now that standing was its whole posture. He fed it work, and it stayed, and he wore the coat over it, and none of it showed, and everyone he loved had made sure of it, and he had made sure of the rest.

*There is a thing nobody tells you about a coat, which is that the wearer pays for it. I wore mine so well that no one ever guessed the price — not the ones who cut it for me, not the ones it was cut against. I have worn heavier coats since. Much heavier ones. And on the days I have wanted to hate them for it — there have been days — I remember that every coat they ever cut me was cut to keep me breathing, and that both things were true. Both things were always true.*
