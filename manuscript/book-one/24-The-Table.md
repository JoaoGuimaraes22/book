# Chapter Twenty-Four — The Table

They came for him at the third hour, and they were exactly on it.

Two of them, in the heavy dark grey with nothing on it. They did not tell him where they were going and he did not ask. He built the route as they walked it — up out of the arena's under-passages, out along the seaward side where the drying-frames had been, past a line of tents with lamps inside them, and up eleven steps onto a floor that had not existed in the autumn.

The whole of it had been raised in six weeks and would be taken down in one. He had watched the poles go in.

At the top of the steps the taller of the two put out a hand, and Kael stopped.

"Arms."

He took the wrap off his back and held it out, and the man took it without opening it and passed it behind him.

"The rest."

Kael thought about that for exactly as long as it took to understand that the man was not guessing.

He reached inside the coat and brought the knife out and put it into the grey glove.

The guard looked at it. Then he looked, one beat longer than the job required, at the place on Kael's coat the knife had come out of — which was not on the belt, and was not anywhere a boy carries a knife to be seen carrying one. Something in his face moved by about a degree, then went back where it had been.

He did not say anything. He put the knife with the wrap.

"Straight in. Do not turn your back on her when you go."

---

The first thing was the smell, and it arrived before the doorway did.

It came down the six feet of passage and went into him and kept going. Perfume, hot and sweet and heavy, the kind that is put into a room on purpose. Under it, charcoal. Under that, meat — more than one, three at least, and one of them was mutton and one of them was something he had never smelled in his life. Fish, grilled, with the oil still going. Bread. Wine, which he knew from the Temple's high table at a distance and had never been near. Something green and cut and dying.

He came through the doorway and the rest of it landed on him all at once.

The room was hot. There were braziers in it, four of them, burning coals in the spring, in a room already full of people, for no reason his body could find. There were lamps along both walls and lamps on stands and candles on the table, and the light off the gold came back up under all of it, and there was gold on the plate and gold in the cloth and gold beaten flat and nailed along the edge of a screen that was there to stand in front of nothing.

And there was the table.

It ran the length of the room and there was food on all of it. Whole fish laid out in a row with their eyes in. Three joints. Birds he had no name for, small, arranged. Bread in a shape bread has no reason to be in. Fruit that did not grow on this island and did not grow anywhere at this time of year, piled, and some of it was already going. Flowers cut and lying along the middle of it, out of season, wilting in the heat off the braziers. Glass. Glass everywhere.

And in the middle, in a wide silver bowl, packed round a jug and sweating into the cloth, there was ice.

Kael stood two steps inside the door and his count came up on its own, the way it always did.

Nobody was eating.

Twelve people in that room, and a fire in four bowls, and enough on that table for the village, and nobody had a plate.

"Nearer," said a woman's voice.

She was on the far side of the table with two others behind her. Kael had seen her at a distance that morning, on the left, in the royal box.

He went nearer.

The princess was in a chair that had been carried here, because nothing on this island was made like that. She had come out of the arena blue and gold; she had changed since. It was a lighter thing now, there was less of it, and it had still taken somebody an hour. Her hair was down.

She looked at him.

She did it the way you look at a horse, or a field, or a piece of work somebody has brought you — from the top, then across, then back to the top — and she went on doing it well past the point at which a person stops.

Kael let her. He had been looked at by masters, by four hundred children, by eleven thousand people that morning. He stood the way he stood and put his hands where his hands went and did not fill the silence, because filling a silence was a thing he had never once needed to do.

"Turn him toward the lamp," the princess said.

The woman said, "Boy—" but Kael had already worked out what was wanted and turned, so that the light came onto the side of his face. That was somehow worse than being told.

The princess looked some more.

"You will address Her Serene Highness properly," the woman said.

"Your Serene Highness," Kael said.

He said it correctly, once, and then it went out of his head entirely, because it was not information and he had nowhere to keep it.

"There is nothing to him," the princess said.

She said it to the woman.

"No, Highness."

"I watched him for a quarter of an hour this morning and I have been trying since to say one thing about what he looks like, and I find that I cannot." She moved a hand at him, at the whole of him, in the light. "Look. He is a boy who might have carried something up here. There is nothing to look at at all."

"No, Highness."

"It is very annoying," the princess said.

Kael stood in the lamp and understood that he had been sent for the way you send for a thing that has been described to you, and that the description had not survived the object.

"They are calling you a ghost," she said, to him this time. "Did you know that."

"Yes."

"Do you like it?"

He thought about it, because she had asked.

"I don't know what it's for yet," he said.

The princess's eyebrows went up about a quarter of an inch, and behind her the woman's face did something that was not a smile and was in the same family.

"You beat a Drusane with your hands."

"Yes."

"My father's cupbearer is a Drusane cousin and has told me for two years that the house is the finest steel in the southern isles." She reached out without looking and somebody put a glass into her hand. "He was badly taught, then. That is all it was. He was badly taught and you are very quick."

"No," Kael said.

The room did not go quiet, because it had been quiet. It changed anyway. He felt it go through the twelve of them like weather crossing a slope, and he had felt exactly that in a hall four years ago.

"No," the princess repeated.

"He was taught very well." Kael had already started, and the thing that had started him was not courage, it was that a false statement had been made in front of him about a subject he knew. "His guard was right. His measure was right, and he kept it four exchanges when he was frightened, which is hard. He herded me with the air instead of throwing it, and that is what you do against somebody who moves, and nobody had to tell him that this morning. He opened my arm with a lunge his master built. It was all correct."

"And yet."

"And yet all of it needs a distance." He heard his own voice doing the thing it did in the letters room when Priscian asked something real, and he could not get hold of it in time. "He was taught a language for talking to a man four feet away. He was taught it very well. Nobody in that school ever had to explain what he should do when there is no four feet, because everybody he has ever fought was standing in the same place he was."

There was a pause of about two seconds.

"You are saying," the princess said, "that the finest steel in the southern isles is fine only against itself."

"I'm saying he was taught well," Kael said. "I said that twice."

The woman behind the chair drew a breath in through her nose.

The princess put the glass down. She had gone still — not the stillness Kael knew from his mother, which was a thing built out of years, and not Valeria's, which warned. This was a girl of fourteen finding a place in her own chest where a thing had gone in.

"Come here," she said. "Where I can see the arm."

He went round the end of the table and stood where she pointed. She looked at the cloth, and at the blood that had come through it and dried brown, and at the older mark on his forearm going up out of the cuff. Kael watched her look at his body the way she had looked at his face. It went on for a while.

"Does it hurt?"

"Yes."

"Will they mend it properly, or will you keep it?"

"I'll keep it."

"Good," the princess said. "Scars are the only interesting thing about soldiers and they get them so late."

And that was where his own faculty came on, and he had no more say in it than he had ever had.

The itch had never once in his life been out-waited. It had walked him down a mountain on the darkest night of a year. It had put him on a stone floor in front of a girl who could hear him coming through her boots. He was standing in the hottest room he had ever been in, four feet from the daughter of the emperor, and what his head did was what his head always did, which was to go back to the thing it had not finished.

"What happens to the food?"

The princess looked up.

"What?"

"On the table." He was not pointing. He had not moved. "Nobody's eating it. There's fish on there that was killed this morning and it's been in this heat since, and the fruit's going, and there's three joints. What happens to it after?"

Nobody in the room said anything at all.

"It is taken away," the princess said.

"Where."

"I have no idea where," she said. "Why on earth would I know that?"

Kael did not have an answer for that. It was not the kind of not-having-an-answer he was used to. He had spent years collecting the sensation of not knowing a thing and putting it somewhere useful. This one would not go anywhere. The question had gone out of him and had not landed on anything. It was going to go on not landing, and the room had already moved past it.

"And the ice," he said.

"The ice."

"There's no ice on this island in the spring. There's barely any ice on this island in the winter." He heard himself keep going, from a small distance, the way you hear yourself on the second night of a fever. "So it must have come in a hull. Packed in sawdust, perhaps? From somewhere north, and most of it was water before it got here."

"Yes," the princess said.

She was looking straight at him now, and she was not looking at his face any more.

"You have been in this room a quarter of an hour," she said, "and you have corrected me about a boy you knocked unconscious, and asked me two questions about my own table, and you have not once asked me for anything."

Kael, who had had nothing asked of him and had asked for nothing, said, "No."

"Every person who has ever stood where you are standing wanted something from it."

"I didn't know I was allowed to want something."

The princess sat back in the chair that had been carried across water for her.

"You are not," she said. "That is not what I meant and you know quite well that it is not what I meant."

He did not know quite well. He was, at that moment, further from knowing than he had been at any point in the last four years, and everything in his body that was any use to him was reporting a room, and a room is not a person.

"I sent for you," the princess said, "because eleven thousand people looked at one thing this morning and I wished to see it closer than they did. That is all it was. That is the entire of it." She had gone very level. "And I have looked at you for a quarter of an hour and there is nothing to see, and instead of being looked at you have stood in my rooms and counted my dinner."

"I—"

"Kneel. Say your farewells. And leave afterwards."

Kael knelt.

He did not know what the farewell was. Nobody had ever taught him one. He knelt on the boards in front of the daughter of the emperor with the heat of four braziers on the side of his face, and the silence went on for about as long as it takes a room full of people to understand that nothing is coming.

He got up. He went backward the first three steps, because the man on the stair had told him to, and it was the one instruction he had been given that he could still carry out. Then he turned and went out past the braziers and the flowers and the gold nailed along a screen standing in front of nothing. The smell came with him all the way down the eleven steps, and a long way after that.

The tall guard gave him back the wrap, and then the knife.

He watched Kael put the knife away, and where he put it, and said nothing whatever, and stood aside.

---

The cistern court at night was four brick walls and a black square of sky, and somebody had put two lamps on the rim.

"There he is," said Aurelian. "We have started without you and we are not sorry."

They were sitting on the brick with a cloth between them, and on the cloth was most of a Temple supper — bread, cold fowl, a wedge of the hard cheese, a jar of the small beer the kitchens gave the older boys and pretended not to.

"They feed the ones who are fighting first," Aurelian said. "Nobody has yet worked out that they will feed you twice if you go back and look tired enough."

Kael sat down on the brick and looked at it for a moment longer than the food required, and then took a piece of bread and ate it.

"Well?" said Elarine.

"She wanted to see what I looked like."

"And?"

"She was disappointed."

Aurelian put his head back and laughed at the sky, which was the loudest thing that had happened in that court in two years.

"Oh, that is *wonderful*," he said. "That is the best thing I have heard all day, and I have had a very bad day. The daughter of the emperor sent for the Ghost of the Southern Isles and got you."

"What did she say?" said Elarine.

"That there was nothing to look at."

"There is," said Valeria.

She said it from the other side of the cloth without looking up from the cheese she was cutting, flatly, as a piece of information, and Aurelian made a sound like a man hit in the stomach with a cushion.

"Enough," said Elarine. "Aurelian. Tell it."

"I do not wish to tell it."

"You have been waiting three hours to tell it."

"I have been waiting three hours to tell it," Aurelian agreed, and told it.

He told it well. He was good at this and always had been; it was the same instrument that had moved six backs as one in a yard when he was ten, pointed now at his own defeat, and he did not spare himself one inch of it. His opponent in the games, Meloran, on the sand at seventeen with four years of reach and a spear. The first pass, in which Aurelian had done something clever. The second pass, in which Meloran had shown him what the clever thing was worth. The third, in which Aurelian, by his own account, had been "moved about the floor like furniture, by a man being polite about it."

"He apologised afterward," he said. "In front of my father's steward. He said, *your house has been very good to me*, and then he asked whether my arm was all right, and I said yes, and it is not, and I shall be pretending it is until Thursday."

"You asked for him," said Kael.

"I asked for him four days a week."

"You got what you asked for."

"I got *precisely* what I asked for. That is the thing nobody warns you about." Aurelian looked into the beer jar. "I have been telling everyone for a year that I intended to be pulled apart at the highest available level, and I meant every word, and I was correct, and it turns out that being correct about it in advance does not do one single thing for you at the time."

He was quiet for about a second and a half.

Then he said, "He is going to win the whole thing, by the way," and reached for the cheese, and the moment closed over like water.

"He is not," said Valeria.

"He is not," Aurelian agreed cheerfully. "I withdraw it. I was being noble."

Elarine had not eaten anything. She was sitting with her back against the brick, knees up, arms round them, which Kael had never seen her do. The slate was nowhere.

"We are not doing tomorrow," she said.

"Nobody said—"

"Somebody was going to. It was going to be you, and it was going to be in about four minutes, and you were going to make it a joke first." Elarine tipped her head at Aurelian without any heat in it at all. "We are not doing it. It will happen at the third bell and it will happen without either of us discussing it tonight in a cistern."

"All right," said Aurelian.

"Good."

"May I ask one question about it."

"No."

"Then I shall ask a question about something else." Aurelian turned round on the brick. "What is the capital actually like? Everybody who has been will only tell you who they met."

"Ask her about the gates," said Kael. "Val, the seaward gate on that arena is hung to open against the crowd. Is that how they build them in the capital, or did somebody do it here because it was quicker?"

The court went quiet in a way it had not been quiet all evening.

Kael went back over his own sentence and found the word sitting in the middle of it, where he had put it without asking himself one thing about it.

He had said it before, at the end of the cold term, on cold stone in the dark. It was simply the word his mouth had for her now, and it had arrived in the middle of a question about a gate.

"*Val*," said Aurelian.

"Don't," said Valeria.

"A year." Aurelian put both hands flat on the brick and addressed the court at large, in the manner of a man laying out evidence. "A year. I have trained beside her. I have been struck by her. I have written to my brother about her at a length he described as *worrying*. I once held her cloak." He turned. "He has had the same year, and he says it in the middle of a sentence about a *gate*."

"You have never once asked her a question you wanted the answer to," said Elarine.

"That is beside the—"

"It is the entire point." Elarine had not moved off the brick. Her chin was still on her knees. "And you."

Kael looked round.

"Four years," said Elarine. "Four years of *Sedrane*. I have collected you off a sea wall twice a week since we were eleven. I taught you fire. I gave you a principle for a floor and I never once asked what you wanted it for." She let it sit exactly as long as she let everything sit. "And you have not shortened one syllable of me in your life."

"Your name doesn't—"

"Do not tell me what my name does. I am extremely aware of what my name does." There was something happening in her face that Kael could see with total clarity and could not read at all. "Go on. Try it."

"...Elarine."

"That is the whole of it. That is my entire name." She put her head back against the brick and looked up at the black square of sky. "Unbelievable."

"Val," said Aurelian, experimentally, to nobody.

"Stop."

"*Val.*"

"Elarine. Make him stop."

"I am not going to make him stop," said Elarine. "Val."

Valeria cut a piece of cheese and did not look up at any of them.

"It is grey," she said. "It is grey and it is very large and there is nothing to eat that is any good."

"That is the worst description of the capital I have ever heard."

"You asked me what it is like. That is what it is like."

"Elarine. Help me."

"I have never been," said Elarine. "My house sends letters there. It does not send me."

"Then we shall all go," said Aurelian, "and Val shall show us the grey, and we shall find something to eat that is good, and I shall pay, because my house is enormous and my brother has a very poor grasp of what things cost."

And they laughed.

All three of them, at once, easily, at nothing, in a brick court with two lamps on the rim of it — the sound going up off the walls and out of the square of sky, three children of the empire's finest houses laughing at a table on a night with nothing in it.

Kael's body flinched.

It was very small. It had been very small for years. He had stopped being surprised by it somewhere around his eleventh year. It arrived when people laughed. It arrived worst when they laughed together. It had never once been wrong about anything, and never once been right.

He took a drink of the small beer to have something to do with his hands.

And over the top of the jar he saw that Valeria had not laughed either.

She was sitting with her knife in the cheese and her face doing nothing whatever, in the way of a person who has watched a thing happen from outside it and has understood the shape of it perfectly and has no idea at all how the others knew when to start.

Kael looked at her for slightly too long.

Valeria's eyes came up and met his. She saw him seeing it. Neither of them did one thing about it, and she went back to the cheese.

They stayed another hour.

Aurelian talked, mostly. Elarine took the argument apart twice for the pleasure of it. Somebody knocked the jar over and it went into the cistern and Aurelian lay flat on the brick with his good arm down the hole and could not reach it, and described in detail what his house would say if it could see him. Valeria ate about four times as much as anybody.

Kael did not say very much, and was not asked to, and that was the part he had no word for at all.

When they broke it up the lamps were low and the sky had turned.

"Tomorrow," Elarine said to him, at the top of the steps, and that was all of it, and it was enough.

Valeria walked out with him as far as the corner where the sea court path went up.

"I am going to miss this," she said.

Kael stopped.

"After the games I will be going back to the capital," Valeria said. She was looking down the path and not at him, and her voice was doing exactly what it did when she called a number after a blow. "It was always the year. My father wrote in the autumn that it would be the year and that I should not become attached to the arrangement, and I wrote back that I had not. That was true when I wrote it."

"What will you do?"

"Wherever father says I should. It is not a thing I am told in advance." She shifted the strap on her shoulder. "I have been on the island a year and I have eaten supper with other people more times than all of my life combined, and all of them were in that court."

Kael, who had been given no word for this either, said nothing at all, which was the only honest thing available to him.

And Valeria Vel turned her head and smiled at him.

It was not much of a smile. It was there and gone, and it did not do anything a smile is supposed to do to a face. Kael had watched her for many mornings from a treeline and stood across from her every day for a year. He had seen her satisfied. He had seen her bored. He had seen her decide not to hit somebody. He had never once seen this.

"Third bell," she said. "Do not lose to Sedrane. I want you."

Then she went up the path, and Kael went down the road, and it was a very long way home and he did not notice one step of it.
