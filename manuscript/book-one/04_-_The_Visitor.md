# Chapter Four — The Visitor

The boat came at midmorning, and no fingers dropped.

Kael saw it from the upper pen: a small boat, one sail the color of old canvas, cutting a line for the island the way no boat was permitted to cut a line for the island. He was nine, and the rules lived in his body now, deeper than deciding — his knees were already bending toward the fence post, his breath already going long — and he looked to his father for the sign, and his father was leaning on the gate, watching the boat come, the way you watch weather you know.

"Go wash," his father said. "Both hands. Comb your hair."

Kael stood very still, and not in the game way. Somewhere a floor he had stood on his whole life moved half a finger's width.

He had theories about the exemption before he reached the trough — the game was for boats that watched, and this boat sailed like a sentence plainly spoken — and he knew, scrubbing his knuckles, that they were the kind of good that wrong theories were.

The house, when he came in, had changed the way the light changes before snow. His mother had the good bread out, the one with the seeds, that rose on feast days and no other days, and it had risen, which meant she had started it last night, which meant she had known last night. His father had put on his other shirt. Kael read the room the way he had been taught to read everything. His father's shoulders had come down from wherever they usually lived. His mother was singing, not bird, not downhill, just humming, loose, the way the goat wandered when the gate was open.

They were not afraid. That was the wrongness. He had no word for how he knew that a stranger should have made them afraid, but he knew it. A stranger was coming up the mountain, and the two of them had gone soft as summer, and the door stood open.

The man who came up the path was old the way the split rock was old — worn to what mattered. Tall, gone lean, grey all the way through, in a coat too plain to be a villager's and too well-made to be a villager's, carrying a wrapped bundle on a strap. He walked with a scholar's stoop that sat on him the way a borrowed tool sits in the hand, and the wrists coming out of the sleeves were too thick for a man whose work was paper; Kael filed him with the boat that did not fish. Then the man stopped in the yard and looked at Kael for a long, unhurried moment, the way Kael's mother looked at the sea.

"So," the man said. "You're the reason the honey's always gone."

His father laughed. Kael heard it and filed it and could not file it: a short, real laugh, spent like coin, and his father did not spend. His mother came out wiping her hands and the man said something to her in the soft downhill language, the strict one, and she answered in it, easily, three sentences with a smile inside them, and the man bowed his head a little.

"This is the master," his mother said. "He has come before. You were on the mountain."

You were on the mountain. Four words, given like bread, and Kael took them and began, without wanting to, to count. The days he had been sent up with his father before light for no season's reason. The days of the far snares, the days of the high meadow. The days he had come home and there was paper in the house that had not been there — the books. The books came from the master. The books had always come from the master. The best things he owned had been carried up this path in that bundle by this man, over years, on days arranged so that Kael would not be standing in this yard, and no one had ever lied to him about it, because he had never known to ask.

The wrongness of that went by him fast and quiet, like something small running the treeline, seen and gone.

Inside, the bundle opened. Two books — one fat, one thin — and a roll of real paper, and a stick of ink, and under those, wrapped separately, a cord of dark silk tied in a knot Kael had never met before. The master set the knot on the table between them like a plate of food.

"Your mother says you like questions," the master said. "I brought one."

The fat book was about engines of water — wheels and screws and the ways a river could be made to work — and Kael wanted it so much his hands ached. But the knot was in front of him, and the knot was now, so he took it up, and while he turned it the master asked him things. Not teaching-things. The master's questions were doors with nothing written on them.

"What is the goat afraid of?"

Kael considered it, fingers working. "Nothing that's there," he said. "She spooks at the shadow and sleeps through the storm. Her fear has bad eyes."

The master put the answer away somewhere, and asked on. "And if you could ask the sea one question, and be answered. What would you ask?"

No one had ever asked Kael what he would ask. The itch, the deep one that could not be out-waited, came up under his ribs and sat there like a second heart — and the answer was already there, because it was the oldest question he owned.

"When it will freeze," he said. "All the way over, so you could walk on it like a floor. I test it every winter. The pail freezes. The sea just moves." And then he was talking, he could hear himself doing it and could not find a reason to stop, and his mother went on slicing the bread, evenly, all the way through his answer, and the master listened the way he looked, unhurried, and Kael was in it the way you are in warm water.

And all the time his fingers worked the knot, which was a liar's knot — it showed you three ways in and all of them tightened it — and somewhere in the middle of saying what he would ask the sea, his fingers stopped believing the knot. It was not thinking; thinking he would have noticed. It was the way his feet were on scree in the dark: the hand knew before the eye told it. There, said his fingers, and he felt the little architecture of it, the one loop that carried all the others, the lazy place — everything heavy is lazy — and he drew it, and the knot came open all at once, soft as a mouth, a whole breath before his fingers could have reached the loop they pulled.

The room went still.

Kael did not notice the room. He was nine and the knot was open and he was mid-sentence about the sea. He noticed only, in the way you notice a door close somewhere in a house, that the master had set his cup down without drinking, and that his mother's hand had come to rest on the table, flat, and that his father was looking at his mother, and none of it was anything, and he kept talking.

Then his mother said there was wood that wanted splitting, which was true — wood always wanted splitting — and Kael went out, and the door shut behind him, and he stood in the yard holding the axe and did not split anything.

Because the voices through the wall had changed shape.

Not the words. The wall kept the words. But sound has bones, and he knew his parents' voices the way he knew their footsteps — his mother's that went up at the ends, his father's that put things down — and through the wall their voices were doing neither. They were quick, and flat, and even, all three voices taking turns without stepping on each other, like drill. Strangers' voices, in his house, wearing his parents' throats.

The axe stood against the block. Kael went down beside the woodpile, into the shadow at the foot of it, knees to chest, hands quiet, breath long — and became part of the house. It was the first time he had set himself down inside the game unasked. The stillness came to him like a friend, and he pointed it, all of it, at the wall, and the wall gave him nothing but shapes, and he stayed anyway, until the shapes stopped.

He was splitting wood, honestly and loudly, by the time the door opened.

The master left in the grey of the afternoon. At the yard's edge he stopped by Kael and said, "Read the fat one first. The thin one will still be true when you're older." And then, to no one, to the mountain: "He unties things." And went down the path.

Supper was quiet. The good bread was eaten. Everything was the same, and Kael lay in the loft doing his numbers and could not find the list, because the day would not file. The books were the master's. The absences were arranged. His parents had a third voice. A knot had opened before his fingers — he had already stopped believing that one; fingers were fast, that was all; his father's hands did things by themselves, why not his.

He woke deep in the night to the sound of his parents arguing in a language he did not have.

He knew all four of his languages the way he knew paths, and this was no path of his. It was low and fast and it had edges, and it was coming up through the floorboards of the loft in hard little runs — his mother the more, his father the less, the same three sounds coming back and back like a knot that would not give. He moved to the ladder without deciding to, one board at a time, breath long — and the third board creaked.

The house went silent the way a fire goes out.

Kael lay where he was, part of the loft, part of the dark, for a hundred long breaths. The silence held. Then, below, ordinary sounds: the shift of the chair, the small iron noise of the fire being settled for the night. He slid back to his blankets and lay with his eyes open, and after a long time he came down, water was a true reason, everyone was allowed water — and saw her.

His mother sat at the window in the dark with her back straight and her face to the sea, and across her knees, unsheathed, lay a blade he had never been shown, long and thin and older than the tools on the wall, and her hands rested on it the way her hands rested on bread dough, at home.

"Why do you have that?" Kael whispered.

"It keeps the cold out," his mother said, not turning.

He took his water and went back up. It was a strange answer, and he kept it, because it was hers. Blades kept the cold out. Mothers sat facing the sea.

In the morning his mother was at the table as always, and the blade was nowhere, as always, and when the chores were done she did not set the table to be a market or a garden. She stood in the doorway with her shawl on and said, "Leave the basket. Come."

She took him up, past the pens, to the flat rock where she had once turned his chin down to show him the fallen stars. The morning was cold and clean. She sat, and sat him in front of her, facing the sea, and for a while she said nothing at all, and the itch in him climbed and climbed and he held it.

"You untied the master's knot," she said at last. "Before your fingers did."

Kael began to say that fingers were fast, and stopped, because her voice had no question in it, and because he had known. Under his own theory, in the dark of him: there had been a moment when the knot had no more secrets, and his fingers had been told, and something in him had done the telling.

"There is one more school," his mother said. "It has been waiting for you to knock."

She held her open hand between them, palm up, empty. "You will not move," she said, in exactly the voice of every lesson he had ever had. "You will not ask. You will watch my hand, and you will find the thing in this that is like the loop in the knot. Not with your eyes. Your eyes lie like caps lie. Watch the way your fingers watched."

Kael watched.

The sea moved below them, the whole cold shining size of it. Her hand did nothing at all, and did it the way the boat had not-fished, and slowly, at the edge of what he was, in a sense he had no name for, something in her empty palm was — carrying. The lazy place. The loop that held all the others.

The wind came up the slope and found them and went on.

"There," his mother said softly, watching his face. "Now we begin."
