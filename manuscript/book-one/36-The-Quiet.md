# Chapter Thirty-Six — The Quiet

The wind went out an hour before first light.

Kael felt it go. He was lying on his side on the boards with his brother inside his coat, and he had not slept, and he felt the hull stop leaning and come level and sit down on the water and stay there.

He waited for it to come back. It had come down off that mountain every night of his life and it had never once stopped before the sun did.

It did not come back.

"That's the land breeze finished," said Severin, from the stern. "We will not have another until tonight."

"Is that bad."

"It is what it is."

Kael lay still and listened to the boat instead. Without the wind in it the hull had a great many small sounds that had been underneath the other noise all night: water knocking along the outside of the planks, something wooden turning over somewhere forward, a rope working against wood with nobody touching it. And people breathing. Seven of them, at seven different speeds, in a space about the size of the yard at home.

Eight, with his brother.

He put his hand flat on the front of his coat and felt the small ribs go up and down under it, quick and shallow, the way they had been going since he had been handed the child on a burning boat and told not to put him down.

Then the sky went grey, and he could see the boat.

It was worse in the light.

There was water over the boards, a hand deep at the low end, going back and forth with the roll. Three of the strakes on the left side were burned through in patches and one had a hole in it the size of a fist that Severin had stuffed with somebody's coat. Where the mast had stood there was a stub about as high as Kael's knee, black and split and still smelling. The rope ends lay about the deck where they had been cut, and every one of them ended in char.

Aurelian Corvane was asleep sitting up against the side with his mouth open and his bad arm across his body. Elarine Sedrane was asleep against Aurelian, which was not something either of them would have arranged. Aeliana lay on her back on the boards with her ruined hands turned up on either side of her, and she was not asleep, and she did not move.

Valeria Vel was not asleep. She was sitting on the thwart nearest the princess with her blades on and her eyes open, and Kael would have bet a great deal that she had been sitting exactly like that since the shore went behind the shoulder of the island.

And his mother was in the middle of the boat with her back against the strake and her knees up.

The light came onto her face and then onto her hair, and Kael, who had been looking at her for a while without anything happening in his head at all, saw the quartz white her hair and mixed into.

---

"Boy."

He got up. His leg had set overnight into something that took about eight strides to argue out of.

"There is water in her and it needs to come out," said Severin. "There is a scoop under the after thwart. When you have got it down to the boards, tell me, and I will show you where she is taking it."

"Yes, Master."

It was the best hour of that day.

He bailed. It was a stupid job and it did not stop, because the sea came in as fast as he took it out for the first while, and then slower, and then he was ahead of it. The scoop was a cut-down wooden pail with a handle burnt onto it. He knelt in the water with his knees going numb and worked the low end of the boat and did not think about one single thing.

Aurelian woke up and watched him for a minute and then came and did it with him, one-handed, badly, and neither of them said anything about the arm and neither of them said anything at all.

When Kael got down to the wet boards, Severin came forward on his hands and knees and put a thumb on the two places the water was coming in, and showed him how to plug one with tallow and rag, and how the other could not be plugged and would have to be lived with.

"That one is above the water when she is level and under it when she is not," he said. "So keep her level. That is the whole of it."

"How do I keep her level."

"You put the people where I tell you and you leave them there."

The old man's hands were shaking. Not much. It was the same amount they had been shaking on a white floor in front of eleven thousand people two mornings ago, when Kael had been the only person in the building who saw it.

Nobody said anything about that either.

---

There was a box under the stern thwart with a lid that fitted.

Severin got it open about the middle of the morning, and everybody who had been pretending to be asleep stopped pretending.

There were two small casks of water. A stone jar of oil, a bag of hard biscuit, a smaller bag with about four pounds of dried figs in it, a coil of line, a fish hook wrapped in cloth. And one loaf.

Severin took the loaf out and looked at it and put it on the thwart.

"That is a day old," he said. "It will not be worth eating tomorrow. So it is eaten now."

He broke it with his hands and passed it down the boat, and it went from hand to hand, and every person aboard took a piece off it and sent the rest along.

Kael got his and put it in his mouth and stopped.

There was nothing in it.

It was plain bread. It was good plain bread, made by somebody who could make bread, and there was flour in it and salt in it and nothing else in it whatsoever.

At home there was a loaf that rose in the night and had seeds all through it, and it did not rise for feast days and it did not rise for the turn of the year. It rose when the master was coming. It had risen when Kael was nine and did not yet know what a tell was, and it had risen when he was ten and knew, and it had risen the night before he first went down that hill to the Temple, and his mother had put it on the table without one word about why.

He sat on the boards of the old man's boat and ate the old man's bread and there were no seeds in it at all.

"How much water is there," said Valeria.

"Two casks."

"How much is that."

Severin looked at the casks. Then he looked at Kael, and did not say anything, because he did not have to.

Kael put his hand on the nearer one and rocked it, and listened to it, and did it again.

"They are both full," he said. "Call it eleven gallons together. Eight people. In this weather a grown man wants three pints a day and will live badly on two, and my mother needs more than that, because she is feeding him." He stopped. He had been going to say the next part in the same voice as the rest of it. "Nine days. Ten, if nobody is stupid. If it rains it is a different number and I do not know what number."

Nobody said anything.

"Thank you," said Severin.

"That is not a good number, Master."

"No," said Severin. "It is not."

And that was all anybody said about it, for the rest of that day and the whole of the night after it.

---

Aurelian started talking again a little before noon, and Kael only noticed because of the silence that had come before it.

He had talked the whole way down that mountain. He had talked over Wren crying and over four hundred men coming down a slot in the dark, steadily, correctly, for nobody, and when Valeria told him to be quieter he had been quieter and had not stopped. Somewhere on the water he had stopped, and Kael had been too busy to notice him do it.

"—so it is south from here, or it is nothing," Aurelian said, to the sky. "You would go south and then east along the shoulder of the continent. There is no other road. You cannot go north. North is a month of nothing and then it is theirs."

"You have never been on a boat in your life," said Elarine.

"I have been on a great many boats."

"You have been carried onto a great many boats and then sat down in the middle of them by somebody who was paid to do it."

"That is true," said Aurelian, "and it does not make the map different."

Kael came and sat down against the side near them, because there was nowhere on that boat to go, and because he wanted to.

Aurelian looked at him for a moment. Then he went on.

"She has to reach the capital," he said. "That is the thing. Whatever else happens, and however it happens, she reaches the capital, and every one of us on this boat is part of how she does it now. That is not a duty anybody here was given. It is one that arrived." He was not talking to the sky any more. "My father's men were on the eastern stretch of that wall. Sedrane's were on the north with hers. There were seven houses' colours on that wall and I know what five of them are worth in a fight, and I have not stopped doing that sum since the middle of last night. That is what I have instead of knowing."

"Aurelian," said Elarine.

"I am all right."

"You are not, and I did not say you were. I said your name."

He shut his mouth and sat with it.

Kael did not know what to do with his hands, so he did what his mother did, which was to ask about the practical thing.

"Your house is not on the island," he said.

"No. My house is at the capital, and my house is at Corvane, and there is a good deal of my house in the east where it should not be." Aurelian's mouth did something. "Two of us were on that island. My father, and me. Twenty-nine people carry that name and two of them were there, and I have spent four years being extremely tiresome about the other twenty-seven, and this morning it turns out they are all perfectly safe and it is not the smallest comfort to me. Which I did not expect."

"Sedrane is four hundred miles from any of this," said Elarine, without being asked. "My grandfather is alive. Two of my uncles are alive. The seat is alive. My house will go on choosing my school and my seat and the man, and it will do all of it on schedule, and it will not be inconvenienced by yesterday by so much as a week."

She said it the way she said everything, as though she had already been over it twice and found it correct.

"Only my mother was there," she said.

Kael waited.

"She said a thing to me at the far gate. I have been holding on to it since about the third hour of that morning, the way you hold a rail." Elarine turned her hands over in her lap and looked at them. "And I have worked out that she said it because she knew she was not going to see me again. Which means the thing itself was true, and the reason it was said out loud is that she was dying. So I do not know what to do with it. I have never once not known what to do with a piece of information."

"They are probably dead," said Aurelian.

"Yes."

"Both of them."

"Yes."

"Then say it, and let us have it said."

"They are probably dead," said Elarine Sedrane.

Kael sat with the two of them and did not offer them anything, because there was nothing to offer, and because the one thing he had that they did not was certainty, and it was not the better thing to have. He had watched his. He had counted the wounds on it and got to eleven and had not been able to stop. These two would go a month or a year not knowing, and would build something on the not knowing, and part of him wanted to tell them exactly how much he would have given to trade.

He did not say that either.

What he said was: "My father is dead."

"We know," said Aurelian. "We were there."

"I know you were there."

"I meant that you do not have to say it to us as though we might not have heard."

And Kael found, to his enormous surprise, that this was a kind thing to be told, and that he was glad — flatly and simply, with nothing in the way of it — that the two of them were alive and on this boat.

Nobody in that district had opened a gate for him. He had stood under a wall with thirty thousand people at his back and looked up and seen the pair of them standing on it, and what he had understood in that second was still true this morning and would be true in ten years.

It had never once had anything to do with either of them. He had known that at the time. He had known it while he was working it out.

---

"You are talking about the capital," said Aeliana.

She had come up onto one elbow. Valeria came off the thwart and onto her feet in the same movement, without hurrying, and stood where she had been standing since the cut.

"Your Serene Highness," said Aurelian, and got most of the way up before he remembered the boat and sat down again.

"Do not do that. There is nowhere for you to do it." Aeliana pushed herself the rest of the way up and put her back against the side. Her hair had come down in the night and salted into ropes, and her hem had been through six miles of goat ground, and none of it made the smallest difference to what happened on that boat when she spoke, which was that every person on it stopped and turned. "You are right. It is the capital. There is nowhere else it could be."

"Yes, Highness."

"I have been thinking about who is to be told what, and in what order, because my father will want it in an order." She said this as another person would say they had been thinking about the weather. "You will carry your own houses' news. I shall carry my guard's."

"Yes, Highness."

"They will be spoken for properly. I want you to know that. It will be done at court with their houses standing, and their names will be read." Her chin came up a little. "My guard did what my guard is for. Twelve of them, and the captain last, and there is nothing to be said about that except that it was correct, and it would be an insult to them to say more. That is the whole of what a guard is. My father says so, and he is right."

"Yes," said Valeria Vel.

"It is the same for the rest," said Aeliana. "The men on that wall. The Temple's children. Everyone who was lost getting me off that island was lost getting me off that island, and I am not going to pretend it was some other thing, or be delicate about it, because that would make it smaller than it was."

"They came for you," Kael said.

He had not decided to say it. It came out in the voice he used for a number.

Valeria Vel did not move.

"I should think so," said Aeliana. "There is nothing else on that island worth a fleet coming for."

"There were eleven thousand people in that bowl and they started putting stones into it before the bell had finished ringing." He was going faster. He could hear himself doing it and could not stop. "There is a boy of about six lying on the village road who pointed at me two days ago with his whole arm. There were thirty thousand people standing outside that wall and I did the sum on it four times looking for a way that any of them lived, and there was not one, and then I went east and listened to them stop. And then they came up my father's path, four hundred of them, all night, because you were standing in his yard." His hands had gone cold on his knees. "He put himself between them and his own door and he stayed there until they took his head off, and I watched every second of it. And you are not worth it."

Nobody on that boat said anything at all.

Aeliana did not answer him. She sat with her back against the side of the hull and looked at the water going along it, and she looked at it long enough that Elarine Sedrane began examining her own hands and Aurelian Corvane found something to do with the sky.

"The big one," she said, at last. "With the sword."

"Yes."

"Then he did well," said Aeliana Cassivara Vessantine, kindly, and she meant it entirely. "You should be proud of him. I shall tell my father about him when we reach the capital. I shall say all of their names, and I shall say his, and I shall have it written down."

Kael's body went before he did.

He was not aware of standing up. He got about a stride and a half and his arm was already going, and there was nothing whatever in his head — no plan, no anger he could have named, nothing that had produced any of it — and then Valeria Vel was in front of him and she had his wrist.

She had not appeared to move quickly. She had simply arrived, the way she arrived, and her hand closed and stopped, and the arm stopped with it, and Kael found out in about a quarter of a second that there was nothing he could do about that at all. She was standing closer to him than she had ever stood. Half her face was burned red and shining down one side, and her left arm was still carried in against her ribs, and she had done it with the other one.

She did not say anything.

She did not let go, either.

"Kael," said his mother.

That was all she said. She said it from the middle of the boat, in the voice she had used across the yard when he was small and going near the block, and everything in him came down out of wherever it had gone.

Valeria opened her hand.

He sat down on the boards.

"What is the matter with him?" said Aeliana.

Kael was looking at the wood. He heard Valeria Vel put her feet back where they belonged, which was on the princess's left, at the distance she kept.

"He is tired, Highness," she said. "We are all tired."

"Yes," said Aeliana. "It has been a very great deal."

And that was the end of it, and nobody on that boat ever said one word to him about it, then or afterwards.

---

Later, with the sun off the top of the day and the water lying like a floor, Kael went aft and sat down near the tiller, because it was the only place on that hull nobody else was.

Severin had the tiller under his forearm out of habit. There was nothing for it to do. The boat lay where it lay and turned very slowly all day, so that the island went round them by degrees, like something on a table.

"What is this called," Kael said, and put his hand on the piece of wood he was leaning against.

Severin opened his eyes.

"That is a strake. All of them are strakes. That is the top one, so if you want to be understood you call it the gunwale." He watched Kael take it. "The cross-pieces you have been sitting on are thwarts. The bench at the back there is the stern sheets, which is not a sheet at all; a sheet is a rope. The front is the bow and the back is the stern, and if you say front and back nobody will hang you, but nobody will hurry either."

"And left and right."

"Larboard and starboard. They do not swap round when you turn about, which is the entire reason for having them." The old man's mouth moved very slightly. "Which side is starboard?"

Kael thought about it and got it wrong.

"No," said Severin. "Again tomorrow."

He gave him about twenty words in an hour, and made him say each one back, and then made him say them back in a different order, and then sent him forward to touch each thing and name it out loud to nobody, which Kael did, in front of six people, without feeling anything about it whatsoever.

He came back and sat down.

"That is a small language," Kael said.

"It is."

"There is one word for each thing and it only does that thing."

"Yes."

"Why is it like that?"

"Because a man shouts it once, in the dark, in weather, and the boat lives or it does not." Severin shifted his shoulder against the stern, and something in him hurt when he did. "There is nothing clever in any of it. There has never been one clever word in a boat. It is four hundred years of people finding out what has to be said quickly, and then never changing it again."

Kael turned that over.

"My mother would like that."

"Your mother," said Severin, "would have had the whole of it off me by supper, and would have found two of the words redundant, and would have been right, and I should have gone on using them anyway."

Kael laughed.

It came out of him before he knew it was in there, one note, ugly, and it stopped as fast as it started, and then he sat with his hands over his face for a moment and the old man let him.

"Master."

"Mm."

"How many of them got out?"

Severin was a long time answering.

"There were four hundred and six children in my Temple on the morning of the third bell. I walked as many of them as I could find toward the northern arch, and I did not look up while I did it, because if I had looked up I would have stopped walking. Ninety-one of them were standing in a courtyard in that district by the evening. That is the number I know. There will be others. There were boats that morning, and there were households that took children in, and there was a great deal of that island I could not see." He looked out at the water. "I do not know how many. I have stopped doing that sum at night, because it does not have an end. I would recommend you do the same with yours, and I know perfectly well that you will not."

"No."

"No."

Then Severin reached under the stern sheets and brought out the sword.

It had been lying there all night wrapped in a piece of the burned sail. He took the cloth off it and held it across both hands, and did not hand it over at once, and Kael understood that he was not being made to wait for anything, only that an old man's hands were slow.

"This is yours," said Severin. "It was put into your hands, and I took it off you, because you had been holding it four hours and your fingers had stopped working. You will not use it for a year and possibly two. It is far too much sword for you."

"I know."

"Then keep it where it will not roll."

Kael took it. He laid it along the boards beside him with the grip toward him, the way his father had shown him with everything sharp for as long as he could remember, and put one hand flat on it, and after a while he stopped noticing he was doing it.

---

His mother had the child out when he came forward.

She had her back against the strake and her knees up, and Wren lay along her thighs with the linen still across his eyes, and she had one hand under his head, and she was doing nothing else. She had been doing nothing else for a long time.

Kael sat down beside her.

"What have you got in your coat," she said.

He went through it. His father's knife, which had come off a wall in a room that no longer existed and had been in that coat since a morning on the other side of the world. A length of cord. A flat grey stone about the size of a coin, which he had no memory of picking up and must have picked up somewhere between the arena and the water.

He put the three of them on the boards between them.

His mother looked at them for a while.

"I packed two bundles," she said. "I roped them and stood them by the door on the morning the games opened, and they stood there for three days, and everybody in that house walked past them about forty times a day."

"I know. I saw them."

"The books were in one. Both of the small ones and the one your grandfather did not write, and the pressed sheets, and the instruments, and the good glass. In the other there was salt, and needles, and every seed I have kept for eleven years." She turned her hand over. "Not one thing out of them is on this boat."

Kael did not say anything.

"That is what I have," said his mother, flatly and evenly, with no grief anywhere in the voice, in the tone she used for the result of a trial. "You have a knife, a bit of cord and a stone. And I have you, and I have him. That is the inventory."

Then she stopped, and did not add anything to it.

After a while she said, "Do you want to hold him?"

"Can I?"

"Sit back. Put your arm along the top of your leg. No — flat. Like that."

She put the child into the space he made.

He weighed almost nothing and he was extremely warm, which Kael had not expected either time and had forgotten in between. His head had no neck under it worth the name; the whole thing worked because Kael's hand was under it. His fists were shut up beside his face, the way they had been shut through the whole of a barrage. Under the coat and the linen he smelled of nothing Kael had a word for, warm and faintly sour and completely particular, and Kael understood that he would be able to find this in a room for the rest of his life.

His breathing was wrong. That was the first thing. It went fast, and then very fast, and then stopped for long enough to close Kael's whole chest, and then started again.

He counted it, because he could not do anything else.

He counted for four minutes and got the shape of it, and found that it repeated, and that it was not wrong at all, and that a thing this size simply breathes like that.

Then he went on counting for another ten minutes anyway.

His brother's hand came open and shut on Kael's finger and stayed shut.

He did not have much grip. He had exactly as much as a thing that has been alive for a day and a night has, which is not enough to hold anything, and he held on with it anyway, and Kael sat completely still on the boards of a drifting boat and did not take his finger back, because he did not want to be the one who did that.

The linen was still across the eyes. Kael did not touch it. Nobody on that boat had touched it, and nobody had said one word about why it was there, and his mother had put her palm flat over the whole of it in a room with a fight going on against the wall, and had not explained it then either.

"Kael," said his mother.

"He's holding my finger."

"I know," she said. "He does that."

---

Aeliana came forward in the middle of the afternoon, which took her past five people in a boat forty feet long, and stood there.

"May I hold him?"

Kael looked up.

She had asked. He turned it over twice looking for the other thing in it, and there was no other thing in it. She had been given a newborn on a hillside by a woman who told her she would be extremely good at it, and she had held him through the length of a fight and given him back, and nobody had said anything to her about it since, and she had crossed a boat to ask.

"Sit down first," said his mother. "Nobody stands up with him."

The princess sat down on the wet boards in what was left of a gown made for a raised floor with ice on it, and put her arms where she was told to put them, and Kael leaned across and put his brother into them.

She got the head wrong, and corrected it before anybody said anything.

"There," said his mother.

Kael sat back and watched his mother watch a princess of the empire hold her son, two hours after her elder son had come up off the boards to hit that girl in the face.

His mother said nothing about that. She had said his name once, and she had never said anything else about it, and Kael understood, sitting there, that she was not going to, and that this was not the same thing as her not having seen it.

"He is warmer than I remember," said Aeliana.

"They are."

"Is he well? I do not know what a well one looks like."

"He is well."

The princess sat on the floor of the boat with her ruined hands under a herder's child and looked at him for a long time, and did not say anything, and did not appear to want anything from anybody, which was, Kael thought, the first time he had seen that happen to her.

---

She gave him back before the light went.

His mother took the child and put him inside her coat, and then she said, "Tell me the night."

Kael had never once not started.

He started. He gave her the top of the slot and the shape of the party coming down it, and the interval on the crying and the six hundred strides it carried and what he had done about that arithmetic, and the four holdings, and the forty and the sixty and the hundred and sixty, and the second column round the head of the slot. He gave her Valeria's terrace and Elarine's bowl in the right order with the right numbers on them, and his voice did what it always did in a debrief, which was go flat and get faster.

He gave her the shore, and the nineteen lights, and the first one falling forty short.

He gave her the count coming apart. He told her he had lost thirteen entirely, and that he had been at twenty with nothing behind him between fifteen and twenty, and that he had stopped saying them aloud because they were arriving after the things did, and that he did not know what number he had been on when the mast went.

"Good," said his mother. "That is the correct thing to have stopped doing."

He gave her the woman on the low rock, and what she had done, and how long the dark had lasted afterwards.

Then he went backwards, because she had said the night, and the night had a beginning, and he came up the mountain into the yard.

He got Lucan Drusane out. He got the column and the four seconds and the store wall and *did I get him* out, all of it, in order, correctly.

Then he said, "Oss stopped at the top of the slot."

"Yes."

"He put a wall up. Four feet, eleven strides, out of nothing at all, and it held four seconds. I have never seen him do anything worth anything." His voice was still going flat and fast, which was the strange part, because there was water coming down his face while it did it. "And they went round it. And I was forty feet away."

"Yes."

"I never said one word to him. Not this year. Not on any day of this year, and I saw him on both days of the games and I found him in about a second and a half both times and I did not go over, and then he was in my own yard and he got half a step and the line moved him on, and I never said one word to him, and now I am not—"

And that was where it stopped being a debrief.

He got no further than that. He sat on the boards with his hands over his face and the whole of it came up out of him at once, thirteen years old, in front of six people who all looked at the water at the same moment, and his mother pulled him in against her with one arm and put her hand on the back of his head and held it there.

She did not tell him it was not his fault.

She did not say that Oss had chosen it, or that there had been no time, or that there was nothing he could have done, or any of the other things that are true and are said to people and do not do anything.

She held on to him and let him get through it, and when he had got through it she went on holding on to him a while afterwards, and Kael, who had been carried up that mountain by his father two nights before and had been thinking about it ever since without letting himself, lay against his mother on the boards of a boat and was, for about four minutes, nothing whatever except a person's child.

The sun went down while he was there.

---

He came out of it slowly and did not move for a while.

His mother's hand was on the top of his head. Inside her coat, three inches from his ear, his brother went on breathing in his fast broken pattern with the long stops in it, and Kael counted twenty of them, and it held.

*Ask me on the water.*

She had said it with the shore coming down at the boat and nothing left in her, and she had given him the reason, and the reason had been honest: that she had nothing to spend on it, and that he would get it badly and would have it badly for ever.

They were on the water.

He had the question sitting whole and finished at the front of his mouth. He had had it there since the middle of the afternoon.

He looked up at her instead. He looked at the day-old white in the front of her hair, and at what four miles of that mountain had done to a woman who had opened on her own floor eighteen hours before she ran them, and at the way her hand had shaken very slightly when she put the child back inside her coat, and he ran the sum he had been built to run.

Then he put it down.

She had felt him take the breath. He knew she had, because her hand stopped moving on his head for about a second and then went on, and because she did not offer him anything, and because his mother had never once in her life failed to notice a thing like that.

Neither of them said one word about it.

---

The last of the light went off the water and the island came up black behind them.

It had been out there all day, going round them by degrees, never getting any further off, because there was no wind to take them anywhere and nothing under the boat but the slow crawl of the water. It sat on the horizon at about the width of two hands. The smoke had stopped being smoke and become a smear in the air with no shape to it, going east, and the orange under the cloud had gone out at some point in the afternoon without anybody watching it go.

Kael put his back against the gunwale and looked at it, and let himself want things, which he had not done since the third bell of the morning before.

He wanted his mother alive in a year. He wanted his brother alive in a year, and grown, and with the linen off. That one came first and it did not need any working out and it had nothing underneath it.

He wanted to know what his parents had been.

He had refused it twice. His father had given him three sentences in eleven years and he had taken them and not pushed; his mother had shut the door with the same words every time, and he had let her, and he had told himself both times that this was a kind of discipline. It was not, and he had known that as well. It had been comfortable. A boy who does not ask does not have to hold the answer, and Kael had been extremely comfortable for a very long time, and then men had come up his father's path who knew his father's face and had a name ready for it. There was no version of the rest of his life now in which he did not find out. He would find out from her if she ever wanted to tell him. He would find out some other way if she did not. He would be a long time about it and he did not care how long.

And he wanted to find the small neat man who had walked round the edge of his own ruin with his hands behind his back, and he wanted to kill him.

He turned that one over, because he turned everything over, and it did not change shape under the handling. It was not hot. There was nothing in it that made his chest go, the way the wanting had gone through him on that mountainside when he went up the slope with nothing in his head at all. It sat where he put it, flat and cold and extremely patient, and it was going to be there in the morning.

He did not say any of it out loud. He did not swear anything to anybody. There was nobody to swear it to and he would not have known the words.

The men who had done all of this had come across this water and burned an island, and they were the enemy, and that one Kael did not turn over at all, because there was nothing in it that needed turning over.

Behind him somebody was bailing. Somewhere forward, Aurelian Corvane had started talking again, quietly, to Elarine, about roads.

Kael sat against the side of a boat with a sword he could not lift properly lying along the boards at his feet, and watched the island go dark.

He had seen that shore come alight every night of his life. The two by the boat sheds first, always, and then the long scatter along the water, and then the ones going up the hill in no order at all as the houses got round to it, until the whole of it lay along the black in a shape he could have drawn from memory at four years old, and had.

He waited for them.

He knew before he started waiting. He waited anyway, with his eyes open, and he went on waiting a long time after the light had gone completely, and no lights came on anywhere on that island at all.
