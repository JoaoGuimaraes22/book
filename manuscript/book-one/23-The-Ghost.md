# Chapter Twenty-Three — The Ghost

Forty feet of raked sand, and a boy at the other end of it with a sword.

The draw had gone up on the colonnade at first light. Kael had read it once and had it. Sixteen names in eight pairs, the pairs folded into each other twice more, then a line where the last bout went. His half of the sheet ran Drusane, then whichever of the four in the quarter under it was still standing tomorrow.

Lucan Drusane came to the measure the way he had been taught to. The front foot down, the back foot turned out, the sword up into the guard a paid master had spent four years putting into his shoulder. The off-hand open, empty, out to the side, because that was doctrine, and doctrine held that a Resonant kept one hand for the Art.

The whole system stood in front of Kael and told him what it was for.

It was built to fight at the measure. Every part of it — the guard, the straight back, the lunges that came off it, the empty hand — assumed a man standing exactly this far away with a blade of his own, doing the same grammar back.

The air came off the sea, went across the sand, and did nothing whatever for the heat.

Kael's eye went over the north curve once. Four hundred children of the Temple in the school's colours, on their feet, a wall of them. Three rows up and off to the left, a soft-built boy with his hands already going, saying something to whoever was next to him. Kael found him in about a second and a half and moved on. The finding cost him nothing and was not free.

Then he came to his own mark, and he stopped.

He did not draw.

The knife was where his father had put it, off the belt, inside the coat, and it was going to stay there. The two short blades were across his back in their wrap. They had edges on them that would go through a man's forearm if a man were careless with them, they had never once been near a fight, and they were not going to be near one today. He had thought about it for six days and come out of it the same way every time.

He put his hands at his sides and left them empty.

"You have to draw," Lucan said, from the measure. He said it the way you tell a man his lace is undone. "There's a form to it."

Kael looked at him and said nothing.

After a moment Lucan saluted anyway, because the grammar said to. The blade came up and came down in the line it wanted. The Drusane seats made a noise about it, and Kael did not answer that either, and the noise changed shape.

Somewhere up in the cheap seats one man laughed.

Lucan's face did what his face did, which was arithmetic, out loud, where anybody could read it. Kael watched it arrive at *contempt* and stop there.

Then a judge dropped his hand, and the sound came down.

---

Lucan opened correctly.

He took the measure — a step, then a step, without hurry, exactly the distance the master had put in him — and his off-hand came up flat.

The air between them went hard.

Kael felt it a half-beat before it left, because it did not come out of the hand. It came off the shoulder and the ribs and the whole front of the boy at once, the way everything did, and the hand was only where he had been taught to point it. So the shoulder was the tell, and the shoulder went, and Kael was already moving when the line arrived.

It went past his hip the width of a door and hit the rail behind him. Sand came up off the floor in a sheet.

The arena made a sound.

Lucan reset. Front foot, back foot, guard. He did not look at where the column had gone, which was good discipline, and he came forward again.

Second one. Higher, and aimed where a boy would have gone.

Kael did not go there. He went the other way and half a step in, and the air took the empty floor and the noise off the rail came back across the sand.

Third. Lucan did not throw it at him.

He threw it at the sand two feet to Kael's left, and the floor came up in a wall, and Kael's feet did what feet do and took him right, and Lucan was already there, and the measure was already made, and the sword came off the guard in the lunge four years had built.

Kael got most of his arm out of it.

The edge went along the outside of his left arm above the elbow and opened it, and he felt the cold of it before he felt anything else, and then it was warm all the way to his wrist.

The whole arena heard the sound it made.

The noise changed. It was not the noise of a crowd enjoying itself any more. Eleven thousand people had just been reminded, all together and at the same instant, that there was no ceiling on this, and the only thing between the white sand and the far end of it was an old man in a chair on the western curve.

Kael did not look at his arm.

He had been taught what looking at it costs, and had paid for the lesson in his father's own currency. The arm was information he could get later. He kept his eyes where they had been, moved off the line, made Lucan spend a step. The blood went down his hand and off his fingers and made a row of black marks in the sand as he went.

In the north curve Elarine Sedrane had a slate flat on her knees and a stylus in her hand, and while four hundred children stood up around her and shouted, she was writing.

Lucan came again. He was not enjoying it either. He was working — herding with the air and cutting with the steel, and the two things fitted together, and a paid master had fitted them, and it was, in every particular, entirely good.

And it needed a distance.

Kael had it standing on the sand with his arm running: he had spent four exchanges going *around*, because around was what you did with a thing coming at you in a line — and around was exactly where the sword lived. The column and the blade were not two problems. They were a fence and a gate, and he had been running along the fence.

The line the air came in was straight. It had to be. It came off a boy's front, and a boy's front had to be square to what he wanted to hit, and squaring took a half-beat, and a half-beat is a very long time.

So do not go around it.

Go up it.

Lucan's shoulder went, and Kael went in.

Not away and not aside. Straight down the middle of the thing, under the line and inside the release, and the air went over his back with a noise like canvas tearing and blew the rail apart behind him, and he came out of it at half the measure and kept coming, and then there was no measure at all.

Valeria Vel was the only person in the north curve who was not standing. She had her elbows on her knees and her chin on her fists and she was not watching his hands. She was watching his feet, and she had been watching his feet since the second column, and somewhere in the last three seconds she had stopped blinking.

A one-handed longsword is a magnificent object at four feet.

At six inches it is a stick with the handle in the wrong place.

Kael took the wrist. Not the sword — the wrist, in the way his father's school took a wrist, which was the way you take a thing that is going to be in your hand whether it agrees or not. He turned it against itself and the fingers opened because fingers do, and the blade went out and down and stood up in the sand three feet away, quivering, and neither of them looked at it again.

Then he was inside Lucan's arms.

Nobody had ever been inside Lucan's arms. Nothing in years of a paid master's grammar had one word to say about this, because the grammar was a language for talking to a man across a distance, and the distance was gone. Lucan's elbow came round because an elbow will, and Kael was not where the elbow went. Lucan tried to make room, and making room is a thing you do with your feet, and Kael's foot was already inside his and had been for a beat and a half.

Lucan spent the Art at nothing range.

It was the last thing he had and he was right to try it. The whole front of him went hard and the line came up between them out of no distance at all, and it would have opened Kael from the hip to the collar.

Kael turned him.

That was all of it. A hand at the shoulder and a hand at the hip and five years of *everything heavy is lazy*, and the boy's front went nine inches off where it had been aimed and took the line with it, and the column went out of him into the floor and blew a crater in the sand beside them the size of a table, and the two of them came off their feet in the middle of it.

Kael got up first. He had been going down on purpose and Lucan had not.

The elbow went in above the eye and shut it. The knee found the outside of the thigh, in the place his father had shown him in the dark when he was nine and had never once explained. Lucan's leg stopped being a leg. He went down on the sand on his face, and Kael went down with him, riding it, and took the arm and folded it up behind the back, and put his knee across, and stopped.

Lucan tried to get loose.

He tried three times, and he was strong, and he had been drilled since he was six, and there was nothing to get loose with. Both his hands were full: one of them pinned, and one of them still open at the end of an arm that had nothing left to put in it.

He reached for it anyway.

Kael felt him do it, the way you feel a man take a breath under your knee. He reached down for the thing he had been spending since the first step of the first exchange — the columns, the herding, the whole front of himself going hard at nothing range — and he went all the way down after it, past where he had ever been sent, and his body stopped him at the floor of it the way a body does. There was nothing there. He had emptied his capacity into a crater in the sand and the crater was still there and the rest of it was not.

Kael let go and stood up.

He had not decided to. His father's school had no move for a man on the ground with nothing in him, because his father's school did not put men on the ground and leave them there.

Lucan got his hands under himself and got up.

He did it badly and it took him a long time, and the arena watched every part of it. One eye was already closing. The leg would not carry, so he took his weight on the other one and stood crooked, and his sword was standing in the sand eleven feet away with the light going along it, and he did not look at the sword.

He looked up into the seats.

Kael followed it, because he followed everything. Nine rows up on the eastern curve, in the block where the noise had come from when the herald said *Drusane*, there was a man on his feet with his hands on the rail.

Kael read him at sixty feet in bad light, the way he had been reading men off hillsides since he was five, and got it exactly right the first time. There was no anger in it. Anger would have been something. The man on the rail had the face of somebody watching an expense.

Then he sat down.

Something went through Lucan Drusane's shoulders, and he turned round, and came at Kael.

There was no form in it. Four years of a paid master, the guard, the measure, the straight back, the lunges like a crossbow — all of it went off him between one stride and the next, and what came across that sand was a boy of thirteen with his hands up wrong, running.

Kael took him apart.

He did it the way he had been taught to do it, which was the way you do it when the thing has to end and there is no one to appeal to and no bell anywhere in the world. He met him coming, because meeting a man coming is cheaper. He took the wrist that was nearest and turned it and let the running do the work, and Lucan went past him and down, and the sand took most of it.

He got up.

Kael put him down again. Elbow, and then the ground.

He got up.

The third time Kael did not use his hands at all. He put a foot in behind the leg that was still working and took the arm across, and Lucan went down onto his back hard enough that the sound of it came off the seating and came back.

The noise in that arena had been going since the horns. Somewhere in there it stopped.

Eleven thousand people watched a boy with no house and no colours stand over a Drusane in the middle of the empire's own floor, waiting, entirely without hurry, for him to get up a fourth time — and understood, all together, that he was going to keep doing this until it was finished, and that he did not appear to know that there was any other way for a thing like this to end.

Lucan got his elbow under him, and pushed, and got as far as one knee.

Kael hit him once.

It was not a big blow. Nothing whatever about it was big. It went in short and level under the angle of the jaw with about eight inches behind it, into the one place on a man's head that a mountain had taught him about before he was nine, and it did not knock Lucan Drusane down so much as switch him off.

His hands did not come up.

That was it. His hands did not come up, and his knee did not brace, and he did not go down the way people go down — he left himself somewhere between one instant and the next, and what arrived on the sand was the rest of him, loose, with no argument anywhere in it.

Kael stood over him and waited to see whether he would get up, because that was the only question his father's school had ever asked about anybody.

He did not get up.

Eleven thousand people had come to that arena to watch children fight for a city, and they had been loud since the horns went at first light. What happened now was that the noise came off the building the way water comes off a roof when the rain stops. Underneath it there was nothing at all, and the nothing went on for four full seconds, which in a place that size is a very long time.

In the judges' enclosure on the southern turn, a broad, comfortable man with grey at his temples sat with his hands folded on the rail in front of him, looking down at a boy standing over another boy on the sand. He was smiling. He had been smiling for some while.

The First Master rose from his chair.

He did not come to the rail and did not raise his voice, and he did not have to, because he was standing in the one silence that building had made all morning and it carried him to the back of the seaward curve without any evidence of effort at all.

"The bout is decided."

Menders were coming out onto the sand before he had finished saying it.

"Kael, of the Island."

Kael walked back to his mark and stopped there with his hands at his sides.

Empty.

---

The arena erupted out of the cheap seats and off the seaward end and up from the standing ground behind the rails, where the island was, where the village was, where men who had rowed in that morning and gutted fish for their fathers were, and the sound was enormous. It went up and it did not stop going up. It was not a cheer for a house. Nobody up there had a house.

They had seen a boy with no name, no colours and no house take a Drusane apart with his hands, in front of every family that mattered in the empire, and decline to use the sword he had left standing in the sand, with two of his own across his back that he had not touched. Half of that arena had watched a fight. The other half had watched an argument, and had lost it.

He looked out over the seaward rail while the noise came down on him.

From the middle of the floor you could see over the top of the far seating, and past it there was the sea, and on the sea there were more ships than he had known existed, and behind them a horizon that went on being a horizon. He had been looking at that line since he was four years old from a rock on a mountain, and it had never once had anything on it. Now it had banners on it belonging to houses out of a chronicle he had read in a cold room, and every one of those houses had somebody in this building with a list, and they were all looking at him.

He was thirteen, and he was bleeding, and he had never in his life been so happy.

He did not notice that thought arrive. It went into him the way water goes into cloth, and it did not have a name on it, and behind him the menders were kneeling on the sand around the boy he had put on the ground.

The First Master had sat down again before the noise started, and he sat as he had sat all morning, with one hand along the arm of the chair and his grey head level, watching the sand. He said nothing to the steward at his shoulder, and the steward did not ask him anything. Four more bouts were fought on that floor before midday and he did not stand up for any of them.

In the royal box the princess had stopped talking to the woman on her left. She had stopped somewhere around the third column and had not started again, and her hands had gone still in her lap, and the twelve in dark grey stood exactly where they had been standing since she sat down. One of them looked, once, at the sand.

It started in the standing ground, and it was not a word at first.

It was a man shouting something to another man about where the boy had been when the air went, and then it was four of them, and then it had a shape, and then the shape came down out of the cheap seats and along the rail and into the north curve, where four hundred children of the empire's finest houses were on their feet and had not yet decided what they were allowed to think.

It went round the arena in under a minute, and it fitted, and that was the trouble with it.

*The Ghost.*

Kael heard it arrive and did not, at first, understand that it was about him. When he did, what he felt was a kind of narrow cold, because he had heard it before in other words. A girl with her back against a door in a brick court in the autumn had told him that in a room where everything talks the silent thing is the loudest thing in it.

It had taken the world a winter and four minutes to come to the same conclusion and put a name on it.

He had spent his whole life being nothing to look at.

Eleven thousand people had just found a word for it, and were shouting it.

Ruthen met him at the mouth of the competitors' gate with a cloth in one hand and his face doing nothing whatever.

"Arm."

Kael gave him the arm. The yard-master turned it to the light, ran a thumb along the outside of it well below the cut, and grunted.

"Long. Not deep. You will keep the scar and it will be a stupid one." He wrapped it, tight, in four turns. "You went inside the second column instead of the fourth."

"I hadn't worked it out at the second."

"No." Ruthen tied it off. "Sedrane tomorrow, at the third bell. She has been in the stand all morning with a slate."

"I saw."

"Then you know more than most of the boys in this building." He put the cloth away. He looked at Kael for one breath longer than he needed to, which was a thing he had done once before, four years ago, from beside a well. "That was not anything I have a name for."

"No," Kael said.

"Good," said the yard-master, and went to stand somebody else's bout.

They brought Lucan Drusane past the gate on a board a few minutes later, with one eye shut and his jaw already going the colour it was going to go, and somewhere on the way he had come round, because his eyes were open.

His face was doing arithmetic. It was not the same arithmetic. It had stopped being about luck a long time ago and it had stopped being about a charity case somewhere in the last four minutes, and whatever column it was going into now, it was going into it permanently.

He did not look at Kael. That was how Kael knew.

---

The corridor under the seating was cold. It smelled of new timber and old sea, and Kael got about twenty strides down it before the light at the far end changed.

A man was standing in it.

Heavy dark grey, with nothing on it. He was not blocking the corridor. He had arranged himself in it the way the twelve had arranged themselves across that ground in the morning, which was to say that he was already standing where he would need to be standing, and had been for some while, and Kael's whole body came up out of the fight and went cold and quiet in about a quarter of a second.

The man did not put a hand anywhere near him.

"Kael of the Island."

"Yes."

"Her Serene Highness the Princess Aeliana Cassivara Vessantine sends for you." He said it flat, in the voice of a man delivering a thing exactly as it was given to him, with nothing of his own left in it anywhere. "At the third hour after the closing bout. Alone. You will be brought."

Kael stood in a cold corridor with his arm bound in four turns of a yard-master's cloth and the noise of the arena coming down through the timber above his head, and ran every instrument he had at that sentence, and got nothing back off any of them.

*Sends for.* Not asks. Not invites.

He had a school for the road and a school for a room full of men and a school for a hillside at four hundred paces. He had a coat that had held for four years against masters, against a judge of the games, against two of the cleverest children in the empire, and had been cut for him on a road when he was ten by a man who had built it to survive precisely this kind of morning.

He had nothing at all for this.

"Alone," Kael said.

"Alone," said the man in grey.

Then he turned and went up the corridor toward the light, and did not look back to see whether he was being obeyed, because there had never been the smallest question of it.
