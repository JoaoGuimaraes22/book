# Chapter One — The Stars That Fell

Before anything else, there was the fire.

It lived in a hole in the floor, down where the wind could not find it. The wind wanted the fire. Kael knew this because the wind came to the door every night and pushed, and pushed, and the door held, and the fire stayed small and orange and his.

The fire was not allowed to be big. That was one of the things that were true, like snow being cold, like the goat hating him. Fire lived in its hole and was fed small pieces and it never, ever went outside. Once he asked why, and his father looked at him for a while, the way he did, and said, "Because outside belongs to the wind."

That was a good answer. Kael thought about it often.

The house had one room and a loft and the fire-hole and the door that held. In the room there was the table his father had made, and the beds, and the chest of woolens, and the wall where the tools hung in their places. Every tool had a place. If Kael took one down and put it back crooked, his father would cross the room later, not even looking, and make it straight. His father's hands did things by themselves. They straightened tools and they took stones out of the path and once, when a slate slid off the roof in a storm, his father's hand went up and the slate was in it, and his father kept talking to the goat the whole time, because the goat was upset about the storm.

His mother's hands were quieter. They were the hands that undid knots.

His mother sang while she worked, but not words. His father said words were extra. His mother's songs went up at the ends like questions, and sometimes a bird outside would answer, and his mother would stop and listen with her whole body, and then laugh, and sing the answer back. Kael did not know you could talk to birds. Then he did know, and it was one more true thing: snow is cold, fire lives in its hole, his mother speaks bird.

His father called his mother Neris. It sounded like a wave going back down the beach. His mother called his father Vask. It sounded like a stone put down on another stone. Kael tried both names in his mouth when he was alone with the goat. The goat did not care.

In the day there was the mountain. The mountain was everything the door opened onto: the path, and the split rock the path was not allowed past, and the pens, and the high meadow where the wind lived when it was not at the door. The mountain went up behind the house until it turned white, and it went down in front of the house until it turned blue, and the blue part was the sea, and the sea was the biggest floor in the world. Boats walked on it. They were small and slow, and they leaned, and Kael's mother counted them.

She counted them in the morning and she counted them at dusk. She counted them the way Kael counted his knuckles, not because the number changed, but because it was good to know it. Some evenings there were three boats, and some evenings there was one, and some evenings there were none at all, and his mother stood in the door and counted the none, taking just as long about it.

Nights were for inside. That was true the way winter was true. But one night — and this was the beginning of everything, though he did not know it then — one night his mother woke him and wrapped him in the big wool and carried him out into the dark, and the dark was enormous and mild, and she stood with him at the flat rock and said, "Look."

He looked up, because up was where looking went at night. The stars were out. He knew stars.

"No," his mother said, and turned his chin down.

And there, far below, at the bottom of the mountain, at the edge of the biggest floor in the world, was a little spill of stars. They lay in a heap where the land met the sea. They were warmer than the sky stars. They flickered like the fire flickered. Some of them were doubled in the water, and the doubled ones moved.

Kael understood at once, the way he understood most things, completely and wrong.

"They fell down," he said.

His mother did not say anything.

"The stars fell down the mountain," he said, "and they landed there, in the corner. By the water." It made sense. Things that fell on the mountain always ended up at the bottom. His ball. The slate. Him, once, and his father's hand had been there before the ground was, the same hand that caught slates.

"Can I touch them?"

"No," his mother said.

"When can I touch them?"

His mother was quiet for a time. Under the big wool her heart was going. He could feel it against his back, a small hurrying thing, though her voice when it came was slow as always.

"When the sea freezes," she said. "When the sea freezes over, all the way, so you could walk on it like a floor. Then."

Kael looked at the fallen stars for a long time. One of them went out while he watched, and he felt it go out in his own chest, a little cold spot.

"Is the sea cold?" he asked.

"Very cold."

"Then it will freeze," he said, satisfied. Cold things froze. The water in the goat's pail froze. This was simply a bigger pail.

His mother made a sound that was almost her laugh and not quite, and carried him back inside, and the door held, and the fire was small and orange in its hole, and that was the night the waiting started.

He waited well. Waiting was a thing his father taught, like carrying wood was a thing his father taught: you did it with your whole body and you did not spill any. All that winter, Kael went out in the mornings to test the world. He put his hand in the snow and counted how long. He broke the ice on the pail and held the broken piece up to the sun. And he looked down the mountain at the sea, the whole cold shining size of it, and it moved, and moved, and would not be a floor.

"Not yet," his father said, splitting wood. His father split wood in one stroke, always. Crack. "Not this winter."

"Next winter?"

"Perhaps." Crack.

Kael nodded and went to tell the goat.

At night, sometimes, he woke in the loft and heard nothing — the wind gone home, the fire asleep in its ash — and in the nothing he would hear his mother, downstairs, moving from the window to the door. Not going anywhere. Just standing at the window, and then, after a while, standing at the door. He never wondered what she was doing, because he knew. She was counting the boats.

There were no boats at night. She counted them anyway. It was good to know the number.

And far down the mountain the fallen stars burned in their corner, low and warm and doubled in the black water, and Kael lay in the loft with his hands behind his head, four years old, rich with certainty, waiting for the sea to freeze.
