# How to write a fight scene
https://www.reddit.com/r/writing/comments/1jfsad3/some_advice_on_writing_a_fight_scene_from_a_15/

CONDENSED:
## 1. Stage before blows

Establish the space *before* the fight starts, not during it.

- Position both parties: where is the hero, where does the opponent enter from.
- Ground it with sensory detail, not just geometry — smell, sound, the wobble of a chair.
- Any prop that gets used mid-fight must already exist on the page. If a chair becomes a weapon, the reader saw the chair first.
- Cheapest version of this: stage a scene in the location several chapters earlier, then fight there.

## 2. Length is proportional to importance

- The buildup and the payoff must match. A conflict built over hundreds of pages resolved in two paragraphs reads as a letdown.
- Importance can come from the *conflict*, not the opponent. A first kill matters regardless of who the target is.
- Inverse also holds: a nameless thug given five detailed pages trains the reader to skim. Reader patience scales with perceived stakes.
- Length is bought with **change**, not word count: change location, change who's winning, change tactics, pause for dialogue. A long fight without reversals is just a slow fight.

## 3. Tone must match the book

- Fight register is a tonal commitment. Quips and improvised traps in a horror novel break the spell; spilled intestines in a cozy fantasy do the same in reverse.
- Tone can shift across a book, but gradually and deliberately. Sudden tonal ambush ("cozy, then carnage") usually reads as a betrayal rather than a subversion.

## 4. The fight must be about something

The most important item on the list.

- "He wants to kill the hero" is functional but flat. Elevate it: brothers and blame, father and legacy, zealot and mercy, ideals that were always going to collide.
- The more the two sides interact *before* the confrontation, the more the confrontation pays.
- Best version: each side embodies a concept, so the physical outcome resolves an argument. The choreography is evidence for a thesis the reader has been following.
- Corollary (the Indiana Jones gag): a fight given more ceremony than it has earned is comic, not epic.

## 5. Zoom — the core technical control

**More specific detail = slower perceived time = closer to POV.** This is a dial, not a setting.

The same events at two magnifications:

- **Zoomed in** — foot placement, weight shifting to the back heel, which punch went to the kidneys and which to the neck, cartilage breaking. Reader sees everything. Costs pacing.
- **Zoomed out** — same sequence in a third of the words. Elbow to the nose, several punches to back and neck, a knee to the face. Reader gets the shape and the momentum. Costs clarity.

Neither is better. Choose by what the chapter needs:

- Multiple fights across multiple locations in one chapter → stay wide, protect momentum.
- Large-scale battle → wide by default. Blow-by-blow at battlefield scale traps the reader in one square metre. Describe flow: the line crumbling, the charge.
- Climactic duel → **oscillate**. Tight at the opening for texture, pull back through the middle so time and exhaustion register, tighten again into the finish.

## 6. Flow template for a climactic fight

1. **Stage + placement.** Location details, both fighters positioned.
2. **Dialogue.** Restate stakes, establish who's favoured (usually the antagonist — underdog wins read as satisfying).
3. **Opening exchange, moderately detailed.** Establish the narrative of the fight: who's pressing, who's absorbing.
4. **Plant the tells.** Rising heart rate, sweat, a confident smirk. Signal that the current approach won't hold.
5. **Reversal, zoomed in.** Technical detail on *why* it's going wrong — loose guard, body shots landing, weak counters being ignored.
6. **Pause.** Dialogue and interiority. Reader breathes. Restate what brought them here and who's counting on them.
7. **Second turn, mid-zoom.** Less choreography, more explanation of why the vibes changed. Hits land with increasing weight.
8. **Equals.** Antagonist surges back. Blows described in flashes rather than sequence. Exhaustion layered in.
9. **Final phase.** Shorter sentences. Slower attacks, sluggish responses, worse wounds. Cut in crowd noise, a face, whatever the fight is actually about.
10. **The blow, tight zoom.** Then emotion only — relief, exhaustion, grief. Aftermath belongs to the next chapter.

## 7. Communicating who's winning

Never state it. Show it through:

- **Tells** — coughing, spitting blood, ragged breathing, bouncing on the heels to build nerve.
- **Attack quality** — attacks "born of desperation," an obvious ploy, a wordless shout of pain and fury, telegraphed changes of direction.
- **Wound specificity** — torn tendons, ribs twisted inward, a punctured lung. Precise anatomy paints the state of the loser better than any adjective.
- **Interiority** — the winner's perception widening and slowing; the loser's narrowing to panic.

## 8. Mid-fight pauses

You can suspend the action without losing the reader:

- Dialogue between exchanges.
- Backstory that *explains the outcome* — why one fighter trained harder, sparred better, earned this. Extends the fight, deepens the relationship, and justifies the result at once.
- Interiority: fury, self-disappointment, the people waiting outside.

Personality must survive the violence. Characters still sound like themselves while bleeding.

## 9. Language

- Combat is a heightened experience — verbs should carry force, senses should all be engaged.
- **Caveat:** a thesaurus run of hitting-synonyms produces noise. Specificity outperforms intensity. One exact image (a shirt parting to show blood and a hint of cracked bone) beats five loud verbs.

## 10. On clichés

Two fighters flashing past each other, one collapsing — fine. Clichés in fight scenes are load-bearing conventions the reader can parse instantly. Spend your originality on what the fight *means*, not on refusing to let anyone cross swords in passing.
