Medium: loose expressive watercolour and ink on rough cold-press
paper, visible paper tooth and pigment granulation, scratchy fine ink
linework, wash blooms with hard irregular edges, generous bare
unpainted paper.

No reference image.

Setting: a long stone room off a courtyard, by daylight from a high
window out of frame. On a heavy table, one enormous open book — a
ledger as tall as a door when it stands, so wide open that its two
pages fill the frame. Old thick paper, cream and slightly warm, ruled
in faint lines, the gutter and the binding heavy at the left.

Composition: 4:3. The camera looks down onto the open right-hand page
from just behind and above the writing hand, close enough that the
page fills almost the whole frame. The near top corner of the picture
holds A MAN'S HAND AND FOREARM ONLY, in a plain dark sleeve, holding a
quill upright and still with its nib a finger above the paper. The
side of that hand is stained with old ink, worked into the skin like a
mark that gets renewed every day. No face, no head and no shoulder is
in the picture, and nobody else is in it.

THE PAGE IS THE SUBJECT AND IT IS AN ARGUMENT ABOUT LENGTH. The
entries above run the whole width of the page, one after another, each
a long unbroken chain of writing that goes on and on and reaches the
far margin — and the writing in them is not readable: fine dark ink
strokes with the rhythm of handwriting and no letter in them that can
be made out.

THE LAST ENTRY IS FOUR LETTERS LONG. At the left of its ruled line,
alone, in plain capitals, is the word KAEL — and it is the only thing
anywhere in the picture that can be read. After it the ruled line runs
on empty to the far margin, the longest stretch of bare paper in the
frame, and at the end of that emptiness there is one short horizontal
stroke of ink, no longer than the word itself, closing it off the way
you close an account.

Do not write any other word, letter or number anywhere in the picture.
Everything on the page except KAEL is unreadable ink.

Lighting: flat cool daylight from high on the left, no sun and no hard
shadow; the paper is the lightest thing in the frame and holds most of
the picture's light.

Colour: the ink is a true blue-black, the paper warm cream, the table
and the sleeve dull grey-brown. The one saturated colour in the
picture is a red silk tape lying down the gutter of the book as a
place-marker, and it is small.

The word and the closing stroke are the most finished marks in the
image and the sharpest step from light to dark happens at their edges.
The long entries above stay at quiet middle values with soft wash-bled
edges, the hand and sleeve stay loose, and the table beyond the book
falls away into shadow.
