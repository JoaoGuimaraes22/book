Same medium and style as the reference images: loose watercolour and
ink on rough cold-press paper, scratchy ink linework, wash blooms,
bare paper in the lights.

Setting: the yard of a small stone-and-turf mountain house on a summer
afternoon, as loose wash suggestion — thin dry grass and bare pale
rock underfoot, the house wall behind her with its plank door standing
open, and the ground running away flat to the right before it falls
off into pale wash. Low late-afternoon sun from the left, out of
frame, and it is the only light in the picture: warm along the grass
and the stone, with everything it does not reach going cool blue-grey.

Composition: 4:3. The camera is out in the yard a few paces off, at
about the height of a grown person's head, looking slightly down so
the ground reads as a flat plane. THE WOMAN stands at the left, full
length, about two thirds of the frame's height, in front of the house
wall and beside the open doorway. HER SHADOW lies away from her
across the sunlit ground to the right, roughly three times her own
height, running level across the picture. THE BOY is out along that
shadow, smaller with the distance, at about two thirds of its length.

NOTHING IS HAPPENING. This is a chore that has turned into a game:
she is standing still to be measured, and he is measuring her.

THE WOMAN: standing straight and square, weight even, both arms held
straight out from the shoulders and level, hands open and empty —
holding a pose somebody has asked her to hold. Her head is turned to
the boy and her mouth is open on a laugh, short and surprised out of
her. THE LAUGH HAS ITS EYES: the eyes open and creased at the outer
corner and on him, not creased shut. Not fond, not maternal, not
tender — a grown woman being made ridiculous by her own son and going
along with it. Long dark brown hair falling loose past the shoulders,
warm rather than cool, plainly brown and not black. Plain coarse
village working cloth, an overdress over a plain undershirt, in dull
grey-brown and undyed oatmeal, worn soft and much mended, with no
ornament anywhere on her.

THE BOY: eight years old, small, walking out along the shadow with his
head down and his eyes on his own feet. His weight is on the back foot
and the front foot is already set down against it, heel to toe, one
boot's length in front. Both arms hang a little out from his sides for
balance and both hands are empty. Absorbed and entirely serious, mouth
closed, the whole of him at work on it. Dark hair, cut short and
uneven at home by somebody who was not being careful. Coarse undyed
mended wool in dull oatmeal and dead olive, thin and too large for
him.

THE ATTENTION RUNS ONE WAY, AND IT IS THE SUBJECT OF THE IMAGE. She is
looking at the boy and the boy is looking at his feet; the two lines of
sight do not meet, and neither of them is looking at the viewer.

THE OPEN DOORWAY IS IN THE FRAME BEHIND HER AND A LITTLE TO ONE SIDE,
whole, from its stone step to its lintel, so that her height and the
door's can be compared by eye. That comparison is what the picture is
about.

The shadow is a flat cool blue-grey wash with nothing rendered inside
it, and its edge is clean and definite where it crosses the sunlit
grass. The grass is dry straw-gold, the wall pale grey-brown, and at
the stone step there is a low stand of mint growing — the one
saturated green in the picture.

Her face is the most finished thing in the frame and the sharpest step
from light to dark happens inside it; the wall, the grass, the far
ground and the shadow are all held at quiet middle values with soft
wash-bled edges. There is nobody else in the yard and no animal in it.

Preserve each exactly as shown in their reference image — faces, hair
and colouring. Do not blend their features. The boy is eight here and
small; the woman is fully grown and unworn.
