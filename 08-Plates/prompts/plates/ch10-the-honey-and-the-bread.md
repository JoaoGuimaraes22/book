Same medium and style as the reference images: loose watercolour and
ink on rough cold-press paper, scratchy ink linework, wash blooms,
bare paper in the lights.

Setting: a scholar's study at a temple, as loose wash suggestion —
books to the ceiling on the wall behind them, a stone window sill to
one side carrying a row of small clay pots with one tired fern among
them. Thin cool daylight from the window, and it is the only light in
the room: no lamp, no candle and no fire anywhere in it.

Composition: waist-up, the two of them across the corner of a small
table, the tabletop crossing the bottom of the frame. The boy near
side on the left, seated, turned three-quarters away from the viewer
and toward the table. The old man on the right, seated, facing him.

WHAT IS ON THE TABLE AND WHAT EACH HAND IS DOING: a stoneware jar of
honey standing between them, and a plain round loaf beside it with one
cut face. The old man's right hand still rests against the jar where
he set it down; his left hand lies flat on the boards. Both the boy's
hands rest on the table edge in front of him and are not reaching —
they touch neither the bread nor the jar. Four hands,
two objects, nothing else on the table and nothing held in any hand.

THE GAZE IS ONE-SIDED AND IT IS THE SUBJECT OF THE IMAGE. The boy's
eyes are down on the jar, the lids lowered, his face turned to it. The
old man's eyes are on the boy's face, watching him look. The two lines
of sight do not meet, neither of them looks at the viewer, and neither
mouth is open.

THE JAR IS THE MOST SATURATED THING IN THE PICTURE AND THE TWO FACES
ARE THE MOST FINISHED. The honey is a deep warm gold, loose and
soft-edged with no detail in it, and skin is the only other warm
colour anywhere. The room is cool — the books, the stone, the boards
and all the cloth in cool grey and grey-blue, going quiet at the
edges. The sharpest step from light to dark in the picture happens in
the two faces. The light on the window sill and the cut face of the
loaf are reserved as bare unpainted paper. The ink linework stays the
strongest element throughout and does not blur into the wash.

THE OLD MAN GIVES NOTHING AWAY. Courteous, unhurried and entirely
attentive, the brows level and not lowered — not warm, not kindly, not
stern, not amused and not sad. Hair fully grey, the whole head, a true
even neutral grey with no streak, no seam and no patch anywhere in it,
neither warm silver nor white. Tall, lean and still broad at the
shoulder, not frail. His garment, by construction: one long straight
dark undyed overgarment closed edge to edge down the centre front,
standing collar shut at the base of the throat, no lapel, no turned
collar, no buttons, no braid and no metal on it anywhere. Plain full
sleeves ending square at the wrist with no cuff and no sleeve beneath,
so both forearms lie bare on the table — thick and heavy through the
wrist, too heavy for a man whose work is paper.

THE BOY IS TEN AND THE HANDS CARRY IT. His hand on the table is a
child's, small and short-fingered, and the old man's hand beside it is
nearly twice its width. Eyes near-white neutral grey, cool and
colourless, the lightest value in his face. Dark hair cut short and
uneven at home. Plain undyed working wool, coarse and much mended, the
collar loose at the throat — the ink carries the cloth in dense
scratchy linework at the seams, the patches and the mended places.

NO OTHER PERSON IS IN THE ROOM: nobody in the doorway and nobody
behind either of them.

Preserve both exactly as shown in the reference images — faces, hair,
colouring, apparent age. Do not blend their features and do not mix
their clothing: the long overgarment belongs only to the old man and
the mended wool only to the boy.
