# Chapter Two — Part of the Mountain

Some things were true about the mountain, and you did not ask about true things. You asked about other things — why the goat's eyes went sideways, why honey didn't freeze — and his mother would answer until his head was full. But true things were not for asking. True things were the floor you asked from.

The path ended at the split rock. That was one. The path went down from the house through the pens and along the shoulder of the mountain, and at the split rock it ended, the way the roof ended at its edge. There was more ground past the rock. There was more air past the roof. That was not the same as there being more path.

Fire lived in its hole. That was two. Kael had known that one his whole life.

And when a boat turned toward the island, there was the game. That was three.

The game was the only thing his father played. His mother played many things — the counting game, the herb game, the game where she sang a bird wrong on purpose and they listened to it get angry — but his father played one game, and it was the best game, because his father played it too. Not the way his mother played, letting Kael win a little, laughing when she lost. His father played beside him, the whole way, every time. Whatever Kael was asked to be, his father was next to him being it longer.

The game went like this. His father made the sign — two fingers dropped, like something small being set down — and wherever you were, you stopped being Kael. You became part of the mountain. A stone was good. A stump was good. A shadow at the foot of the woodpile was very good. Parts of the mountain do not itch. Parts of the mountain do not look at the interesting thing going by. Parts of the mountain breathe so slow and small that a bird landing on them does not know it has landed on anything that eats.

When he was little, the game was little. His mother would hunt for him around the pens, calling his name in a voice that was pretend-worried, and he would be a stone until his laugh spilled out of him, and being found was warm like bread. That was the game when he was little, the same way his boots when he was little were boots he now could not get his feet into. The game grew the way clothes grew.

His father always won. This was another true thing. Kael had never seen his father lose at anything, but the game was the only thing they played, so it was the only place you could watch the winning happen. It happened like this: nothing. His father set himself down beside Kael and became nothing at all, and stayed nothing, for as long as the game lasted, and then stood up out of the nothing without a single stiff step, as if being nothing cost him nothing. Kael had decided that when he was big, it would cost him nothing too. He was working on it.

The day of the long game was a white-sky day at the end of summer.

He saw the boat before his father said anything, and was proud of seeing it, and the pride was the last loose thing he felt for a long time. It was a wide boat with six oars, and it was doing the thing that made a boat matter: it was not fishing. Kael knew fishing the way he knew the goat. Fishing boats worked. They crawled along their lines, they stopped and fussed, they leaned when the nets came up. This boat did not work. It came along the coast slow and even, and then the oars went up out of the water all together, like a beetle folding its legs, and it just stayed. A man at the front held a dark stick up to his face, pointed at the mountain.

Two fingers dropped beside him.

Kael was at the corner of the pens. He went down along the fence post and became the bottom of it, where the shadow pooled, knees to chest, hands quiet, face down but eyes up, the way you were taught: parts of the mountain do not show the whites of their eyes. His father was four steps away and then there was no father, only a shape by the woodpile that the eye slid off. Kael knew exactly where he was and still had to tell himself the shape was him.

The boat stayed.

He counted his breaths, long ones, the size his father's hand had taught his chest. He counted to the end of his numbers and folded the count and started again. The sun came through the white sky as a warm pressure on his left side, and then slowly it was on the back of his neck, which meant it was moving, which meant a long time was passing, and the boat stayed.

An itch lit on his ankle and asked, and asked, and he did not answer it, and after a while it stopped asking, which was a thing itches did if you were not their friend.

Then his leg began. Not an itch. A wire. A hot wire in the folded muscle, tightening as if a hand were winding it, and this one did not stop asking. Parts of the mountain do not have legs, he told himself. This is not a leg. This is the bottom of a fence post. But the wire wound, and his breath wanted to come up short and fast, and he made it stay long, and his eyes went hot and spilled — but that was allowed, or not allowed, but not counted, because water is not movement. Tears were like rain. Rain fell on stones and the stones were still stones. He let his face rain without letting his face move, because the face is movement too, the face is the loudest thing you own.

His father was a shape by the woodpile. He did not come. He did not whisper that it was almost over. He was nothing, beside a fence post that was trying so hard to be nothing, and he let it try.

The sun was on Kael's right side now. The boat had put its oars down twice and moved a little and folded them again. His whole leg was made of wire. He needed to make water and then, after a forever of holding that too, he could not anymore, and the warm went through his trousers and down, and the shame of it came up his throat like a stone he could not cry out around, because crying out is movement, shame is movement, everything he was, was movement, held.

His father's eyes came to him once. Kael felt them the way you feel the sun through cloth. There was nothing in them that was angry. There was nothing in them that was anything. They went back to the sea.

Water is not movement, he told the wire. Rain falls on stones.

The oars came down. The boat unfolded, turned its nose slow along the coast, and walked away on its six legs, and Kael's whole body rose toward the ending like a fish toward air — and no fingers dropped. The shape by the woodpile stayed a shape. Because the game did not end when the boat went. The game ended when his father said, and his father said it with the sky going grey and the first bat out and stitching over the pens:

"Done."

Kael's legs did not work. He got up the way the newborn goats got up, in pieces, and went down, and his father stood near him — close enough to catch him, and did not catch him — while the legs remembered. Then they walked up to the house, Kael in his cold wet trousers with his steps coming back one at a time, his father beside him carrying nothing, offering nothing, matching his pace exactly.

At the door his father said, "Again tomorrow."

That was all. It was nearly praise. *Again* meant the game was not over for him, that he had not been taken out of it, and Kael held that the way he had held the stillness, with everything.

His mother was in the doorway and had been, he understood, for some part of the afternoon — from the door you could see the pens, and the woodpile, and the sea. She said nothing. One of her hands was up on the doorframe, the knuckles pale, and Kael thought she was holding the door up, and thought no more about it. She took him in and stripped the cold trousers off him and washed him with water warmed at the fire, all without a word, not the washing-words she usually had, and there was honey in his milk at supper. There was not usually honey.

In the loft that night he did his numbers. Last summer, on the short game with the two fishing boats, he had spilled — moved his whole arm at a wasp — before he had even filled one count. Today he had filled his count more times than he could keep, and the only things that had escaped him were water, and water was not movement. He was getting bigger inside, where the game was played. Nobody had said so. Nobody had to. *Again tomorrow.*

He fell asleep proud, the way you fall asleep after winning, with his leg still humming its wire-song under the blanket.

He hoped the boat would come back. He was ready to be nothing for as long as it took.
